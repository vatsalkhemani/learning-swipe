# US political parties and the culture wars

## What this is and why it matters

American politics runs on two parties, the Democrats and the Republicans, and they have swapped coalitions multiple times across two centuries. If you map their positions in 1900 onto today, almost nothing matches. The labels stayed the same. The voters, regions, and ideologies under them flipped. This matters because most foreign coverage of US politics treats "Democrat" and "Republican" as stable team labels, like Coke and Pepsi. They are not. They are coalitions of convenience that realign every 30 to 60 years, and the current realignment is one of the sharpest in American history.

## The Democrats

- Founded in **1828** around **Andrew Jackson**. Originally the populist party of small farmers, slaveholders, and the white South. Built on hostility to centralized banking and federal power.
- Held the South for over a century after the Civil War because it was the party that opposed Reconstruction. "Solid South" meant Democratic.
- Transformed under **Franklin Roosevelt** starting in **1932**. The New Deal coalition was labor unions, urban immigrants, Catholics, Jews, Northern Black voters, and the white South all in one tent. Held together for 36 years.
- Lost the South after **Lyndon Johnson** signed the **Civil Rights Act of 1964** and the **Voting Rights Act of 1965**. Johnson reportedly said "we have lost the South for a generation." He was right, except it was more like several generations.
- The modern Democratic coalition (roughly 1990s onward): urban voters, college-educated professionals, Black and Latino voters, women (especially unmarried women), younger voters, and most of the coasts. Increasingly an educated-class party.

## The Republicans

- Founded in **1854** as an explicitly anti-slavery party. **Abraham Lincoln**, elected in 1860, was their first president.
- For decades after the Civil War, the GOP was the party of Black voters in the South (when they could vote) and Northern industrial business.
- Through the early 20th century became the party of business, low taxes, and limited government. Hoover lost the Depression. Eisenhower won in 1952. Goldwater in 1964 was a libertarian-conservative who lost badly but reset the ideology.
- **Richard Nixon's Southern Strategy in 1968** flipped the white South to the Republican column by signaling sympathy on race and law and order. Took decades to fully complete but the realignment was real.
- **Ronald Reagan in 1980** built the modern conservative coalition: business interests, the religious right (newly mobilized by evangelical leaders like Jerry Falwell), and "Reagan Democrats" (Northern white working-class voters worried about culture and crime).
- **Donald Trump in 2016** remade the party again. Less small-government, more populist-nationalist. Less free trade, more protectionist. Less hawkish on Russia, more hawkish on China. The traditional Reagan coalition is mostly gone. The new GOP is white working-class, non-college, plus a growing share of Latino men and Black men.

## The realignment, what just happened

- For most of the 20th century, the working class was Democratic and the college-educated were Republican. That has fully reversed.
- Today the Republican Party is the party of voters without college degrees. The Democratic Party is the party of voters with them. This is called the **diploma divide** and it sorted the country in less than 20 years.
- Race has not flipped as cleanly. Black voters remain heavily Democratic (around 85 percent). But Latino voters, especially men, have moved toward Republicans since 2016. Asian voters lean Democrat but the shift among them is also live.
- Geography sorted too. Cities are deep blue. Rural areas are deep red. Suburbs are the battleground. There is almost no rural Democrat or urban Republican left.

## The culture war flashpoints

- **Abortion**. **Roe v. Wade** in 1973 established a federal right. **Dobbs v. Jackson** in 2022 overturned it, sending the issue back to states. Roughly half the states have banned or restricted it. The issue has cost Republicans elections post-Dobbs even as they got the legal outcome they wanted.
- **Guns**. The US has more guns than people, around 400 million civilian firearms. The Republican position is Second Amendment maximalism. The Democratic position is gun control. **District of Columbia v. Heller (2008)** locked in an individual right to own guns. Mass shootings have not produced meaningful federal legislation.
- **Race**. Debates over **critical race theory**, school curricula, affirmative action (ended by the Supreme Court in 2023), reparations, and policing. After George Floyd's murder in 2020, the country had a "racial reckoning" that has since produced a strong conservative backlash.
- **Gender and sexuality**. Gay marriage settled by **Obergefell in 2015** and is no longer politically live. Trans rights are. State-level fights over trans athletes, bathroom access, and youth medical care define a lot of current culture war energy.
- **Immigration**. The border is the most consistent Republican issue under Trump. The DREAM Act for childhood arrivals has been pending in Congress for over 20 years. High-skilled immigration reform is stalled. Annual undocumented border crossings hit record levels in the early 2020s.
- **Religion**. The religious right was a 1980s political invention by Reagan-era organizers. Today the GOP is increasingly aligned with **Christian nationalism**, the view that the US is and should be a Christian nation. Democrats are increasingly secular.

## Why polarization is structurally worse now

- **Gerrymandering**. State legislatures draw congressional districts. Both parties do it but Republicans have done it more aggressively since 2010. The result: most House seats are safe, so the only real election is the primary, which rewards extreme candidates.
- **The primary system**. American parties pick candidates through primary elections with low turnout, dominated by activists. Moderates lose primaries. Extremes win them.
- **The media ecosystem**. Fox News launched in 1996. MSNBC followed. Then social media. Most Americans now consume news from sources that confirm their priors and demonize the other side.
- **Geographic sorting**. People have moved to places where their neighbors agree with them. Cross-cutting friendships across the political divide have collapsed.

## Real consequences

- **Government shutdowns** when Congress cannot pass a budget. Happened 4 times in the last 15 years.
- **Debt ceiling fights** that periodically threaten US default and rattle global markets.
- **January 6, 2021**. A mob of Trump supporters stormed the US Capitol trying to stop Congress from certifying Biden's win. First time the Capitol was breached since the British in 1814. Trump was impeached over it (acquitted by the Senate).
- Norms that used to hold (peaceful transfer of power, accepting election results, deferring to expert agencies) are now contested.

## What people get wrong

- The parties did not just slowly drift apart. They swapped coalitions. The 1900 Democrats and 2025 Democrats have almost nothing in common.
- "Conservative" and "liberal" don't map cleanly onto European meanings. American conservatives are more religious and more libertarian on economics than European conservatives. American liberals are more pro-capitalism than most European left parties.
- Polarization is not symmetric. Most academic measurements show Republicans have moved further right than Democrats have moved left, but both have moved.

## What to carry away

- The two-party labels are stable. The coalitions under them are not.
- The current realignment is education-based: non-college vs college, not rich vs poor.
- Culture war fights are downstream of who feels the country is being taken away from them.
- In one sentence: American politics is two coalitions in active swap, sorted by education and culture more than by class, fighting over a country neither side fully recognizes anymore.
