# The Constitution and three branches: how American government actually works

## What the Constitution is and why it matters

The US Constitution is the oldest written national constitution still in force, signed in 1787 by delegates meeting in Philadelphia. It is a short document, around 4,500 words, that designed a federal government from scratch. Its central obsession was preventing tyranny. The founders had just fought a war against a king. They had also watched ancient democracies collapse into mob rule. So they built a system designed to slow power down, fragment it, and make any one faction find it almost impossible to dominate. That design choice is why American politics often looks gridlocked from the outside. It is gridlocked by design.

## Why the Constitution looks the way it does

- The founders distrusted concentrated power. **James Madison**, the architect, wrote in Federalist 51: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." Since they were not angels, you had to set ambition against ambition.
- They feared three things in particular: a tyrannical executive (a new king), a tyrannical majority (mob rule), and over-mighty states or an over-mighty federal center.
- The fix was a system of **separation of powers** and **federalism** layered on top of each other. Power is split horizontally across three branches and vertically between the federal government and the states.

## The three branches

- **Legislative (Congress)** makes the laws. It has two chambers.
  - The **House of Representatives** has **435 members**, allocated by state population, elected every two years. Designed to be responsive to public mood.
  - The **Senate** has **100 members**, 2 per state regardless of population, elected for 6-year staggered terms. Designed to be slower and more deliberative. Wyoming with 580,000 people gets the same Senate vote as California with 39 million.
- **Executive (President)** enforces the laws. Elected to 4-year terms, max 2 terms since the 22nd Amendment in 1951. Commander in chief of the military, nominates federal judges and cabinet officials, can veto legislation. The presidency has grown massively since Franklin Roosevelt.
- **Judicial (federal courts)** interprets the laws. The **Supreme Court has 9 justices**, appointed by the president, confirmed by the Senate, and they serve **for life**. Below the Supreme Court are 13 Courts of Appeals and 94 District Courts.

## Federalism and the 10th Amendment

- Article I of the Constitution lists the powers Congress has: tax, regulate interstate commerce, declare war, coin money, and a handful more. These are the **enumerated powers**.
- The **10th Amendment** says everything not given to the federal government is reserved to the states. So states run their own criminal law, family law, education, drivers licenses, most everyday governance.
- This is why the US has 50 different sets of state laws and why something legal in Colorado (recreational cannabis) can be illegal next door. The federal government cannot directly order a state to enforce federal policy.

## The Electoral College

- The president is not elected by popular vote. Each state gets a number of **electors** equal to its House delegation plus its 2 senators. Total: **538 electors**. To win, a candidate needs **270**.
- 48 of the 50 states use **winner-take-all**: whoever wins the state's popular vote gets all its electors. Maine and Nebraska split theirs by district.
- The result: only about a dozen "swing states" actually matter in a presidential race. California and Texas get ignored.
- A candidate can win the popular vote nationally and still lose the presidency. It has happened **5 times**, most recently in **2000 (Bush beat Gore)** and **2016 (Trump beat Clinton)**. This is one of the deepest sources of legitimacy stress in American politics.

## The Bill of Rights

The first 10 amendments, ratified in 1791, were a deal to get the Constitution ratified at all. Several states refused to sign without explicit protections from the federal government.

- **1st Amendment**: freedom of speech, religion, press, assembly, and petition.
- **2nd Amendment**: right to bear arms. The single most contested clause in the document.
- **4th Amendment**: no unreasonable searches and seizures, basis of modern privacy law.
- **5th Amendment**: due process, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination ("pleading the Fifth"), no taking property without compensation.
- **6th Amendment**: right to a jury trial, right to a lawyer, right to confront witnesses.
- **8th Amendment**: no cruel and unusual punishment.

## The 14th Amendment, the real second founding

- Passed in **1868** after the Civil War, the 14th Amendment is arguably more consequential than the original document.
- Its **equal protection clause** says no state may "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."
- Almost every civil rights ruling of the last 75 years runs through it: school desegregation, interracial marriage, women's rights, gay marriage.
- Lawyers sometimes call this the "second founding" of the United States.

## Why the Supreme Court matters more than people expect

- The Supreme Court has the power of **judicial review**: it can strike down laws and executive actions as unconstitutional. This power was not explicit in the Constitution. The Court gave it to itself in **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, written by Chief Justice John Marshall.
- Lifetime appointments mean a single president can shape the country for 30 years through their nominees. Trump nominated 3 justices in 4 years. The Court is now **6-3 conservative**.
- People from parliamentary democracies often underestimate how much the Court runs American social policy. Britain or Canada decide abortion via parliament. The US decides it via 9 lawyers in robes.

## The rulings that shaped modern America

- **Brown v. Board of Education (1954)** ended legal school segregation.
- **Roe v. Wade (1973)** established a constitutional right to abortion. Overturned by **Dobbs v. Jackson (2022)**, sending the issue back to states. Roughly half the states have since banned or sharply restricted abortion.
- **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)** legalized same-sex marriage nationwide.
- **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)** ruled that corporations and unions can spend unlimited money on political ads, transforming campaign finance.
- **Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023)** ended race-based affirmative action in college admissions.

## What people get wrong

- The Constitution is not a sacred text written by infallible founders. It is a compromise document with serious flaws (slavery counted enslaved people as 3/5 of a person for representation, baked in for 80 years).
- The federal government is not all-powerful. State governments run most of daily life.
- The president has less power than people think on domestic policy and more than they think on foreign policy and military action.

## What to carry away

- The whole design is built to slow power down, not enable it.
- The vertical split (federal versus state) matters as much as the horizontal split (Congress versus president versus court).
- The Supreme Court is structurally the most powerful court in any democracy and its membership shifts the country for decades.
- In one sentence: American government is a system designed in 1787 to make any one faction find it nearly impossible to dominate, and that design choice still defines the country in 2026.
