# Immigration in America: the wave that built the country and the politics that fights over it

## What it is and why it matters

The United States is essentially defined by immigration. Outside Native Americans, nearly every American family arrived in the last four centuries. The country has admitted more immigrants than any other in history, around 86 million since 1820, and it has fought about each wave at the time. Immigration shapes labor markets, voting blocs, cultural identity, and foreign policy. Today around 14 percent of the U.S. population is foreign-born, close to the historical peak. The fight over who gets to come, how, and how many, is now alongside abortion and race as the most polarized issue in American politics.

## The early waves (pre-1880)

- The first big group: **Northern and Western Europeans.** English, Scots-Irish, **Germans** (largest single immigrant group in much of the 1800s), Scandinavians.
- **Irish, post-1845.** The **Great Famine** drove around 1.5 million Irish to America in a decade. They faced intense anti-Catholic backlash. "No Irish need apply" signs were real.
- This wave settled most of the Midwest and built the early industrial workforce.

## The great wave (1880-1924)

- Around **20 million immigrants** came to the country in this window, mostly from **Southern and Eastern Europe.**
- Roughly **4 million Italians.** Around **2 million Eastern European Jews** fleeing pogroms in the Russian Empire. Large numbers of Poles, Greeks, Slavs, Hungarians.
- **Ellis Island** opened in New York Harbor in 1892. It processed around **12 million immigrants** between 1892 and 1954.
- The pattern: arrive, settle in ethnic urban neighborhoods (Little Italy, Lower East Side), work brutal factory jobs, push the next generation into the middle class.

## The backlash and the closing of the door

- The early 1900s nativist movement is now mostly forgotten but was politically dominant.
- **Chinese Exclusion Act, 1882.** First race-based U.S. immigration law. Barred almost all Chinese immigration. Stayed in force in some form until 1943.
- **Eugenics** had widespread elite support, and shaped the immigration debate. The argument was that Southern and Eastern Europeans were biologically inferior to the existing stock.
- **Immigration Act of 1924** (Johnson-Reed Act) set a **national-origins quota system** based on the 1890 census, deliberately chosen to favor Northern Europeans and exclude Southern/Eastern Europeans and Asians.
- The 1924 Act essentially **closed the door for 40 years.** Annual immigration dropped from over 700,000 to around 165,000.
- The **Bracero Program, 1942-64**, brought millions of Mexican guest workers in for agricultural labor during and after World War II.

## Reopening: the 1965 Act

- **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** (Hart-Celler), signed by **Lyndon Johnson** at the Statue of Liberty in October 1965.
- Abolished the national-origins quota system.
- Made **family reunification** the primary basis of immigration. Skilled-worker visas were a secondary track.
- Architects believed the demographic mix would stay roughly similar. They were wrong by an order of magnitude.
- The 1965 Act is the single most important immigration legislation of the modern era. It is the reason the country looks the way it does now.

## Modern waves and the demographic shift

- **1960 U.S. population:** roughly 85 percent non-Hispanic white. **2020:** around 58 percent. Projected to drop below 50 percent around 2045.
- **Latin American immigration** dominated post-1965. **Mexican** was largest through around 2010, when net Mexican migration to the U.S. went negative. **Central American** (Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador) and **Venezuelan** flows have grown.
- **Asian immigration** has been the fastest-growing source for over a decade. **Indian** and **Chinese** and **Filipino** and **Vietnamese**. **Indian Americans** now have the **highest median household income** of any U.S. demographic group, around $145,000.
- **African immigration** is the fastest-growing share of the U.S. Black population. Significant flows from **Nigeria** and **Ethiopia**.
- **Middle Eastern** immigration grew especially after the Iraq War and the Syrian civil war.

## The undocumented population

- Estimated around **11 million undocumented immigrants** in the U.S. The number has been roughly stable for over a decade.
- **DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals)**, created by Obama executive action in **2012**, protects some immigrants who arrived as minors from deportation. Recipients are nicknamed **"Dreamers,"** named after the DREAM Act that has never passed Congress. The program has been in legal limbo since the Trump administration's first attempt to rescind it.

## The legal immigration system

- **Family-sponsored:** around 70 percent of legal permanent residents.
- **Employment-based:** around 14 percent.
- **Refugees and diversity lottery:** roughly 16 percent.
- **H-1B visa program** for skilled workers caps at around 85,000 a year. Around **70 percent of H-1B visas go to Indian nationals**, around 14 percent to Chinese.
- The **per-country cap** for green cards (no country can receive more than 7 percent in a given category) creates extreme backlogs. **Indians in the employment-based category face wait times of over 20 years** for permanent residency, depending on category. China is also backlogged but less severely.

## The modern political fight

- **Immigration was weaponized politically starting around 2015-16** with Trump's campaign. The pattern continues in every election since.
- **Trump-era policies:** the border wall, multiple travel bans, family separation at the border in 2018 (which drew bipartisan public backlash), record-low refugee admissions.
- **Biden-era pattern:** rhetorically more open, operationally constrained. Inherited record border surges in 2021-24. **Around 2 million asylum cases pending** in immigration courts at the backlog peak.
- The **2024 election** ran heavily on immigration.

## What people get wrong

- Most immigrants come **legally.** Undocumented immigration is real but smaller than the share of legal arrivals.
- Immigrants commit crimes at **lower** rates than the native-born population. The data on this is consistent across studies and is one of the more robust findings in U.S. criminology.
- Border numbers swing on factors well outside U.S. policy: economic conditions in sending countries, cartel violence, regime changes. The president gets blamed regardless.
- The "nation of immigrants" line is true and also incomplete. Each wave was opposed at the time. Italian, Irish, Jewish, and Chinese immigrants were all called unassimilable in their day.

## Why it matters

- Immigration is the single biggest input into U.S. **labor force growth** for the next 30 years, since native birth rates are below replacement.
- It is the most polarized topic in American politics alongside race and abortion.
- It shapes **foreign policy** (Cuba, Mexico, India, China).
- It is the **demographic engine** behind the country's continued population growth and behind every long-term economic projection.

## What to carry away

- The country was built by successive waves of immigrants, each one fought over while it was happening and absorbed within a generation or two.
- The 1965 Act reopened a door that had been closed for 40 years and reshaped the country's demography by an order of magnitude more than its authors expected.
- In one sentence: America is the country it is because of immigration, and arguing about who gets to come next is one of the few traditions that has been continuous since the founding.
