# The Civil War: the bloodiest war America has ever fought, against itself

## What it was and why it matters

The American Civil War, 1861 to 1865, was the war between the United States and the eleven Southern states that seceded to form the Confederacy. The trigger was the election of Abraham Lincoln and the cause was slavery, despite a hundred and fifty years of attempts to call it something else. It killed around 750,000 Americans, ended slavery, and rewrote the Constitution. The amendments it produced are still the legal foundation of modern civil rights. Every fight over race, federal power, and American identity still routes through this war.

## How the country got there

- Slavery had polarized the country since the founding. Every generation tried to keep it contained by compromise.
- **Missouri Compromise 1820** balanced slave and free states. **Compromise of 1850** included a brutal Fugitive Slave Act. **Kansas-Nebraska Act 1854** tried "popular sovereignty," letting territories vote on slavery, which sparked open violence in "Bleeding Kansas."
- Abolitionism rose in the North: **William Lloyd Garrison's** *Liberator*, **Frederick Douglass's** memoirs and oratory, **Harriet Beecher Stowe's** *Uncle Tom's Cabin* in 1852 (sold around 300,000 copies in a year, a staggering number for the time).
- **Dred Scott decision, 1857.** Supreme Court ruled Black people could not be citizens and that free states could not ban slavery within their borders. The decision radicalized the North overnight.
- **John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry, October 1859.** Failed insurrection. He was hanged. Southerners read it as Northern intent to incite slave revolt.
- **Election of November 1860.** **Abraham Lincoln**, Republican, anti-slavery expansion but not an abolitionist, won the presidency without a single Southern state's electoral vote.
- **South Carolina seceded December 1860.** Ten more states followed by spring, forming the **Confederate States of America** under **Jefferson Davis**.
- **Fort Sumter, April 12, 1861.** Confederate guns fired on the federal garrison in Charleston Harbor. The war started.

## The war itself

- Around **750,000 dead**, more than every other American war combined for the next century. Recent demographic studies push the number higher than the long-standing 620,000 figure.
- **Antietam, September 17, 1862.** Around 22,000 casualties in a single day, the bloodiest day in American military history.
- **Emancipation Proclamation, September 22, 1862**, effective January 1, 1863. Lincoln freed enslaved people in Confederate-held territory. Practically limited (it didn't apply to slave states still in the Union) but morally and diplomatically decisive. It kept Britain and France from recognizing the Confederacy.
- **Gettysburg, July 1-3, 1863.** Around 50,000 casualties over three days. Lee's invasion of the North was stopped. The military turning point.
- **Gettysburg Address, November 1863.** 272 words. Lincoln redefined the war as a fight over whether a nation "conceived in liberty" could endure.
- **Sherman's March to the Sea, November-December 1864.** Union general **William Tecumseh Sherman** cut a sixty-mile-wide path of destruction from Atlanta to Savannah. Total war strategy. Broke the Confederacy's will and infrastructure.
- **Appomattox Court House, April 9, 1865.** Confederate general **Robert E. Lee** surrendered to Union general **Ulysses S. Grant**.
- **Lincoln assassinated, April 14, 1865.** Shot at Ford's Theatre in Washington by actor **John Wilkes Booth**. Died the next morning. The country had won the war and lost its president inside the same week.

## The amendments

- **13th Amendment, December 1865.** Abolished slavery.
- **14th Amendment, 1868.** Granted citizenship to anyone born in the country, guaranteed equal protection and due process. This single amendment is the basis of nearly every civil rights ruling since, from school desegregation to same-sex marriage.
- **15th Amendment, 1870.** Black men could not be denied the vote on the basis of race. In theory. Southern states would spend the next ninety years finding workarounds.

## Reconstruction and the long backslide

- **Reconstruction, 1865-77.** Federal troops occupied the South. Black political participation peaked, with around 2,000 Black officials elected including U.S. senators and representatives.
- **Compromise of 1877.** In exchange for the disputed presidential election going to Republican Rutherford Hayes, federal troops withdrew from the South.
- **Jim Crow** followed almost immediately. Voting was suppressed through literacy tests, poll taxes, and violence. Segregation was legalized by *Plessy v. Ferguson* in 1896 and stayed in force until the 1960s.

## The Lost Cause myth

- After the war, Southern writers and politicians built a story: the war was about "states' rights," not slavery. Confederate soldiers were noble. Slavery was a benign institution misrepresented by Northern propaganda.
- Historians firmly reject this. The Confederate states' own secession declarations name slavery explicitly. Mississippi's opens "our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery."
- The Lost Cause built monuments, named schools, and reshaped textbooks. Most Confederate monuments were erected between 1890 and 1920, and a second wave during the civil rights era. They are political artifacts, not historical ones.

## What people get wrong

- It was not about tariffs or states' rights in the abstract. Read the secession declarations. They name slavery.
- Lincoln did not start as an abolitionist. He believed in stopping slavery's expansion, not in immediate abolition. His position moved during the war.
- The war did not end racial subordination. It ended slavery and replaced it within a generation with Jim Crow, sharecropping, and lynching.

## Why it still matters

- Race is the unresolved fault line of American politics.
- Confederate flags and monuments are still live controversies (**Charlottesville 2017**, the **2020 racial justice protests**).
- The **14th Amendment** is the legal scaffolding of nearly every modern civil rights case.
- Civil War rhetoric, including "secession" talk and "second civil war" framing, reappears in modern polarization. The original war's vocabulary never really left the room.

## What to carry away

- The country fought a war against itself over whether human beings could be property, and around 750,000 people died deciding.
- The war ended slavery and produced the legal foundation of modern civil rights, then the country spent the next century rolling back what the war had won.
- In one sentence: the Civil War settled secession, ended slavery, and left the racial question open in a way the country has been trying and failing to close ever since.
