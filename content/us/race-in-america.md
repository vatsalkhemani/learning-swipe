# Race in America: slavery to civil rights to today, the conversation you can't skip

## What this is and why it matters

Race is the deepest fault line in American history, the one that has shaped every other domestic issue. It is also the topic foreign observers most often underestimate when trying to read the US. Slavery built the early American economy. The Civil War almost destroyed the country over it. Civil rights laws passed only 60 years ago. The wealth gap, the prison system, the residential map of nearly every American city all trace back to it. You cannot understand contemporary American politics, culture, or even why a Trump rally feels the way it does, without this through-line. It is not a sub-topic. It is the topic.

## Slavery (1619-1865)

- **August 1619**, the first enslaved Africans arrived in **Jamestown, Virginia**, brought on a Portuguese ship intercepted by English privateers. About 20 people. The colony had been there 12 years.
- The Atlantic slave trade trafficked roughly **12.5 million Africans** to the Americas over 350 years. About 400,000 came directly to what became the US. The rest went to the Caribbean and Brazil.
- By **1860**, the year before the Civil War, the enslaved population of the United States was about **4 million people**, almost all in the South. Slavery had grown massively because of cotton. The cotton gin (1793) made cotton hugely profitable. The American South supplied roughly 75 percent of the world's cotton by 1860.
- Slavery was not just labor. It was a legal system. Enslaved people could not legally marry, learn to read, testify in court, or own property. Children inherited the status of their mother. Resistance was punished with extreme violence.
- Black abolitionists like **Frederick Douglass** (born enslaved, escaped, became one of the most powerful orators of the 19th century) and **Harriet Tubman** (led around 70 enslaved people to freedom via the Underground Railroad) shaped the moral case against slavery.

## Civil War and Reconstruction (1861-1877)

- The **Civil War (1861-1865)** killed about 750,000 Americans, more than any other US war. The North won. The 13th Amendment in **1865** abolished slavery. The 14th in **1868** guaranteed equal protection. The 15th in **1870** gave Black men the right to vote.
- **Reconstruction (1865-1877)** was a serious attempt to make Black Americans full citizens. Federal troops occupied the South. Black men were elected to Congress (around 16, including 2 senators). New public schools opened. It was the most racially equal moment in American history until the 1960s.
- It collapsed. After the disputed 1876 election, federal troops withdrew. Southern whites violently overthrew Reconstruction governments. The Supreme Court gutted civil rights laws (the **Slaughterhouse Cases 1873**, **Plessy v. Ferguson 1896**). Within 20 years the South was a one-party authoritarian region built on racial control.

## Jim Crow and the Great Migration (1877-1940s)

- **Jim Crow laws** segregated everything in the South: schools, water fountains, hospitals, restaurants, transit, neighborhoods. Backed by lynching as terror. From 1877 to 1950, roughly **4,400 Black Americans were lynched** in the South.
- The North was not equal either. It was segregated more by housing policy than by law. Redlining (a federal policy starting in the 1930s) marked Black neighborhoods as bad credit risks, locking out home loans for generations. Most of the modern Black-white wealth gap traces to this.
- The **Great Migration (1916-1970)** was one of the largest internal migrations in human history. About **6 million Black Americans** moved from the rural South to Northern and Western cities (Chicago, Detroit, New York, Los Angeles). It reshaped American culture, gave the world Motown, blues, jazz, hip-hop, and changed the political map of the North.

## The Civil Rights Movement (1954-1968)

- **Brown v. Board of Education (1954)**. The Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional. Compliance took years and was often forced at the point of federal bayonets.
- **Rosa Parks** refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus in **December 1955**, triggering a year-long boycott that ended bus segregation in the city. It launched a 26-year-old Baptist minister named **Martin Luther King Jr.** to national prominence.
- The movement was a strategic, disciplined, organized campaign of nonviolent direct action. Sit-ins, freedom rides, voter registration drives. Protesters were beaten, jailed, and killed. They kept showing up.
- **March on Washington, August 1963**. 250,000 people. King's "I Have a Dream" speech.
- **Civil Rights Act of 1964**. Banned discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and federally funded programs.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965**. Banned literacy tests and gave the federal government oversight of elections in places with a history of voter suppression. This is the law that actually let Black Southerners vote.
- **MLK was assassinated on April 4, 1968** in Memphis. Riots broke out in over 100 cities. The movement fractured.

## Post-1968: legal equality, persistent disparities

- Black Americans now have full legal equality. The reality has been more complicated.
- **Wealth**. The median white family has roughly 8 times the wealth of the median Black family. Most of that gap traces to housing policy, not income.
- **Incarceration**. The US has the largest prison population in the world, roughly 2 million people. Black Americans are imprisoned at about 5 times the rate of white Americans. The War on Drugs (Reagan era) is central to this story.
- **Education**. Schools today are roughly as segregated as they were in 1968, by neighborhood not by law.
- **Health**. Black women die in childbirth at 3 times the rate of white women. Black men have life expectancies 5 years shorter.
- **Police violence**. Black men are about 2.5 times more likely to be killed by police than white men.

## Obama, then backlash, then BLM

- **Barack Obama** won the presidency in **2008**, the first Black president. Many hoped this signaled a "post-racial" America. It did not.
- The backlash was immediate. The Tea Party movement, "birther" conspiracies (Trump pushed them), and a hardening of racial polarization in voting.
- **Black Lives Matter** was founded in 2013 after **George Zimmerman** was acquitted in the killing of **Trayvon Martin**, a 17-year-old Black teen in Florida.
- BLM surged in **2020** after a Minneapolis police officer murdered **George Floyd** on video, kneeling on his neck for 9 minutes 29 seconds. Tens of millions of Americans, of all races, protested. It was the largest protest movement in US history.

## Today's debates

- **Structural vs individual racism**. Is racism mostly about individual prejudice, or about systems (housing, policing, schools, hiring) that produce racially unequal outcomes even without individual racists? This is roughly the dividing line between conservative and progressive views.
- **Reparations**. Should the federal government pay descendants of enslaved Americans? California formed a task force that recommended yes. National opinion is sharply divided.
- **Critical race theory (CRT)**. An academic legal framework from the 1980s that has become political shorthand in K-12 curriculum fights. Multiple states have banned teaching it. Most schools were not teaching it.
- **Affirmative action**. The Supreme Court ended race-based college admissions in **Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023)**.
- **Policing**. Calls to "defund the police" peaked in 2020 and faded. Reforms have been piecemeal.

## What people get wrong

- Race in America is not just about Black and white. Indigenous peoples were displaced, killed, and confined to reservations. The US imprisoned 120,000 Japanese Americans during WWII. Latinos have their own deep history. Asian Americans face their own pattern of model-minority stereotype and exclusion (Chinese Exclusion Act 1882).
- Civil rights was not just MLK. It was thousands of organizers, lawyers, students, ministers, and ordinary people who put their bodies on the line.
- The wealth gap is mostly not about current income. It is about inheritance from a century of housing and credit policy.

## What to carry away

- The American story is the universal-equality promise of 1776 colliding with the slavery reality of 1619, and the working out has taken 400 years and is not done.
- Legal equality came in 1964-65. Material equality has not.
- Every domestic political fight (housing, education, criminal justice, voting, immigration, even tax policy) has racial dimensions that other countries' equivalents do not.
- In one sentence: race in America is not a subtopic of American politics. It is the topic underneath every other topic.
