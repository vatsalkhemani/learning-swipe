# How Silicon Valley and Wall Street became the two pillars of American power

## What this is and why it matters

The two most powerful sectors in modern America are finance, concentrated in **Wall Street** (Lower Manhattan and Northern New Jersey), and software, concentrated in **Silicon Valley** (the San Francisco Bay Area). They are not just industries. They are the two pipes that capital and code flow through, and almost every other industry depends on at least one of them. They produce the country's billionaires, attract its best graduates, set its cultural tone, and increasingly determine its political alignments. If you want to understand contemporary American power, you have to look at both of these places together, not separately.

## Wall Street: origins (1792-1900)

- The **New York Stock Exchange** was founded on **May 17, 1792**, when 24 stockbrokers signed the **Buttonwood Agreement** under a tree at 68 Wall Street in Lower Manhattan. The street itself takes its name from a wooden wall the Dutch built in the 1600s to protect New Amsterdam.
- New York beat Boston and Philadelphia for finance because of the **Erie Canal (1825)**, which made it the trading gateway between Europe and the American interior. Banking followed the trade.
- By the late 1800s, **J.P. Morgan** was the most powerful financier in America. During the **Panic of 1907**, with no central bank, Morgan personally locked bankers in his library and refused to let them leave until they coordinated a bailout. The episode convinced Congress to create the **Federal Reserve in 1913**.

## Wall Street: the modern era (1980-today)

- Wall Street was a relatively contained industry until the 1980s.
- **Reagan-era deregulation** unleashed it. Glass-Steagall (the 1933 law separating commercial and investment banking) was weakened starting in the 1980s and repealed in 1999 under Clinton. The financial sector exploded.
- Between 1980 and 2007, the financial sector's share of US corporate profits roughly **tripled**, from around 10 percent to over 30 percent.
- **The 2008 financial crisis** exposed how systemic this had become. The collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 nearly froze global credit. The federal government bailed out the major banks (the TARP program was $700 billion). Almost no senior bankers went to prison.
- The crisis seeded a decade of populist anger, on both the left (Occupy Wall Street 2011) and the right (the Tea Party, then Trump).
- Today the financial sector is roughly **8 percent of US GDP** but captures **around 25 percent of corporate profits**, an enormous mismatch that is itself a political issue.

## Silicon Valley: origins (1940-1970)

- **Frederick Terman**, an electrical engineering professor at Stanford starting in the 1930s, is the founding figure. He pushed graduates to start companies near the university instead of going east. He helped two students named **Bill Hewlett** and **Dave Packard** start a company in a Palo Alto garage in 1939.
- **William Shockley** invented the transistor at Bell Labs and won the Nobel Prize for it in 1956. He moved to Mountain View to start Shockley Semiconductor. He was a brilliant scientist and a famously terrible boss.
- In 1957, eight of his best engineers (the "**traitorous eight**", including **Robert Noyce** and **Gordon Moore**) quit and founded **Fairchild Semiconductor**. This is the moment Silicon Valley as a self-perpetuating system was born.
- Fairchild seeded dozens of spinoffs ("Fairchildren"). Noyce and Moore left Fairchild in 1968 to start **Intel**. The cycle of engineers leaving to start companies became the Valley's defining feature.

## Why Silicon Valley concentrated where it did

- **Stanford and Berkeley** produced world-class engineers and (unlike most universities) actively encouraged commercial spinouts.
- **Venture capital** developed as an industry on Sand Hill Road in Menlo Park in the 1970s. Firms like Sequoia (founded 1972) and Kleiner Perkins (1972) wrote the playbook for backing high-risk startups in exchange for equity.
- **California's non-compete law** is the secret structural advantage almost no one talks about. California is one of the few states where non-compete clauses are unenforceable in employment contracts. Engineers can quit, take what they know, and start a competitor across the street. This is why ideas spread faster in the Valley than anywhere else.
- **Open culture**. The Valley copied the open-source, share-credit, post-hippie ethos of 1960s-70s California more than it copied East Coast corporate hierarchy. The Homebrew Computer Club where Steve Wozniak built the first Apple was literally a hobbyist meetup.

## The Big Tech era (2000-today)

- The five biggest tech companies, sometimes called **FAANG** (Facebook, Apple, Amazon, Netflix, Google) or **MAGMA** (Microsoft, Apple, Google, Meta, Amazon) depending on the year, have market caps that rival the GDP of medium-sized countries.
- **Apple** alone was worth over $3 trillion at peak, more than the GDP of every country except the US, China, Japan, Germany, and India.
- Software is now embedded in everything: retail (Amazon ate it), media (Netflix and YouTube ate it), advertising (Google and Meta ate it), hardware (Apple), enterprise IT (Microsoft).
- The current wave is **AI**. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and a handful of others are competing to build the foundation models. The frontier labs are themselves becoming a third power center distinct from old SV.

## Why these are the two pillars

- **Software is in everything**. Manufacturing, logistics, retail, transportation, finance itself. The companies that own the dominant software platforms have leverage over every industry that runs on them.
- **Finance funds everything**. Every new factory, every startup, every infrastructure project, every public company depends on Wall Street capital. Even Silicon Valley's venture capital ultimately taps into the same pools of pension fund and endowment money that Wall Street manages.
- The two sectors are entangled. Tech companies IPO on Wall Street. Wall Street uses Silicon Valley software to run its trading. They share talent (a Stanford MBA can go to Goldman or to a venture firm).

## Their cultural pull

- Top graduates from Harvard, Stanford, Wharton, and MIT increasingly head to one of these two places. Roughly 40 percent of Harvard graduates take jobs in finance or tech.
- The founders and financiers are celebrities in a way bankers and engineers never used to be. **Elon Musk**, **Mark Zuckerberg**, **Sam Altman**, **Jeff Bezos**, **Jamie Dimon**, **Larry Fink**. Their tweets move markets and shape policy debates.

## Their politics

- For most of the 2000s and 2010s, Silicon Valley leaned strongly Democratic. Tech workers donated heavily to Obama. The Valley's social libertarianism (pro-gay-marriage, pro-immigration, pro-environment) fit the Democratic coalition.
- That has fractured. A **tech-right** faction has emerged around figures like **Peter Thiel**, **Marc Andreessen**, **David Sacks**, and **Elon Musk**. Their concerns: regulation, taxes, what they see as cultural overreach from the left, and competition with China. Many moved to Trump in 2024.
- Wall Street has historically been more Republican on taxes and regulation, more Democratic on social issues. Wall Street CEOs tend to be politically careful in public.

## The current question

- The next decade's defining question is what happens when AI rearranges both sectors at once.
- AI threatens to automate large parts of finance (research, trading, advisory work) and much of software development itself.
- The leverage is shifting toward the small number of companies that own the **frontier models**. If you believe the labs themselves, the entire competitive picture for both Wall Street and Silicon Valley gets restructured by the AI labs that are emerging as a new pillar.
- The honest answer right now: nobody knows. But the smart money is watching the model labs, not the incumbents.

## What people get wrong

- Wall Street is not just one thing. Investment banks (Goldman, Morgan Stanley), commercial banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America), hedge funds, private equity, and asset managers (BlackRock manages $10 trillion) all do different things.
- Silicon Valley is no longer just a place. Tech hubs in Austin, Miami, Seattle, and remote-first companies have spread it. But the Bay Area still has the densest concentration of talent and capital.
- These two sectors are not all of American power. Healthcare, energy, defense, and Hollywood matter. But finance and tech are the two with the most leverage over everything else.

## What to carry away

- Wall Street runs the money. Silicon Valley runs the software the money runs on.
- Both grew enormously after 1980 deregulation and the rise of the internet.
- They produce most of the country's billionaires and absorb most of its elite talent.
- AI is the next wave that could either entrench both or create a new pillar on top of them.
- In one sentence: if you want to know who actually shapes the modern American economy, look at Wall Street and Silicon Valley together, because together is how they work.
