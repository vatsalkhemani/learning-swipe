# The American Revolution: how thirteen colonies broke from history's largest empire

## What it was and why it matters

The American Revolution was the war and political project, roughly 1775 to 1783, by which thirteen British colonies in North America declared independence and won it on the battlefield. It produced the Declaration of Independence, the Constitution, and the Bill of Rights, and founded the republic that became the dominant world power within two centuries. The founding arguments are still cited in every American constitutional debate. The "American experiment" identity, the idea that the country was built on a set of stated principles instead of blood and soil, traces directly to this moment.

## How the fight started

- Through most of the 1700s the colonies prospered under loose imperial rule. London mostly left them alone and they mostly paid no direct taxes.
- The **French and Indian War (1754-63)**, the North American piece of the Seven Years' War, broke that. Britain won, took French Canada, and ended up deep in debt.
- To recoup costs, Britain taxed the colonies directly: **Stamp Act 1765**, **Townshend Acts 1767**, **Tea Act 1773**. The slogan that came out of it was "no taxation without representation."
- **Boston Tea Party, December 16, 1773.** The **Sons of Liberty** dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea into Boston Harbor. It is often remembered as anti-tax. It was also a protest against a monopoly Britain had granted the East India Company, which would have undercut local merchants.
- Britain answered with the **Coercive Acts of 1774**, closing Boston harbor and stripping Massachusetts of self-government. The colonies treated this as a constitutional crisis.
- **First Continental Congress**, September 1774, in Philadelphia, with delegates from twelve of the thirteen colonies.
- **Lexington and Concord, April 19, 1775.** British troops marched to seize colonial weapons, militia fired back, and the war started. Emerson later called it "the shot heard 'round the world."
- **Second Continental Congress**, June 1775, named **George Washington** commander of the Continental Army.
- **Declaration of Independence, July 4, 1776**, drafted by **Thomas Jefferson** with the Committee of Five (Jefferson, **John Adams**, **Benjamin Franklin**, Roger Sherman, Robert Livingston).

## The war itself

- The Continental Army was outnumbered, undertrained, and poorly supplied, fighting the strongest military in the world.
- What saved them: British supply lines stretched across the Atlantic, the geography was enormous, and the war was unpopular at home in Britain.
- **Battle of Saratoga, October 1777** was the turning point. American victory convinced France the colonies could win.
- **France entered the war in 1778**, secured largely by Franklin's diplomacy in Paris. French money, French troops, and a French fleet changed the math.
- **Yorktown, October 1781.** Washington, the French general **Lafayette**, and the French fleet trapped British general **Cornwallis** on the Virginia coast. Cornwallis surrendered. Effectively the end.
- **Treaty of Paris 1783.** Britain recognized American independence and ceded territory west to the Mississippi River.

## The figures worth knowing

- **George Washington** held an outmatched army together for eight years, then walked away from power twice. The "American Cincinnatus" myth was built on that.
- **Benjamin Franklin**, the diplomat in Paris, secured the French alliance that won the war.
- **Thomas Jefferson**, principal author of the Declaration. Slaveholder. The tension between his words and his life is the country's tension in miniature.
- **John Adams**, the lawyer who pushed independence in Congress and later second president.
- **Alexander Hamilton**, immigrant aide to Washington, later the architect of American finance and a Federalist.
- **Lafayette**, French aristocrat who volunteered at 19 and became one of Washington's closest commanders.

## From war to constitution

- The **Articles of Confederation (1781-89)**, the first national framework, set up a federal government too weak to tax, regulate trade, or enforce anything. It nearly broke the country in a decade.
- The **Constitutional Convention** in Philadelphia, summer 1787, scrapped the Articles and drafted the **Constitution**. Ratified in 1788.
- The **Bill of Rights**, the first ten amendments, ratified 1791. Free speech, due process, protection from unreasonable search, and the rest of the rights Americans now consider baseline.

## The ideas underneath

- **John Locke's natural rights**: life, liberty, property. Jefferson edited "property" to "the pursuit of happiness" but the lineage is obvious.
- **Montesquieu's separation of powers**: the three-branch design of the federal government is straight Montesquieu.
- Broader **Enlightenment** political philosophy that argued government legitimacy comes from the consent of the governed, not divine right.

## The tensions the founders baked in

- **Slavery.** The Constitution counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation purposes, protected the slave trade for twenty years, and required the return of escaped people. The founders argued about it and chose union over abolition. The country fought a civil war about it eighty years later.
- **Native displacement.** "The West" was treated as open for taking. It wasn't. The dispossession of Native nations was a precondition for the country's expansion.
- **Federalism.** How much power belongs to the federal government versus the states was the central argument between **Hamilton** (more federal) and **Jefferson** (more state), and it is still the argument behind nearly every modern Supreme Court case.

## What people get wrong

- It wasn't unanimous. Estimates put loyalists at 15-20% of the colonial population, with another large chunk indifferent. Many fled to Canada after the war.
- It wasn't a tax revolt by tax-haters. The fight was about who had the authority to tax, not whether taxes existed.
- It wasn't only fought by farmers with muskets. French regulars, French ships, and French money were central. The American story tends to forget that.

## What to carry away

- A small set of colonies on the edge of an empire wrote a list of principles, fought eight years to back them up, and then designed a government around the principles.
- The founders disagreed sharply, and they baked their disagreements into the system on purpose. Federalism, the Bill of Rights, and the unresolved questions on slavery and Native land are all evidence of that.
- In one sentence: the Revolution founded a republic on stated principles, with the country's deepest contradictions written into the same documents, and Americans have been arguing inside that frame ever since.
