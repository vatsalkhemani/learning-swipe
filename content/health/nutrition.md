# Nutrition: macros, fiber, protein, and the signal-to-noise on food advice

## Why nutrition advice is so confusing

No field in health has worse signal-to-noise. The reasons are structural. Nutrition studies are mostly observational, because feeding humans controlled diets for decades is expensive and almost impossible. People lie on food diaries. The food industry funds research that supports its products. Magazines need a new diet story every year. Influencers find an audience by being extreme. The result is a world where every food has been called both poison and superfood, often by the same outlet within a decade. The actual consensus, when you strip out the noise, is boring and largely unchanged since the 1990s: eat enough protein and fiber, keep processed food low, and the rest is detail.

## What the consensus actually is

- **Protein adequate.** Roughly **0.7-1g per pound of bodyweight per day** for active adults. Most people, especially older adults and women, under-consume. Protein keeps you full, preserves muscle, and is the macronutrient with the strongest "more is fine" evidence.
- **Fiber adequate.** Roughly **30-40g per day**, from vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, nuts. Most Americans get half this. Fiber feeds gut bacteria, regulates blood sugar, lowers cholesterol, and is the macronutrient with the strongest case for "almost everyone needs more."
- **Processed and ultra-processed food kept low.** The strongest dietary finding of the last decade. **Kevin Hall**'s 2019 NIH study locked subjects in a metabolic ward and fed them either ultra-processed or minimally processed diets matched for calories and macros. The ultra-processed group ate about 500 more calories a day without realizing it. Ultra-processed food is engineered to be hyperpalatable, easy to overconsume, and almost impossible to feel satiated on.
- **Calories matter, but they matter less than the food choices that drive them.** Whole foods naturally cap intake. Processed foods uncap it.

## The diet debates

- Keto, paleo, vegan, carnivore, Mediterranean, intermittent fasting, Atkins. All of them work for weight loss IF total calories drop. None of them works if total calories stay the same.
- The diet you can stick to is the diet that works. Adherence dominates.
- Where they differ is long-term cardiovascular and metabolic health. **The Mediterranean diet has the strongest evidence**: heavy on vegetables, fruit, fish, olive oil, nuts, whole grains, beans, with modest dairy and meat. The **PREDIMED trial** (2013, Spain) randomized over 7,000 people to Mediterranean or low-fat diets and saw a 30% reduction in major cardiovascular events for the Mediterranean group. The **Blue Zones** work by Dan Buettner identified five regions of unusual longevity and found all of them eat something close to a Mediterranean pattern.
- Keto is genuinely useful for some conditions, including epilepsy and some metabolic syndrome cases. As a longevity diet it lacks evidence and is hard to sustain.
- Veganism done well is healthy. Done badly it's a B12, iron, and protein deficiency waiting to happen.

## Sugar, fat, and the politics that shaped them

- **Sugar.** Causes an insulin spike, drives fat storage when chronically high, contributes to **glycation** (sugar binding to proteins, damaging tissues over time), and is genuinely habit-forming for many people. Isolated sugar in moderation isn't poison. Sustained excess is the problem, especially from sugary drinks, which deliver large doses without satiety.
- **Fat got vilified wrongly in the 1980s.** The story is worth knowing. **Ancel Keys**, a researcher whose Seven Countries Study in the 1950s linked saturated fat to heart disease, became the dominant figure. His data selection has since been challenged. **Cristin Kearns**, a dental researcher, dug through internal Sugar Association archives and showed the industry quietly funded research to deflect attention from sugar onto fat in the 1960s and 70s. The resulting "low-fat" era produced products like SnackWell's cookies that were low in fat and high in sugar, and Americans got fatter through the 80s and 90s.
- Saturated fat is more nuanced than the 1980s consensus suggested. Trans fats are clearly bad. Olive oil and fatty fish are clearly good. Red meat is debated, but the case against unprocessed red meat in moderation is weaker than headlines suggest. Processed meat (bacon, sausage, deli) has the firmest evidence for increased cancer and cardiovascular risk.

## Calorie tracking, hydration, and coffee

- **Calorie tracking.** Useful for about 2 weeks to learn portion sizes. After that, most people can eyeball it and the tracking becomes a chore that breeds disordered behavior. Athletes and people with specific physique goals are exceptions.
- **Hydration.** The "8 glasses a day" rule is made up. It traces back to a misread 1945 nutrition board recommendation that included water from food. Drink when thirsty. Urine should be pale yellow. Dark yellow means drink more. Clear means you're overdoing it.
- **Coffee.** Probably net positive. Multiple meta-analyses link **3-4 cups a day** to lower all-cause mortality, lower type 2 diabetes risk, and lower risk of several cancers. The caffeine ceiling is around **400mg a day** for most adults, the equivalent of 4 cups of brewed coffee. Cut earlier in the day to protect sleep.
- **Alcohol.** The old "a glass of red wine is good for you" finding has not held up. The current best estimate from large meta-analyses is that no amount of alcohol is health-positive. Moderate use is a personal call about enjoyment and social life, not health.

## What people get wrong

- Obsessing over a single nutrient or food category usually misses the point.
- "Clean eating" as identity often drives orthorexia rather than health.
- Detoxes and cleanses are largely marketing. The liver and kidneys detox continuously.
- Most supplements lack evidence. Vitamin D in deficient people, omega-3 for some, creatine for muscle and possibly cognition, and a few others have real data. Multivitamins are mostly insurance.

## What to carry away

- Eat enough protein. Eat enough fiber. Keep ultra-processed food rare. The rest is detail.
- The diet you sustain beats the diet that's optimal on paper.
- Mediterranean is the safest default if you want a single pattern with the most evidence behind it.
- In one sentence: nutrition is loud, mostly because the boring answer doesn't sell, and the boring answer is enough whole food, enough protein, enough fiber, not too much engineered food.
