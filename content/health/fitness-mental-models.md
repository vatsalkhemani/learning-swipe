# Mental models for fitness: what actually matters past 30

## What this is and why it matters

The fitness industry sells complexity. Most of it is noise. A handful of mental models explain almost everything that actually moves the needle, and once you internalize them you can ignore most of what passes for advice on social media. Past 30, the stakes shift. Muscle starts melting, joints start complaining, cardiovascular fitness starts mattering for reasons more serious than how you look in photos. This is about the principles that survive contact with real life, not the latest program or supplement. It matters because the boring fundamentals work, and the flashy distractions don't, and most people get this exactly backwards.

## The compound interest of consistency

- Three workouts a week for ten years beats six workouts a week for six months.
- Most fitness gains plateau without sustained consistency. The body adapts to imposed demand, then needs ongoing demand to keep adapting.
- The **"exercise snack" idea** (Stuart Phillips at McMaster University, among others) — short bursts of activity throughout the day work and may be easier to sustain than dedicated hour-long sessions.
- The person who shows up imperfectly for years beats the person who is perfect for three months and then quits, every time.

## Progressive overload

- Muscles, bones, tendons, and cardiovascular systems adapt to demand. Increasing demand drives further adaptation. Without overload, you maintain rather than grow.
- Applies to everything: weights (add reps or load), running (add distance or pace), mobility (push range of motion gradually).
- The increments don't need to be heroic. They need to be regular. Five extra pounds a month on a major lift is meaningful over years.

## Specificity (SAID — Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands)

- You get good at what you practice. Running builds running fitness, not deadlift strength. Bodybuilding builds muscle, not necessarily strength expressed in sport-like ways. Strength training builds force production, but not endurance.
- Implications people get wrong:
  - Marathon training won't build muscle. In fact, it usually costs you some.
  - "Functional training" is real but specific. Squatting on a bosu ball makes you good at squatting on a bosu ball.
  - Doing cardio for "general fitness" without resistance training leaves a hole that matters more every decade.
- Pick the adaptations you want, train accordingly, accept the trade-offs.

## Recovery is when adaptation happens

- Training is the stimulus. Recovery is the response. The actual growth, strength gain, and cardiovascular adaptation happen during rest and sleep, not during the workout itself.
- Without adequate sleep, food, and rest days, training breaks you down without rebuilding.
- Most "overtraining" is actually undersleeping and undereating. Athletes who train hard and recover hard outperform athletes who just train hard.

## The Minimum Effective Dose (MED) principle

- Less work for similar gains is often possible, once you've passed the beginner phase.
- **Strength training** — about 2-3 sets per exercise, 2-3 times per week, hits most of the gains. The old bodybuilding-era prescription of 10+ sets per body part per session has been largely debunked for non-competitive lifters.
- **Cardio** — 150 minutes per week of moderate activity (CDC minimum) plus 75 minutes of vigorous activity captures roughly 80% of the health benefits seen in dose-response curves. Returns diminish steeply after that.
- More isn't always better. Past a point, more is worse because it eats into recovery.

## VO2 max is the single best longevity predictor

- The **Mandsager 2018 study** in JAMA found that cardiorespiratory fitness, measured by VO2 max, correlated with all-cause mortality more strongly than any other modifiable factor. Stronger than cholesterol, stronger than blood pressure, stronger than smoking status.
- **Peter Attia** has popularized this aggressively — improving VO2 max is, by current evidence, the most powerful single lever for lifespan and healthspan.
- The practical implication: train cardiovascular fitness seriously, not as an afterthought to weights.

## Zone 2 versus HIIT

- **Zone 2** — conversational pace, roughly 60-70% of max heart rate. Builds mitochondrial density and metabolic flexibility. Requires more time per session but is easy to recover from.
- **HIIT** — high-intensity intervals, around 90%+ of max HR. Time-efficient but harder to recover from. Builds VO2 max quickly.
- **Iñigo San Millán** (who works with Tour de France cyclists) and Peter Attia argue for mostly Zone 2 with some HIIT layered in. The split they suggest is roughly 80% easy, 20% hard.
- Both work. The mix depends on your time, recovery, and goals.

## Strength training past 30 isn't optional

- Muscle mass declines roughly 3-8% per decade after 30, accelerating after 60. The clinical term is **sarcopenia**.
- Strength training reverses this trajectory at any age. **Mike Israetel, Jeff Nippard,** and others have made the scientific case well.
- Frequency around 2-3 times per week works for most people.
- Compound movements (squat, deadlift, bench press, row, overhead press) cover the most ground per minute of training.
- This isn't optional past 40, and ideally you start much earlier. Older people who fall and break a hip often lose the next several years of their life. Strength is how you avoid being that person.

## Mobility and stability

- Get neglected until injuries hit in the 40s. Joints, tendons, and connective tissue need maintenance even when nothing hurts.
- Not the centerpiece of training. But a regular small dose pays compounding returns.

## Sleep is part of training

- Training without adequate sleep wastes the effort. Hormonal recovery, tissue repair, and consolidation of motor learning all happen during sleep.
- 7-9 hours, consistent timing, with enough deep and REM sleep cycles.
- If sleep is broken, dial the training back. You can't compensate.

## What doesn't matter as much as marketing claims

- Perfect form on every single rep. Form should be good, not obsessive.
- Complicated periodization for non-competitive lifters.
- Most pre-workout supplements (caffeine is the exception that genuinely works).
- Fasted cardio for fat loss. Total daily calories matter far more.
- Lots of stretching. Some is good. It isn't the centerpiece.
- The latest viral exercise from Instagram. Almost always a variation on something that already worked.

## What actually matters

- Showing up consistently.
- Progressing modestly over years.
- Recovering well (sleep, food, rest).
- Doing the boring basics: compound lifts, Zone 2, some HIIT, some mobility, lots of sleep.
- Picking a level of intensity you can sustain for decades, not months.

## What to carry away

- Most fitness advice is noise. The principles that work are unglamorous and well-known.
- Past 30, strength and cardiovascular fitness aren't aesthetic concerns. They're longevity concerns.
- VO2 max and muscle mass are the two biggest levers, and both respond to consistent training.
- In one sentence: consistency beats intensity, the boring fundamentals work, and the body that lasts is built by showing up for years.
