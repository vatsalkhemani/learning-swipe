# Vaccines and immunity: how your body learns, and the politics around it

## What this is and why it matters

Vaccines have saved roughly 150 million lives in the last 50 years, mostly children's. They are one of the highest-impact public health interventions ever invented. They are also one of the most politically charged topics in modern medicine, with misinformation spreading faster than the diseases they prevent. Understanding the basic biology of how vaccines work, and the history of how they've been received, helps you read the news more clearly and make decisions for yourself and your family. It matters because this is one area where what you don't know can genuinely hurt you.

## How immunity actually works

Your immune system has two arms:

- **Innate immunity** — general, fast, present from birth. Fever, inflammation, natural killer (NK) cells. It doesn't know what specific threat it's fighting; it just attacks anything that looks wrong.
- **Adaptive immunity** — specific, slow the first time, fast every time after. This is what vaccines train.

Inside the adaptive system:

- **B cells** make **antibodies**, proteins that bind to specific pathogens like a key to a lock.
- **T cells** come in two main flavors. **Killer T cells** destroy infected cells. **Helper T cells** coordinate the wider response.
- **Memory cells** persist long after the initial infection or vaccination. When the same pathogen returns, they recognize it instantly and mount a faster, stronger response.

Vaccines exploit this memory by introducing your immune system to a pathogen, or part of one, in a way that triggers learning without making you sick.

## Types of vaccines

- **Live attenuated** — weakened version of the actual pathogen. MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), yellow fever, oral polio. Strongest, longest-lasting immunity, but can't be given to severely immunocompromised people.
- **Inactivated** — killed pathogen. Flu shot, hepatitis A, injected polio. Safer but typically needs boosters.
- **Subunit or recombinant** — only proteins from the pathogen, not the whole thing. Hepatitis B, HPV (Gardasil), shingles (Shingrix).
- **Toxoid** — inactivated toxins, used when the toxin is what causes harm. Tetanus, diphtheria.
- **mRNA** — messenger RNA instructions that tell your cells to make a piece of the pathogen (the spike protein, for COVID), which your immune system then learns. Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID vaccines.
- **Viral vector** — a harmless modified virus delivers genetic material from the target pathogen. J&J and AstraZeneca COVID vaccines, Ebola.

## How vaccines normally get developed

The traditional timeline is 10 to 15 years:

- **Phase 1** — about 20-100 people. Safety and dose-finding.
- **Phase 2** — hundreds of people. Dose refinement and immune response measurement.
- **Phase 3** — thousands to tens of thousands. Efficacy against actual disease, plus rarer safety signals.

After approval, **Phase 4** continues monitoring once the vaccine is in widespread use.

## Why COVID vaccines came so fast

COVID vaccines compressed roughly a decade of work into about a year. The reasons:

- **Parallel phases** instead of sequential. Phase 1, 2, and 3 trials overlapped instead of waiting for each to finish.
- **Massive funding** — **Operation Warp Speed** poured over $10 billion into multiple candidates, so developers didn't have to wait for one trial to finish before scaling manufacturing on others.
- **Platform readiness** — mRNA research dated back to the 1990s. **Katalin Karikó's** persistent work on modified mRNA, when most of the field had given up on her, made it possible. She and **Drew Weissman** won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it.
- **Recruitment was easy** — a raging pandemic meant trials filled fast and accumulated enough cases to measure efficacy quickly.

The speed wasn't because corners were cut. It was because money, infrastructure, and conditions all aligned at once.

## Herd immunity

When enough of a population is immune, a pathogen can't sustain transmission and outbreaks die out. The threshold depends on the pathogen's basic reproduction number (R0):

- **Measles** R0 is roughly 12-18. You need about 95% immunity to stop it.
- **Original COVID** R0 was roughly 3. Omicron variants pushed R0 above 10.
- COVID's combination of waning immunity, evolving variants, and breakthrough infection means classic herd immunity through vaccination alone is essentially unachievable for this virus, though widespread immunity still reduces severity.

## Adverse events

- Most vaccines have rare serious reactions (anaphylaxis on the order of 1 in 1 million doses).
- These rates are dwarfed by the disease risks they prevent. Pre-vaccine, measles killed millions of children a year. Now it kills hundreds of thousands, almost all in undervaccinated regions.
- Real but rare side effects exist. Myocarditis after mRNA COVID vaccines, mostly in young men, is real but typically mild. Risk from COVID infection itself is higher.

## The history of vaccine hesitancy

- **Andrew Wakefield's 1998 Lancet paper** falsely linked the MMR vaccine to autism. It was based on 12 children, with fraudulent data and undisclosed financial conflicts. The paper was retracted in 2010 and Wakefield's medical license revoked. The damage to public trust is still being measured, decades later.
- Measles is returning to communities with low vaccination rates. **Samoa 2019** (over 80 deaths, mostly children, after vaccination rates collapsed). **New York Orthodox Jewish communities 2019**. The US in 2024 has seen the largest measles surge in years.

## COVID's polarization

COVID vaccines became political in ways earlier vaccines weren't:

- Left-right coding emerged in the US around mandates and personal choice.
- Early efficacy claims oversold transmission reduction. The vaccines reduced severity strongly. They reduced transmission less than initially hoped, and protection waned over months.
- Mandates were controversial and amplified existing distrust.
- This isn't a reason to distrust vaccines generally. It's a reason to read claims carefully, including from official sources.

## The future: mRNA platform

The mRNA platform that powered COVID vaccines has implications beyond infectious disease:

- **Cancer vaccines** — personalized neoantigen approaches, where the vaccine targets mutations specific to a patient's tumor. **Moderna, BioNTech, and Genentech** have shown early promise in melanoma and pancreatic cancer trials.
- **Other infectious diseases** — RSV, flu, HIV, malaria are all in development.

## What to carry away

- Vaccines work by training your adaptive immune system without making you sick.
- They are among the highest-impact medical interventions ever invented.
- Real risks exist but are rare and well-characterized.
- The biggest threats to vaccine programs aren't side effects but misinformation and complacency.
- In one sentence: understanding how vaccines actually work helps you sort signal from noise in a topic where the noise is loud and the stakes are real.
