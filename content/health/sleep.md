# Sleep: REM, deep, circadian rhythm, and why everyone says 7-9 hours

## What sleep is and why it matters

Sleep used to be treated as a passive state, the thing that happened when nothing else was happening. That view collapsed in the last two decades. We now know sleep is when the brain runs maintenance the waking brain can't run: consolidating memory, clearing metabolic waste, regulating hormones, repairing tissue, processing emotion. **Matthew Walker**'s *Why We Sleep* (2017) became the public flashpoint. His TED talk has over 25 million views. The headline finding is brutally simple: short sleep, sustained over years, raises your risk of nearly every disease people fear. It matters because sleep is the highest-leverage health intervention available, and most adults are quietly under-sleeping without realizing it.

## The architecture of a night

- Sleep moves in roughly **90-minute cycles**. A normal adult runs about five of them per night, which is where the 7.5-hour target comes from.
- Each cycle has two big phases. **NREM** (non-rapid eye movement) and **REM** (rapid eye movement). NREM is split into three stages.
- **Stage 1 NREM.** A few minutes. Drifting off. Easy to wake.
- **Stage 2 NREM.** Light sleep. Body temperature drops, heart rate slows. Sleep spindles fire, which seem to help lock in memory.
- **Stage 3 NREM, also called deep sleep or slow-wave sleep.** This is the heavy restoration phase. Growth hormone releases. The glymphatic system flushes waste from brain tissue, including beta-amyloid, the protein implicated in Alzheimer's. Most deep sleep happens in the first half of the night.
- **REM sleep.** Brain activity spikes to near-waking levels. Eyes dart. Body is paralyzed. This is when most vivid dreams happen. REM dominates the second half of the night.
- Deep sleep does memory consolidation and physical repair. REM does emotional processing and creative recombination. You need both. Cut your sleep from 8 hours to 6 and you lose a disproportionate share of REM, because the REM-heavy cycles come last.

## The circadian rhythm

- The body runs on a roughly **24-hour internal clock** called the circadian rhythm. The master clock sits in the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** in the hypothalamus, a cluster of about 20,000 neurons.
- The clock is set by **light hitting the retina**, particularly blue-spectrum light in the morning. This is why morning sunlight is the single strongest signal for fixing a broken sleep schedule.
- Two hormones run the show. **Cortisol** peaks in the morning to wake you up. **Melatonin** rises in the evening to wind you down. Bright light in the evening, especially from screens, suppresses melatonin and delays the whole system.
- **Andrew Huberman** at Stanford has been the loudest voice popularizing the "10 minutes of sunlight in your eyes within an hour of waking" protocol. The underlying research is older but solid.
- Caffeine works by blocking adenosine, the sleep-pressure molecule that builds up the longer you're awake. Caffeine has a half-life of about 5-6 hours. A 2pm coffee still has a quarter of its dose in your system at midnight.

## What chronic short sleep does

- Defined as routinely getting under 6 hours. About a third of American adults fall here.
- **Immune system.** A single short night cuts natural killer cell activity by around 70%. Chronic deficit raises infection and cancer risk.
- **Cardiovascular.** Higher rates of hypertension, heart attack, stroke. The week we shift to daylight saving and lose an hour, heart attacks measurably spike.
- **Metabolic.** Insulin sensitivity drops fast. A week of 5-hour nights makes a healthy young adult look pre-diabetic on glucose tolerance tests.
- **Cognitive.** Attention, working memory, and emotional regulation all degrade. People scoring badly on cognitive tests after short sleep consistently rate themselves as fine. The "I do great on 5 hours" people are a real population, and almost all of them are wrong. The genuine short-sleeper gene is vanishingly rare, well under 1% of the population.
- **Long-term brain risk.** The glymphatic clearance work means chronic short sleep is now considered a meaningful risk factor for Alzheimer's.

## What actually works

- **Consistent wake time** matters more than consistent bedtime. The wake time anchors the circadian clock. Weekend lie-ins drift the system.
- **Cool, dark, quiet room.** Around 65-68°F is the consensus. Body temperature needs to drop to sleep well.
- **No caffeine after about 2pm.** Personalize down if you're sensitive.
- **No screens in the last hour**, or use night mode and dim them aggressively. Blue light is the bigger issue than the content.
- **No heavy meals or alcohol close to sleep.** Alcohol knocks you out but destroys REM. You wake feeling worse despite "sleeping" longer.
- **Morning sunlight, ideally in the first hour after waking.** Ten minutes outside, no sunglasses.
- **Exercise helps sleep.** Avoid intense workouts in the last 2-3 hours before bed.

## Naps, debt, and recovery

- **A 20-minute power nap** is fine. You stay in light sleep and wake refreshed.
- **A 90-minute nap** is fine. You complete a full cycle.
- **A 60-minute nap** is the worst option. You wake mid-deep-sleep, groggy, with sleep inertia that can last an hour.
- **Sleep debt is real.** One night of short sleep is partially recoverable over the following few nights. Chronic debt is not fully recoverable. You can't bank sleep on the weekend to pay for a depleted week, no matter how good Sunday feels.
- **Naps after 3pm** start eating into your sleep pressure and can wreck the following night.

## What people get wrong

- "I'm fine on 5 hours" is almost always wrong. Self-rating breaks down under sleep deprivation.
- Alcohol is not a sleep aid. It's a sedative that destroys sleep architecture.
- Sleeping pills usually produce sedation, not real sleep, and most have side effects with chronic use.
- You can't train yourself to need less sleep. The need is biological, not psychological.

## What to carry away

- Sleep is the foundation under everything else: exercise, nutrition, mental health, cognition.
- Get the boring inputs right first. Consistent wake time, morning light, cool dark room, no late caffeine, no late screens.
- Aim for the architecture, not just the hours. Eight hours of disrupted sleep is worse than seven good ones.
- In one sentence: sleep is the brain and body's nightly maintenance window, and shorting it for years is the most quietly expensive thing most people do to themselves.
