# Aging biology: what scientists actually know about why we get old

## What aging biology is and why it matters

Aging used to be considered an inevitable part of being alive, the natural background against which diseases happened. The field of **geroscience** flipped that framing in the last two decades. Aging itself is now treated as a biological process with mechanisms that can be identified, measured, and possibly slowed. Even if you have no interest in living to 150, the relevant prize is **healthspan**, which is the years of healthy life you get before decline. The science is plausible, the interventions are imperfect but real, and a multi-billion-dollar industry is now built on the bet that this works. It matters because it reframes most chronic disease as downstream of aging itself.

## The Hallmarks of Aging

In 2013, **Carlos López-Otín** and colleagues published a landmark paper identifying nine "Hallmarks of Aging." The 2023 update expanded it to twelve. These hallmarks interlock and reinforce each other, and most therapies target one or more of them:

- **Genomic instability** — DNA damage accumulates faster than repair systems can fix.
- **Telomere attrition** — the protective caps on chromosomes shorten each time a cell divides, eventually triggering senescence.
- **Epigenetic alterations** — patterns of gene expression drift over time, even though the DNA itself is the same.
- **Loss of proteostasis** — protein folding errors accumulate, contributing to diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's.
- **Deregulated nutrient sensing** — pathways like mTOR, IGF-1, and AMPK that respond to food signals get worn out.
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction** — cellular power plants leak and lose efficiency.
- **Cellular senescence** — cells stop dividing but don't die, becoming "zombie cells" that secrete inflammatory signals.
- **Stem cell exhaustion** — the cells that replenish tissues lose capacity.
- **Altered intercellular communication** — chronic low-grade inflammation rises ("inflammaging").
- **Disabled macroautophagy** — the cell's cleanup system slows down.
- **Chronic inflammation**.
- **Dysbiosis** — the gut microbiome shifts unfavorably.

## Key concepts and the science behind them

- **Telomeres** — **Elizabeth Blackburn** won the 2009 Nobel Prize for her work on telomeres and telomerase, the enzyme that can extend them. Boosting telomerase artificially carries cancer risk, since immortal cells are what cancer is.
- **Cellular senescence** — these zombie cells accumulate with age and drive systemic inflammation. **Senolytic drugs**, which selectively kill senescent cells, are an active area. The combination of **dasatinib (a leukemia drug) and quercetin (a plant compound)** has shown promise in early human trials.
- **Caloric restriction and mTOR** — restricting calories by 20-30% extends lifespan in nearly every species tested from yeast to monkeys. The mechanism runs through the **mTOR** pathway, a nutrient sensor. **Rapamycin**, an immunosuppressant used in organ transplants, inhibits mTOR and extends mouse lifespan by 10-25%. Some longevity-focused researchers take it off-label.
- **Metformin** — a diabetes drug costing roughly $5 a month, with possible anti-aging effects beyond its glucose control. The **TAME trial** (Targeting Aging with Metformin), led by **Nir Barzilai**, is recruiting to test it as a longevity intervention.
- **NAD+** — a coenzyme essential for energy metabolism. Levels decline with age. Supplements like NMN and NR aim to restore them, with mixed evidence in humans so far.
- **Parabiosis** — experiments joining the circulation of old and young mice rejuvenated tissues in the older mouse, pointing to blood-borne factors that change with age. **GDF11** was identified as a candidate, though results are contested.
- **The Information Theory of Aging** — **David Sinclair** at Harvard argues aging is fundamentally a loss of epigenetic information, theoretically reversible. His lab has shown that **Yamanaka factor** partial reprogramming can rejuvenate aged tissues in mice. Controversial within the field but generating real experiments.

## The industry forming around this

- **Calico** — Alphabet-funded longevity research company, founded 2013. Quiet, well-funded, long horizon.
- **Altos Labs** — launched 2022 with over $3 billion in initial funding, including investment from Jeff Bezos. Recruited **Shinya Yamanaka** (2012 Nobel for induced pluripotent stem cells) as senior scientific advisor.
- **Loyal** — extending dog lifespan first, since regulatory paths for pet drugs are shorter. Humans planned downstream.
- **Bryan Johnson's Blueprint** — a self-experiment by a tech founder spending millions a year on extreme lifestyle and supplement interventions, tracking hundreds of biomarkers. Controversial, scientifically uneven, but influential as a cultural moment.

## What actually has evidence in humans

The interventions with strongest evidence aren't pharmaceutical. They're the boring fundamentals:

- **Exercise** — both cardio and strength. The single most robust longevity intervention.
- **Sleep** — adequate, consistent sleep correlates with nearly every biomarker of healthy aging.
- **Diet** — fewer calories from refined sources, more fiber and protein, more plants. Specific diets matter less than the general direction.
- **Stress management** — chronic stress shortens telomeres and accelerates inflammation.
- **Social connection** — Harvard's 80-year Study of Adult Development found relationship quality predicted late-life health better than cholesterol.

## What didn't pan out

- **Resveratrol**, the red wine compound, was the early Sinclair-era darling. Effects at human-achievable doses turned out negligible.
- Most off-the-shelf "anti-aging" supplements have weak evidence.
- The "single magic molecule" framing keeps disappointing, because aging has too many interlocking mechanisms.

## Longevity escape velocity

**Aubrey de Grey** popularized the idea that if treatments extend life faster than time passes, you reach a point where each year gains you more than a year. The thesis is provocative, possibly correct in principle, and unfalsifiable in the near term.

## What to carry away

- Aging is now studied as a tractable biological process, not as a backdrop.
- The hallmarks framework gives a real map of what's going wrong, and most interventions target one or more.
- The biggest gains for most people come from the boring fundamentals, not from drugs or supplements.
- In one sentence: the science is plausible enough to take seriously, immature enough to be skeptical of any single product, and the surest play is the unglamorous one — exercise, sleep, diet, and stress.
