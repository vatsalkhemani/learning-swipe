# Cognitive biases: the half-dozen ways your mind quietly lies to you

## What cognitive biases are and why they matter

A cognitive bias is a systematic error in how your brain processes information. Not a random mistake. A predictable, repeatable mistake that shows up in the same shape across millions of people. The biases exist because the brain evolved to make fast decisions in a world where being roughly right and quick beat being precisely right and slow. The same shortcuts that worked on the savannah misfire in modern environments with spreadsheets, term sheets, and 30-year mortgages. **Daniel Kahneman** and **Amos Tversky** built the modern catalog through their experiments in the 1970s. The biases below are the half-dozen that show up most often in business, investing, and everyday judgment.

## Anchoring: the first number sets the frame

- Whatever number you see first becomes the gravitational center of the negotiation, even when you know it is arbitrary.
- **The classic experiment.** Kahneman and Tversky spun a wheel rigged to land on 10 or 65, then asked subjects what percentage of UN member states were African. The people who saw 10 guessed 25%. The people who saw 65 guessed 45%. A random number from a spinning wheel moved the answer by 20 points.
- **Real example.** A house listed at $1.2M and a house listed at $1.4M sell for different prices even if they are identical. The list price anchors the buyer's sense of fair value.
- **In negotiations.** Whoever names the first number wins more often than they should. The myth that you should "never make the first offer" is mostly wrong. Make the first offer when you have good information about value.

## Confirmation bias: you seek evidence that fits what you already believe

- You google to confirm, not to learn. You read the articles that agree with you and skim the ones that don't.
- **The Wason selection task** from 1960 showed even smart subjects ignore evidence that could disprove their hypothesis and chase evidence that could only confirm it.
- **Real example.** An investor who has decided a stock is a buy will hunt for analyst reports that agree, read earnings calls for positive signals, and dismiss bears as noise. The position grows even as the evidence base degrades.
- **The professional fix.** Charlie Munger's rule: "I never allow myself to hold an opinion on anything that I don't know the other side's argument better than they do." Steel-manning, not strawmanning.

## Availability heuristic: vivid and recent feels more probable

- You estimate how common something is by how easily examples come to mind. Easy to recall feels common. Hard to recall feels rare.
- **Real example.** After a plane crash makes the news, flight bookings dip and car travel goes up. Driving is far more dangerous per mile, but the plane crash is vivid and recent, so System 1 overweights it. More people die in the extra driving than would have died in the planes.
- **Shark attacks.** Americans dramatically overestimate the risk of shark attacks because *Jaws* and the news cycle make them mentally available. The actual rate is about one fatality per year in the US.
- **In strategy.** Executives anchor on the recent failure or the recent win. A competitor's flashy launch dominates the meeting even when the boring competitor is taking more market share quietly.

## Sunk cost fallacy: throwing good money after bad

- A rational decision uses only future costs and future benefits. Past costs are gone. They should not enter the calculation.
- Humans hate to "waste" what they have already spent, so they keep paying to justify earlier payments.
- **Real example.** You buy a $200 concert ticket. You get sick the night of. Going while sick is worse than skipping, but you go anyway "because the ticket cost $200." The $200 is gone either way. The only choice is between a bad night and a normal one.
- **In business.** The Concorde, the supersonic jet, kept getting funded by the British and French governments long after it was clear the economics wouldn't work. "We've already spent so much" was the explicit argument. The bias has a nickname: the Concorde fallacy.
- **In careers.** People stay in jobs and degrees because of years invested, not because of expected future return. Sunk cost is the engine of a lot of quiet misery.

## Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good

- Kahneman and Tversky measured the ratio at roughly 2:1. Losing $100 hurts about twice as much as gaining $100 feels good.
- This is the foundation of **prospect theory**, the work that won Kahneman the 2002 Nobel.
- **Real example.** Investors hold losing stocks too long (selling makes the loss real) and sell winning stocks too fast (locking in the gain feels good). The pattern is so reliable it has a name in finance: the **disposition effect**.
- **In product.** Free trials work because once you have it, taking it away registers as a loss. Money-back guarantees work for the same reason. The endowment effect (you value things more once you own them) is a cousin.
- **In strategy.** Leaders defend losing positions longer than they should because exiting locks in the loss. Markets are full of companies that should have killed a product line three years ago.

## Framing effect: how the choice is presented changes the decision

- Same facts, different frame, different answer.
- **The Asian disease problem,** Tversky and Kahneman 1981. Imagine 600 people will die from a disease unless you choose a program. Program A saves 200. Program B has a 1/3 chance of saving all 600 and 2/3 chance of saving none. Most people pick A.
- Same scenario, reframed. Program A means 400 will die. Program B has a 1/3 chance no one dies and 2/3 chance all 600 die. Most people now pick B.
- Identical math. Opposite choice. The word "save" versus the word "die" flipped the answer.
- **Real example.** "90% lean" beef outsells "10% fat" beef. "Survival rate of 90%" reassures patients more than "mortality rate of 10%." Doctors who frame surgery in survival terms get more patients to consent.

## How professionals try to compensate

- **Premortems.** Before making a big decision, imagine it failed badly and write the story of why. Forces the brain to surface risks confirmation bias is hiding.
- **Decision journals.** Write down the reasoning and the expected outcome at the time of the decision. Review later. Stops you from rewriting history after the fact (hindsight bias).
- **Base rates first.** Before estimating "how likely is this startup to succeed," look up how often startups in this category succeed. Anchor on the base rate, then adjust.
- **Steel-manning.** Argue the other side until you can do it better than the other side can. If you can't, you don't understand the decision.
- **Structured frameworks.** Checklists, scorecards, and rubrics force System 2 to engage instead of letting System 1's gut feel masquerade as analysis.

## What to carry away

- The biases are not failures of intelligence. Smart people exhibit them as much as anyone, sometimes more because confidence is high.
- Knowing the name of a bias doesn't immunize you. The names help in retrospect. In the moment, the bias still wins.
- The best defense is structural: build processes that force the slow system to engage on the decisions that matter.
- In one sentence: your brain runs on shortcuts that were great for survival and are mediocre for spreadsheets, so the work of good judgment is catching the shortcuts before they cost you.
