# Memory: how it actually works (and why yours lies to you)

## What memory is and why it matters

Memory feels like playback. You think back to your tenth birthday and a scene loads up, complete with cake and faces. The truth is closer to this: your brain doesn't store memories like video files. It stores fragments and patterns, then rebuilds the scene each time you ask for it, filling gaps with plausible guesses. This matters because you study, testify, parent, argue, vote, and remember your own life through this faulty machine. Knowing how it actually works changes how you learn, how much you trust your own certainty, and how you treat someone whose memory of a shared event differs from yours.

## The three-stage model

- **Encoding.** Getting information in. Attention is the gate. If you weren't paying attention, it didn't encode, and no amount of straining to remember will bring it back. This is why you can't find your keys when you set them down while reading a text.
- **Storage.** Holding it. Different systems for different timescales.
- **Retrieval.** Pulling it back out. Every retrieval is a reconstruction, and every reconstruction slightly rewrites the memory.

## Working memory: the tiny desk

- **Alan Baddeley** built the modern model of working memory in the 1970s. It's the small mental scratchpad where you hold a phone number long enough to dial it, or keep track of where you are in a sentence.
- The classic capacity number is **George Miller's 7 plus or minus 2**, from a 1956 paper. Later work suggests it's closer to 4 chunks for most adults.
- Working memory is the bottleneck for almost every complex task: doing mental math, following directions, holding a conversation while distracted.
- Chunking expands it. **GMV5RBJ** is 7 items if you don't know the pattern, 1 chunk if it's a license plate you recognize.

## Long-term memory: declarative versus procedural

- **Declarative memory** is the stuff you can put into words.
  - **Episodic** memory is your personal timeline: your wedding, the trip to Goa, what you ate yesterday.
  - **Semantic** memory is general knowledge: Paris is the capital of France, water boils at 100C.
- **Procedural memory** is how-to. Riding a bike, typing without looking, the muscle sequence of a tennis serve. You can't really explain it; you just do it.
- These run on different brain systems, which is why someone can lose one and keep the other.

## The hippocampus and patient HM

- Almost everything we know about how memory forms came from one man. **Henry Molaison**, known for decades as HM, had severe epilepsy. In 1953 a surgeon removed most of his hippocampus on both sides to stop the seizures.
- The seizures stopped. So did his ability to form new long-term declarative memories.
- HM lived until 2008, meeting the same researchers every week for fifty years and greeting them as strangers each time.
- **Brenda Milner**, working with HM starting in 1957, made the breakthrough discovery: HM could still learn new motor skills (procedural memory intact) even though he had no memory of the practice sessions. This proved memory wasn't one system. It was many.

## Sleep and consolidation

- New memories are fragile when first formed. Sleep is when the brain replays the day and moves important patterns from short-term storage into long-term storage. This is called **consolidation**.
- REM sleep matters for emotional and procedural memory. Slow-wave sleep matters for declarative memory.
- Practical implication: pulling an all-nighter before an exam is one of the worst things you can do. You're skipping the step that actually files the information.

## The forgetting curve

- **Hermann Ebbinghaus**, a German psychologist, did the original experiments on himself in **1885**. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at intervals.
- He found that forgetting is steep at first, then flattens. You lose most of what you learned within 24 hours. What survives 24 hours has a much better chance of sticking.
- The fix is **spaced repetition**: review just before you would have forgotten, and the curve flattens further each time.

## Memory is reconstruction, not recording

This is the part that should make you a little uneasy.

- **Elizabeth Loftus** spent fifty years showing that human memory is shockingly easy to alter. Her most famous demonstration is the **lost-in-the-mall study** (1995). She and her team convinced about 25% of adult participants that they had been lost in a shopping mall as children, with vivid recalled detail. It never happened.
- Her earlier work on eyewitness testimony showed that the words used to ask a question changed what people "remembered." Witnesses asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" gave higher speeds and were more likely to falsely report broken glass than witnesses asked when the cars "hit."
- This has had a huge effect on the legal system. Eyewitness misidentification is the leading cause of wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence (about 70% according to the Innocence Project).
- Every time you remember something, you're partly making it up from cues. Confidence in a memory is not evidence of accuracy.

## Techniques that actually help

- **The memory palace** (method of loci). Greek and Roman orators used it 2,000 years ago. You place items mentally along a familiar route through a building. Worlds memory champions still use it.
- **Spaced repetition** with software. **Anki**, built by Damien Elmes in 2006, schedules cards for review at expanding intervals tuned to your forgetting curve. Medical students, language learners, and PM interview preppers swear by it.
- **Retrieval practice.** Testing yourself is stronger than re-reading. Re-reading feels productive but produces shallow encoding. Closing the book and trying to write what you remember produces deep encoding.
- **Elaboration.** Connect new information to things you already know. Isolated facts don't stick. Networked facts do.
- **Sleep.** Cannot be hacked.

## The modern wrinkles

- **The Google effect** (Betsy Sparrow, 2011): when we know information is easily searchable, we remember less of the information itself and more about where to find it. The implications for memory in the smartphone era are still being worked out.
- **Aging.** Episodic memory weakens first; semantic memory holds up much longer. The grandmother who repeats stories often still knows everything she knew at 30, she just can't lay down new episodes well.
- **Alzheimer's disease** attacks the hippocampus early, which is why recent memories go first while distant ones survive for years. About 1 in 9 people over 65 in the US have it.

## What to carry away

- Memory is encoding, storage, retrieval, and every retrieval is a small rewrite.
- Working memory is tiny, long-term memory is huge, and they're different systems.
- The hippocampus files things; sleep is when it files them; spaced review is what keeps them filed.
- Confidence in a memory does not mean the memory is correct.
- The best learning tools (spaced repetition, retrieval practice, sleep) are unsexy and well-established. Most study time is wasted on re-reading, which feels good and does little.
