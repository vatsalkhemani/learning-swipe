# Heuristics and decision-making under uncertainty: why experts are often wrong

## What heuristics are and why they matter

A heuristic is a mental shortcut. Not the optimal answer. A good-enough answer fast. The world is full of decisions where you don't have time, data, or computing power to optimize, so the brain uses rules of thumb that get you most of the way there. The interesting question is when heuristics work well and when they fail. The research on this question has produced one of the most humbling findings in social science: experts in most fields are barely better than informed amateurs at predicting outcomes in their own field, and often worse than simple statistical models. The implication isn't that expertise is worthless. It is that uncertainty has structure, and most people, including experts, misread it.

## Bounded rationality: the idea that started the field

- **Herbert Simon**, an economist and cognitive scientist at Carnegie Mellon, introduced **bounded rationality** in the 1950s.
- The classical economic agent was assumed to be a perfect optimizer with unlimited information and unlimited computing power. Simon pointed out this person doesn't exist.
- Real humans, he argued, **satisfice** instead of optimize. They search until they find an option that meets a threshold, then stop. Picking a parking spot, choosing a restaurant, accepting a job offer. All satisficing, not optimizing.
- Simon won the **1978 Nobel in Economics** for this work. He also predicted, correctly, that AI would eventually need to satisfice the same way humans do because optimization in the real world is computationally impossible.

## Gigerenzer's defense: fast and frugal can beat optimization

- **Gerd Gigerenzer**, a German psychologist at the Max Planck Institute, has spent his career arguing the heuristics-and-biases tradition is too gloomy about human judgment.
- His core finding: in environments with high uncertainty and limited data, simple heuristics often outperform complex models.
- **The recognition heuristic.** When asked which of two cities is larger, German students did better picking US cities they had heard of (and ignoring ones they hadn't) than American students did picking American cities they knew details about. Less information, better answer.
- **Take the best.** Find the single most predictive cue, decide based on that, ignore the rest. In many domains this beats regression models that try to weight every variable.
- Gigerenzer's argument isn't that heuristics are always right. It is that the right tool depends on the environment. In a noisy, low-data world, simple beats complex.

## Tetlock and the expert prediction problem

- **Philip Tetlock**, a political scientist at Penn, ran the most ambitious study of expert prediction ever attempted.
- Starting in the 1980s, he asked hundreds of political experts (academics, journalists, government analysts) to make specific, dated predictions about world events. He tracked them for 20 years.
- The results, published in *Expert Political Judgment* (2005), were brutal. Experts performed barely better than chance on long-range political predictions. The famous line: they did worse than a "dart-throwing chimp."
- **The bigger the expert's media profile, the worse the predictions.** TV pundits and confident generalists underperformed cautious, less-famous analysts.
- Tetlock then ran the **Good Judgment Project** in the early 2010s, a forecasting tournament funded by the US intelligence community. He found a small group, the **superforecasters**, who consistently beat both pundits and intelligence analysts with classified information.
- *Superforecasting* (2015), co-written with Dan Gardner, distilled what these people did differently. Not smarter on average. They updated beliefs incrementally, broke big questions into smaller ones, used base rates, and held probabilistic views instead of confident yes/no calls.

## Base rates: the most useful tool almost no one uses

- The single biggest improvement to most predictions is starting with the base rate, the frequency of the outcome in similar past cases.
- **Real example.** A friend pitches you a startup. Before you evaluate the idea, ask: what fraction of startups in this category have succeeded? About 10%. Anchor there. Now adjust based on what is unusual about this case.
- People skip this step constantly. They look at the specifics ("but this team is great, the market is big") and forget that the base rate is doing most of the work.
- **Doctors miss this too.** A patient with mild symptoms in a low-prevalence population is overwhelmingly likely to not have the disease, even after a positive test. Doctors who haven't been trained in Bayesian reasoning often miss this and order aggressive workups that do more harm than good.

## Why even experts in their own field are unreliable

- **Doctors disagree with each other** more than people expect. Studies have shown radiologists reviewing the same scan reach different conclusions a meaningful fraction of the time. Same radiologist, same scan, different day, also different conclusions.
- **Judges sentencing the same case** vary wildly depending on time of day, hunger, and whether their favorite sports team won the night before. Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein call this **noise** (random variation) as distinct from bias (systematic error) in their 2021 book *Noise*.
- **Financial analysts** as a group have predicted recessions about as well as flipping a coin. The forecasts cluster around the recent past, which is the wrong place to anchor.
- The pattern: in any field with high uncertainty and feedback loops longer than weeks, expert intuition is unreliable. Fields with tight, fast feedback (chess, firefighting, anesthesia) develop reliable expert intuition because the world corrects the experts in real time. Fields without it don't.

## The case for checklists

- **Atul Gawande**, surgeon and writer, made the most-read argument in *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009). Surgical checklists reduced complications and deaths in trials across eight hospitals from Toronto to Tanzania.
- The checklist isn't there to teach the surgeon medicine. It is there to prevent the rare, foreseeable mistake that high-pressure expertise reliably skips.
- Pilots have used checklists since the 1935 Boeing Model 299 crash, when test pilot Ployer Hill died because the crew forgot to release a gust lock. Boeing's response was the modern preflight checklist, and aviation has been getting safer ever since.
- The lesson: expertise is necessary but not sufficient. Pair it with structure and you get reliability. Skip the structure and you get talented people making the same avoidable mistakes for decades.

## What good decision-making under uncertainty actually looks like

- **Think in probabilities, not certainties.** "70% chance" beats "definitely will happen." If you are forced to commit to yes or no, build in the uncertainty by stacking multiple decisions and tracking your hit rate.
- **Update incrementally.** Big swings of opinion based on one new data point are usually wrong. The superforecasters Tetlock studied moved their estimates by small amounts in response to small evidence.
- **Separate the decision from the outcome.** A good decision with a bad outcome is still a good decision. The world is noisy. Judge process, not just result. Poker players call this **resulting**, the mistake of evaluating decisions by their outcome.
- **Premortems.** Imagine the project failed. Write the story of why. You will surface risks your optimism is hiding.
- **Track your predictions.** If you don't write down what you predicted and check later, you will remember being right more often than you were. Hindsight rewrites memory aggressively.

## What to carry away

- Heuristics aren't broken. They are well-adapted to some environments and badly mismatched to others. The skill is knowing which is which.
- Most expert prediction in noisy fields is overconfident. Including yours. Including mine.
- The best forecasters and decision-makers aren't the smartest. They are the most calibrated, the most willing to update, and the most honest about what they don't know.
- In one sentence: certainty is cheap, calibration is expensive, and most of the work of better decisions is learning to live inside the discomfort of not knowing.
