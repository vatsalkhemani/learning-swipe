# The hedonic treadmill: why getting what you want doesn't make you happy

## What it is and why it matters

The hedonic treadmill is the observation that humans adapt to their circumstances with surprising speed. Good things stop feeling as good. Bad things stop feeling as bad. People return toward a personal baseline of happiness no matter what happens to them. The phrase was coined by **Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell in 1971**. It matters because most of life planning is built on the unstated assumption that the next promotion, the next house, the next relationship will make you durably happier than you are now. The research says it largely won't. Knowing this changes how you set goals, what you spend on, and where you put effort.

## The famous study

- **Philip Brickman, Dan Coates, and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman** published a paper in 1978 titled *Lottery Winners and Accident Victims: Is Happiness Relative?*
- They interviewed three groups: 22 recent major lottery winners, 29 paralyzed accident victims, and a control group.
- Lottery winners rated themselves slightly less happy on day-to-day pleasures (cereal, jokes, conversation) than controls. They were no more satisfied with their lives overall.
- Accident victims rated themselves less happy than controls but not by as much as common sense predicted, and their projected future happiness was within striking distance of the controls.
- The interpretation: within months to a year, people had adapted. The lottery had reset the bar of what felt special. The paralysis had been integrated into a new normal.
- Caveat the popular retelling often skips: the sample sizes were small, and later research has found accident victims often experience longer-lasting drops than the 1978 paper suggested. But the core finding (adaptation is fast and surprisingly complete for many circumstances) has held up.

## Set-point theory

- The idea that each person has a genetically influenced happiness baseline that they return to.
- **David Lykken and Auke Tellegen's 1996 twin study** estimated genetic contribution to long-term happiness at around **50%**. Identical twins raised apart had remarkably similar happiness levels.
- **Sonja Lyubomirsky** at UC Riverside refined this into her well-known pie chart in *The How of Happiness* (2007): about **50% genetic, 10% circumstances, 40% intentional activity**. The pie's exact percentages have been criticized (Brown and Rohrer have argued the model overstates the precision), but the qualitative shape (genes big, circumstances small, intentional behavior surprisingly large) has stuck.
- The implication: changing your circumstances has a smaller effect on happiness than you'd expect. Changing what you do every day has a larger effect than you'd expect.

## The money question

- **Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton** published an enormous 2010 study using Gallup data from 450,000 Americans. Their finding: day-to-day emotional well-being rose with income up to about **$75,000** and then plateaued. Life satisfaction (a different measure, more about evaluation than feeling) kept rising with income.
- The $75K figure became famous and was widely misread as "money stops helping above $75K."
- **Matthew Killingsworth** at Wharton ran a much larger follow-up study in 2021 with a smartphone-based experience sampling method. He found happiness kept rising with income past $75K, no plateau.
- **Kahneman and Killingsworth collaborated in 2023** on a paper that resolved the conflict. The plateau exists, but only for the unhappiest 15-20% of people. For everyone else, more money keeps helping at a slow, steady rate. The doubling-of-income effect on happiness is roughly constant across the income range.
- Practical reading: money helps. Not as much as you think, especially above middle-class comfort, and especially if you're already happy. Beyond what covers basics and removes fear, the curve flattens fast.

## What doesn't move the needle long-term

- **Stuff.** A new car, a new phone, a bigger TV. The adaptation timeline is weeks.
- **Status.** Promotions and titles. Adapt within months.
- **Houses.** People predict moving to a bigger house will change their baseline. It doesn't, much.
- **Climate and city.** Moving to California from the Midwest does not durably raise happiness, despite what people predict (Schkade and Kahneman, 1998, "Does Living in California Make People Happy?").
- **Cosmetic changes** to your body, partner, or job.

## What does move the needle

- **Relationships, maintained over time.** The single most consistent finding in happiness research. The **Harvard Study of Adult Development**, running since 1938 (the longest study of its kind), keeps producing the same result: relationship quality at 50 predicts physical and mental health at 80 better than cholesterol does.
- **Gratitude practice.** **Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough's 2003 studies** had subjects keep weekly gratitude journals. Significant well-being effects, sustained over weeks. Cheap intervention, strong evidence.
- **Meaning and purpose.** Viktor Frankl's central insight. Having something larger than yourself to work toward correlates with well-being even controlling for circumstances.
- **Exercise.** Among the most reliable mood interventions known. Meta-analyses put the effect comparable to antidepressants for mild-to-moderate depression.
- **Giving.** Spending money on others reliably produces more happiness than spending on yourself (Elizabeth Dunn's work). The amount matters less than the act.
- **Savoring.** Slowing down to fully experience good moments. Sounds soft; the data is strong.

## The dark side of adaptation

- Adaptation also dulls suffering, which is mostly a gift. Most of what people fear ahead of time would land softer than they expect.
- But it also dulls the things you wanted to keep enjoying. The promotion, the marriage, the move. None of them stay as bright as they were.
- And it makes consumer culture's basic offer (buy this and you'll feel different) almost mathematically false. The treadmill is the engine of the upgrade cycle.

## Implications for how to plan a life

- **Don't anchor major decisions to the projected emotional impact of an outcome.** You'll adapt either way. Choose for reasons that aren't "this will make me happy."
- **Optimize for things that don't adapt.** Relationships you invest in. Skills that compound. Experiences (which tend to keep paying back through memory in a way possessions don't, per Thomas Gilovich's research).
- **Treat circumstance-changes as small inputs, behavior-changes as large inputs.** The 40% bucket in Lyubomirsky's pie is where the leverage is.
- **Expect that the next big thing won't change as much as you think.** Then go for it anyway, for reasons that aren't about your baseline. This is the mature version.
- **Watch for the inverse error too.** Adversity will also feel less bad in 6 months than it feels today.

## What to carry away

- Humans adapt fast. Good and bad both fade toward baseline.
- Genes set a strong floor and ceiling; circumstances do less than predicted; intentional behavior does more.
- Money helps until it doesn't, and the cutoff is higher than you might think, but the curve flattens.
- Stuff, status, and houses don't move the long-term needle. Relationships, gratitude, exercise, meaning, and giving do.
- The treadmill cuts both ways. Whatever you're chasing or fearing right now will feel smaller in a year than it feels today.
