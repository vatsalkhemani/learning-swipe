# How modern money actually works: central banks, fractional reserve, and fiat

## What modern money is and why it matters

Modern money is fiat currency, meaning it has value because a government says it does and people accept it. There's no gold in a vault matching the dollars in your bank account. The system runs on three things working together: a central bank that controls the supply, commercial banks that create most of the actual money by lending, and a public that trusts the whole arrangement enough to use it. This is a recent invention. As recently as 1971 the US dollar was technically tied to gold. The current system is younger than most people's parents. Understanding it matters because almost every economic argument you hear (inflation, interest rates, government debt, crypto) is really an argument about how this machine should work.

## How we got here: the gold standard and its bank runs

- Before central banks, most major economies used the **gold standard**. A pound or a dollar was a claim on a fixed amount of gold sitting somewhere.
- It sounded stable. It wasn't. The 1800s and early 1900s in the US were punctuated by **bank runs and panics** every decade or so: 1819, 1837, 1857, 1873, 1893, 1907. Banks would lend out more notes than they had gold for, depositors would panic, the bank would fail, and the panic would spread.
- The **Panic of 1907** was the breaking point. The stock market crashed, several New York trust companies collapsed, and there was no Federal Reserve to lend emergency cash. **J.P. Morgan**, then 70 years old, personally locked a group of bank presidents in his library and refused to let them leave until they agreed to bail each other out with their own money.
- Congress watched a private banker save the entire US financial system and decided that was unacceptable. After six years of secret meetings (including a famous one on **Jekyll Island** in 1910) and political fights, the **Federal Reserve Act** passed in December 1913.
- The Fed wasn't designed to abolish gold. It was designed to be a lender of last resort, so the next panic wouldn't need a J.P. Morgan.

## Fractional reserve banking, the part no one explains

- Most people think banks hold your deposits in a vault. They don't.
- A bank takes your $1,000 deposit, keeps a small fraction (historically 10%, often much less today), and lends the rest. The borrower deposits the loan somewhere, that bank keeps a fraction and lends the rest. Each pass creates new money.
- This is called **fractional reserve banking**, and it's how something like 90% of the money in any modern economy gets created. The central bank prints a relatively small amount. Commercial banks create the rest through lending.
- The Bank of England explicitly confirmed this in a 2014 paper called *Money Creation in the Modern Economy*, which surprised a lot of people who thought it was a fringe view.
- The risk is obvious: if every depositor wants their money at once, the bank doesn't have it. That's a bank run. The Fed and **FDIC insurance** (1933, post-Depression) exist to make sure you never need to actually try.

## Fiat, and the Nixon shock

- After WWII, the **Bretton Woods system** (1944) made the US dollar the world's reserve currency, with the dollar pegged to gold at $35 an ounce. Other currencies pegged to the dollar.
- It worked until it didn't. By the late 1960s the US was printing too many dollars (Vietnam War, Great Society spending) and foreign governments started asking to redeem them for gold. The US didn't have enough.
- On **August 15, 1971**, **Richard Nixon** went on TV and announced the US would no longer convert dollars to gold. He called it temporary. It was permanent. This is the **Nixon shock**.
- Every major currency today is fiat. The dollar is backed by the US government's ability to tax and the world's willingness to use it. That's it.

## What central banks actually do

- **Set short-term interest rates.** When the Fed lowers rates, borrowing gets cheaper, more money gets created through lending, and the economy speeds up. When it raises rates, the opposite. This is the main lever.
- **Open market operations.** The Fed buys and sells government bonds to inject or remove cash from the banking system. Buying bonds creates money.
- **Quantitative easing (QE).** After 2008, rates were already at zero and the economy was still in trouble. The Fed started buying massive amounts of bonds and mortgage-backed securities, expanding its balance sheet from under $1 trillion to over $4 trillion by 2014. **Ben Bernanke** ran this experiment. It happened again at much larger scale in 2020 during COVID.
- **Lender of last resort.** The original 1913 job. Lend emergency cash to solvent banks in a panic so they don't collapse.

## The argument about all of this

- **Austrian economists** (Mises, Hayek, modern voices like Peter Schiff) hate the whole setup. Fiat lets governments inflate away debts, central banks distort interest rates, and the system is structurally fragile. They want a return to gold or a hard-money rule.
- **Mainstream economists** say it works pretty well. The post-WWII era of fiat central banking has had recessions but no Great Depression repeat. The Fed's job is hard and it makes mistakes, but the system is better than gold.
- **Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)**, associated with **Stephanie Kelton**, argues that countries that issue their own currency can't really go bankrupt and should spend more freely on public goods. Inflation, not debt, is the real constraint. Mainstream economists mostly think MMT understates how hard inflation is to control. The 2021-2022 inflation spike gave them ammunition.
- **Crypto people** (the Bitcoin wing especially) treat the whole fiat system as a slow-motion failure waiting to happen and built an alternative. More on that in another card.

## What you actually need to know

- **Inflation** is when too much money chases too few goods, and prices rise. The Fed targets 2% per year as healthy. Above that and things get uncomfortable. The 2022 peak of 9.1% was the worst since 1981.
- **Interest rates** are the price of money. When they go up, your mortgage and credit card cost more, but your savings earn more. When they go down, the reverse. This is the single most powerful tool central banks have.
- **Exchange rates** float against each other based on trade flows, interest rate differentials, and confidence in each country's economy. A strong dollar makes imports cheap and exports expensive. A weak dollar does the opposite.
- The system is more fragile than it looks (2008, 2023's regional bank failures) and more durable than its critics claim (it's survived 50+ years of fiat without a global collapse).
- In one sentence: modern money is a confidence game backed by law, where central banks manage the supply and commercial banks create most of it, and the whole thing works as long as everyone keeps believing it does.
