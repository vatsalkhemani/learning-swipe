# Bitcoin and crypto in plain English: what it actually is and what it isn't

## What Bitcoin is and why it matters

Bitcoin is digital money that no government controls and no company runs. It exists as entries on a public ledger that thousands of computers around the world keep in sync. The premise, when an anonymous person calling themselves **Satoshi Nakamoto** published a nine-page whitepaper in October 2008, was that the financial system had just proven it couldn't be trusted, so here was a way to send money over the internet without trusting anyone. Fifteen years later Bitcoin is worth more than a trillion dollars, BlackRock and Fidelity sell Bitcoin ETFs to American retirees, and most of the rest of crypto has turned out to be either useful infrastructure or expensive nonsense. It matters because regardless of what you think of it, it's the first genuinely new kind of money since the gold standard ended, and the technology behind it has reshaped how a generation thinks about finance.

## How it started

- **October 31, 2008**: Satoshi Nakamoto publishes "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" to a cryptography mailing list. The world was six weeks past Lehman Brothers and still in panic.
- **January 3, 2009**: Satoshi mines the **genesis block**, the first Bitcoin block ever. Embedded in it is a headline from that day's London Times: "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." This was not subtle.
- **May 22, 2010**: a programmer named **Laszlo Hanyecz** pays 10,000 Bitcoin for two Papa John's pizzas. That's the first known commercial transaction. At Bitcoin's 2021 peak those pizzas were worth roughly $700 million. "Bitcoin Pizza Day" is still celebrated annually.
- **April 2011**: Satoshi sends a final email saying he's "moved on to other things" and disappears. His identity has never been confirmed. He still owns roughly 1 million Bitcoin that have never moved.

## Blockchain, in plain English

- A blockchain is a public list of transactions that anyone can read but no one can secretly edit.
- Imagine a shared Google Doc that every participant has a copy of. Every ten minutes, new transactions get bundled into a "block" and added to the chain. Every copy updates.
- The trick is making sure everyone agrees on what's true without a central referee. Bitcoin uses **proof of work**: computers around the world race to solve a hard math puzzle. The winner gets to add the next block and earns new Bitcoin as a reward. This is **mining**.
- Mining costs real electricity. That cost is the security. To rewrite history, an attacker would have to redo all the work for every block since the change, faster than the rest of the network can extend the current chain. It's currently impossible for any single entity.
- **Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million coins**, written into the original code. New coins get released to miners on a schedule that halves every four years (the "halving"). The last Bitcoin will be mined around the year 2140. This deflationary design is the core of the "digital gold" pitch.

## The major chapters

- **2009 to 2013**: a hobbyist phase. Bitcoin trades for cents, then dollars. The Silk Road marketplace makes it briefly famous for drug deals. First real bubble in late 2013 takes it past $1,000.
- **2015**: a 21-year-old Russian-Canadian programmer named **Vitalik Buterin** launches **Ethereum**. Ethereum's innovation is **smart contracts**, which are programs that run on the blockchain. This unlocks a lot more than just money: lending protocols, exchanges, games, NFTs, anything you can code.
- **2017**: the **ICO bubble**. Startups raise billions by issuing their own tokens on Ethereum. Most projects are vaporware. Bitcoin reaches $20,000 in December, then crashes 80%.
- **2020 to 2021**: the institutional bull run. **MicroStrategy** under CEO **Michael Saylor** starts putting its corporate treasury in Bitcoin. **Tesla** briefly does the same. **Coinbase** goes public in April 2021. Bitcoin peaks near $69,000 in November 2021. **NFTs** (digital collectibles) hit mania, with Beeple's "Everydays" selling at Christie's for $69 million.
- **2022**: the collapse. The stablecoin Terra/Luna implodes in May, wiping out $40 billion in days. The crypto hedge fund Three Arrows Capital fails. In November, **FTX**, the world's second-largest crypto exchange, collapses overnight. Founder **Sam Bankman-Fried** had been running a $10 billion fraud, secretly moving customer money to his hedge fund Alameda. He's later sentenced to 25 years in federal prison.
- **2023 to today**: institutionalization. The **SEC approves spot Bitcoin ETFs** in January 2024 after years of refusing. **BlackRock**, the world's largest asset manager, becomes one of the biggest holders. Bitcoin crosses $100,000 in late 2024. The narrative shifts from "internet money" to "digital gold owned by your dentist's IRA."

## What's actually useful and what isn't

- **Bitcoin as a store of value**: this is the narrative that won. A scarce, decentralized, government-resistant asset that looks like gold for the internet age. Whether that's correct is still being argued, but it's now widely held by institutions.
- **Stablecoins** (Tether, USDC, others): tokens pegged to the dollar that move on blockchains. These are genuinely useful, especially in countries with weak currencies. Argentina, Turkey, Nigeria, Vietnam all use them heavily. Tether alone moves more dollars annually than Mastercard.
- **DeFi (decentralized finance)**: protocols like Uniswap and Aave let you swap tokens or take loans without a bank. The tech works. The user base is mostly other crypto people. It hasn't yet replaced normal finance for normal people.
- **NFTs**: collapsed almost entirely after 2022. Most "$300,000 monkey JPEG" purchases lost 95%+ of their value. Some use cases survive in gaming and digital art identity, but the mania is over.
- **Web3 broadly** (decentralized social media, decentralized cloud storage, decentralized everything): mostly hasn't taken off. Users prefer the centralized versions because they're faster, cheaper, and easier.

## El Salvador, the one country that tried

- In June 2021, El Salvador's president **Nayib Bukele** made Bitcoin **legal tender** alongside the US dollar. First country in the world to do so. The government started buying Bitcoin with treasury funds.
- The IMF was furious. Most economists called it reckless.
- The result was uneven. The "Chivo" wallet rollout was buggy, and most Salvadorans never really used Bitcoin for daily payments. But the country's Bitcoin holdings are now worth significantly more than what they paid, the tourism narrative worked, and Bukele is wildly popular at home.
- The lesson is mixed: Bitcoin as legal tender for everyday transactions didn't really work. Bitcoin as a national reserve asset has, so far, paid off.

## Where this probably goes

- **Bitcoin** is likely now a permanent financial asset, the way gold is. Holders include sovereign wealth funds, pension funds, and corporate treasuries. The argument has shifted from "will it exist in five years" to "what's the right allocation."
- **Ethereum and smart contract platforms** keep building. Real adoption is slow. The infrastructure is real even if the consumer use case is still niche.
- **Stablecoins** are the breakout product. Expect them to become a normal piece of how dollars move globally, increasingly regulated and increasingly mainstream.
- **The Web3 vision** of replacing the internet with decentralized versions of everything has mostly failed. What survived is what was genuinely better as decentralized, which turns out to be less than the maximalists hoped.

## What to carry away

- Bitcoin solved a real problem (digital scarcity without a central party) using a clever combination of cryptography and economic incentives. That alone is a genuine intellectual achievement.
- Most of the rest of crypto is a mix of useful infrastructure, ambitious experiments, and outright fraud, in proportions that vary by cycle.
- The "crypto is mostly scams" critics were right about most coins. The "Bitcoin is going to zero" critics were wrong about Bitcoin.
- In one sentence: Bitcoin is a fifteen-year-old experiment in money without governments that has stabilized into a real financial asset, while most of the crypto built around it is either useful plumbing or expensive lessons in what doesn't work.
