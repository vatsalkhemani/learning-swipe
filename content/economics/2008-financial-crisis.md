# The 2008 financial crisis: what actually happened and why it still matters

## What 2008 was and why it still matters

The 2008 financial crisis was the worst economic collapse since the Great Depression. It started in the US housing market, spread through Wall Street's debt machine, and within months had frozen the global financial system. Millions of Americans lost their homes, the world economy lost roughly $10 trillion in output, and a generation's trust in elites, banks, and government broke. The crisis still matters because it explains a lot of what came after: the rise of populism on both the left (Occupy, Sanders) and right (Tea Party, Trump), the popularity of Bitcoin, the deep distrust of expertise, and the regulatory fights that are still being fought today. If you want to understand the political mood of the 2010s and 2020s, you start here.

## The setup, 1999 to 2006

- **Glass-Steagall**, the 1933 law separating boring commercial banks (deposits, loans) from risky investment banks (trading, securities), was effectively repealed in **1999** under President **Bill Clinton** with the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. Banks could now do everything under one roof.
- After the **dot-com crash in 2000** and the September 11 attacks, the Fed under **Alan Greenspan** cut interest rates aggressively. By 2003 the federal funds rate was at 1%, the lowest in 45 years.
- Cheap money flooded the housing market. Home prices started rising. Lending standards started falling.
- **Subprime mortgages** (loans to people with bad credit) exploded from a niche product to a major business. By 2006, subprime was roughly 20% of all US mortgage originations.
- **NINJA loans** ("No Income, No Job, No Assets") became a real thing. So did stated-income loans, where the borrower just wrote down a number. Predatory lenders like **Countrywide Financial** specifically targeted low-income and minority neighborhoods with loans designed to reset to unaffordable rates.
- Everyone in the chain was paid upfront: the mortgage broker, the bank that bought the loan, the Wall Street firm that packaged it. No one had skin in the game past closing.

## The Wall Street machine

- Banks bundled thousands of mortgages together into **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)**. The idea was that even if some loans defaulted, the diversified pool was safe.
- Then they sliced the MBS into **collateralized debt obligations (CDOs)**, with different "tranches" carrying different levels of risk. The top tranche got paid first if anything went wrong.
- The **credit rating agencies** (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) stamped these top tranches **AAA**, the same rating as US Treasury bonds. They did this because Wall Street paid their fees and they competed for the business.
- Pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and European banks bought these AAA bonds by the trillions. They thought they were buying super-safe assets.
- They were actually buying repackaged subprime mortgages. **Michael Lewis** later called this the greatest mass financial fraud in American history. **Michael Burry**, **Steve Eisman**, and a handful of others spotted it and bet against it. Most of them were treated as cranks until they were rich.

## The AIG problem

- A separate piece of the machine was **credit default swaps (CDS)**, essentially insurance contracts on bonds. If the bond defaulted, the insurer paid out.
- **AIG**, an insurance giant with an ordinary insurance business, set up a small London office that sold hundreds of billions of dollars of CDS on subprime CDOs.
- AIG didn't hold reserves against this exposure because CDS were technically not insurance, just contracts. They were unregulated.
- When the bonds started defaulting, AIG owed payouts it could never make. The company was the size of a country and it was about to fail.

## 2007: the cracks

- **February 2007**: subprime lender **New Century Financial** starts collapsing.
- **June 2007**: two **Bear Stearns** hedge funds heavily exposed to subprime CDOs blow up. The first big Wall Street casualty.
- **August 2007**: French bank **BNP Paribas** halts withdrawals from three funds, saying they can't value the subprime assets. The interbank lending market freezes.
- **January 2008**: Countrywide is bought by Bank of America in a fire sale.

## September 2008: the crash

- **September 7, 2008**: the US government takes over **Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac**, the two giant mortgage agencies, to prevent their collapse.
- **September 15, 2008**: **Lehman Brothers**, a 158-year-old investment bank, files for bankruptcy after the Treasury refuses to bail it out. It's the largest bankruptcy in US history. **Hank Paulson** (Treasury Secretary), **Ben Bernanke** (Fed Chair), and **Tim Geithner** (NY Fed) made the call. They later said they had no legal authority to save it. Critics say they could have found one.
- **September 16, 2008**: the Fed bails out **AIG** with an $85 billion loan, eventually $182 billion. Letting AIG fail would have taken the global financial system down with it.
- **September 16, 2008**: a money market fund (the Reserve Primary Fund) "breaks the buck," meaning it falls below $1 per share. This had basically never happened. Panic spreads to every corner of finance.
- **October 3, 2008**: Congress passes **TARP** (Troubled Asset Relief Program), authorizing $700 billion to bail out banks. **George W. Bush** signs it.

## What happened after

- The recession that followed was the worst since 1929. US unemployment peaked at 10% in October 2009. Roughly 9 million Americans lost their homes. Greece, Ireland, Spain, and Italy were dragged into a sovereign debt crisis that almost broke the Euro.
- **Dodd-Frank** (2010) created new banking regulations, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (championed by **Elizabeth Warren**), and required big banks to pass annual **stress tests**.
- **No senior Wall Street executive went to jail**. The only criminal conviction tied to the crisis was a midlevel banker named Kareem Serageldin from Credit Suisse. This fact has shaped American politics ever since.
- The Fed's emergency programs and TARP technically made a profit when the assets were sold back. Politically, that didn't matter. The story people remember is: Main Street got foreclosed on, Wall Street got bailed out.

## Why it still matters

- **Populist politics on both sides.** The Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street both started in 2009-2011 as direct reactions. Trump in 2016 and Sanders in 2016 and 2020 both ran against the same elite-bailout narrative.
- **Distrust of institutions.** Polling on trust in banks, government, and media collapsed and never fully recovered.
- **The Bitcoin origin story.** The genesis block of Bitcoin in January 2009 contains the headline "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." Satoshi was responding to exactly this.
- **The current regulation fight.** Every time someone proposes loosening Dodd-Frank, the 2008 ghost shows up. The 2023 collapse of **Silicon Valley Bank** and Signature Bank reminded everyone that the system is still fragile when stressed.

## What to carry away

- The crisis wasn't an act of God. It was the predictable outcome of bad lending, fake AAA ratings, unregulated insurance contracts, and incentives where everyone got paid upfront and no one held the risk.
- The bailouts probably prevented a second Great Depression. They also taught a generation that the rules don't apply to everyone equally.
- In one sentence: 2008 is when Wall Street's machine for turning bad loans into AAA paper broke, the world economy almost stopped, and the political settlement that followed (bailouts up top, foreclosures below) defined the next 15 years.
