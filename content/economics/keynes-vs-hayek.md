# Keynes vs Hayek: the argument economics has never finished

## What the argument is and why it still matters

The argument between **John Maynard Keynes** and **Friedrich Hayek** is the longest-running disagreement in modern economics, and it's never actually been settled. Keynes said that markets can break, governments have a job to fix them, and demand management is sometimes the only way out of a slump. Hayek said that markets are how millions of people coordinate without anyone in charge, prices carry information no central planner can match, and government intervention tends to make things worse and freedom smaller. Their fight started in the 1930s, defined the postwar period, was abandoned and revived multiple times, and is still the spine of almost every economic policy argument you'll hear. When people argue about COVID stimulus, government debt, industrial policy, or whether the Fed should have raised rates faster, they're almost always arguing about some version of Keynes vs Hayek. It matters because understanding the two sides clearly is how you can actually follow the debate instead of just picking a team.

## Who they were

- **John Maynard Keynes (1883-1946)** was the establishment Englishman. Cambridge-educated, son of a Cambridge economist, member of the Bloomsbury Group, advisor to the British Treasury through two world wars. He bet on currencies for his own account, made and lost fortunes, married a Russian ballerina, and ran King's College's endowment so well that it outperformed almost every comparable institution. He was the consummate insider.
- **Friedrich Hayek (1899-1992)** was the outsider. Born in Vienna, served in WWI as a teenager, studied under **Ludwig von Mises** in the **Austrian School** of economics. Moved to the London School of Economics in 1931 specifically because the LSE wanted someone to argue against Keynes. Later moved to Chicago, then back to Germany. He spent most of his career in exile from the economic mainstream.
- They knew each other personally. During WWII, when Cambridge was evacuated, Hayek and Keynes did fire-watching duty together on the roof of King's College Chapel. They reportedly got along fine in person while completely disagreeing about everything in print.

## The 1930s, when the fight started

- The Great Depression was the defining event. Unemployment in the US hit 25% in 1933. Output collapsed. Classical economic theory said this couldn't really happen in equilibrium, and yet here it was.
- **Keynes published "The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money" in 1936**. The argument: economies can get stuck in bad equilibria where there's plenty of capacity, plenty of workers, but not enough demand to put them together. If consumers and businesses are too scared to spend, the government has to spend instead, even if it means borrowing. Cut taxes, build infrastructure, hire workers, get the economy moving again. His most famous line, on critics who said markets would eventually self-correct: **"In the long run we are all dead."**
- **Hayek argued the opposite**. His Austrian view was that the Depression was the painful but necessary correction after the artificial 1920s boom, which had been caused by easy credit and bad investments. Keynesian stimulus would just delay the correction and create more distortions. His 1931 lectures at the LSE and his book *Prices and Production* (1931) tried to make the case. He mostly lost the argument in the 30s. The world wanted action and Keynes was offering action.
- The deeper Hayek argument, his most famous one, came in 1944 with **"The Road to Serfdom"**. Written during WWII as a warning to British socialists, it argued that even well-intentioned central planning destroys the price system, which is how a society coordinates without coercion. Once you start planning, you can never plan enough, and the logic pushes toward authoritarianism. The book was a surprise bestseller in the US, especially after Reader's Digest published a condensed version.

## The postwar Keynesian era

- After WWII, Keynes won. Almost completely.
- **Bretton Woods (1944)**, which Keynes helped design, created a managed global monetary system with the IMF and World Bank.
- Most Western governments adopted some version of Keynesian demand management. The UK built the NHS and a welfare state. The US built the interstate highway system and ran consistent budget deficits during recessions.
- The 1950s and 60s were a long boom. Growth was strong, unemployment was low, inflation was modest. Mainstream economists declared the business cycle solved. **Paul Samuelson's** textbook, the bestselling economics textbook of the era, taught Keynesianism as the default.
- Hayek spent these years as a fringe figure. He moved to Chicago in 1950, where he was treated as an interesting eccentric. He wrote about political philosophy more than economics. The economics mainstream considered Austrian economics a curiosity.

## The 1970s broke Keynesianism

- The model that worked in the 50s and 60s assumed a stable relationship between inflation and unemployment (the **Phillips curve**): more inflation, less unemployment.
- In the 1970s, the world got both at the same time. **Stagflation**. The 1973 oil shock made it worse but didn't cause it. Inflation in the US hit 13.5% in 1980 while unemployment was over 7%.
- Keynesian policy had no good answer. Stimulating demand made inflation worse. Tightening to fight inflation made unemployment worse.
- **Milton Friedman** and the **Chicago School** had been arguing this would happen for years. Their **monetarism** said inflation was always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon, and the only sustainable cure was tight money even if it caused a recession.
- **Hayek won the Nobel Prize in 1974**, sharing it with the Swedish socialist Gunnar Myrdal in what looked like a deliberate jab at the consensus. **Friedman won in 1976**.
- **Paul Volcker**, as Fed Chair from 1979, used Friedman-style tight money to crush inflation, deliberately triggering the 1981-82 recession. Unemployment hit 10.8%. Inflation came down. The Keynesian consensus broke.
- **Margaret Thatcher** (elected 1979) and **Ronald Reagan** (elected 1980) ran on Hayek and Friedman ideas: deregulate, cut taxes, shrink government, free markets work. Thatcher reportedly carried a copy of Hayek's *The Constitution of Liberty* into Cabinet meetings, slammed it on the table and said, "This is what we believe."

## 2008 brought Keynes back

- The 2008 financial crisis looked exactly like the kind of demand collapse Keynes had described.
- Governments around the world responded with massive stimulus. **The US passed an $830 billion stimulus in 2009** under Obama. China spent an even larger amount as a share of GDP. The Fed cut rates to zero and started **quantitative easing**, an aggressive Keynesian move.
- The result was that 2008 didn't become a second Great Depression. Recovery was slow, especially for workers, but the catastrophic outcome was avoided.
- **Paul Krugman** and other neo-Keynesians became dominant public voices arguing the stimulus should have been bigger. Hayekian voices like **Peter Schiff** argued the bailouts and money printing were creating the next crisis.
- For most of the 2010s, despite all the money creation, inflation stayed stubbornly low. Keynesians took this as proof that the Hayekian inflation warnings were wrong.

## COVID and the inflation question

- **March 2020**: COVID hits. The US passes the CARES Act ($2.2 trillion). Later stimulus bills under Trump and Biden bring the total to roughly $5 trillion. The Fed expands its balance sheet by trillions more.
- This is the largest Keynesian intervention in peacetime history.
- Inflation stayed quiet through 2020 and most of 2021. Then it didn't. **US inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022**, the highest in 40 years.
- The argument that immediately broke out: was the COVID stimulus too Keynesian? **Larry Summers**, no Hayekian, warned in early 2021 that the American Rescue Plan was too big. Most mainstream economists said the inflation was temporary supply-chain disruption. Summers turned out to be more right.
- Hayekians took the inflation as overdue vindication. Keynesians pointed out that the alternative (less stimulus, slower recovery, longer unemployment) would have been worse.
- The Fed under **Jerome Powell** raised rates from near zero to over 5% in 18 months, the most aggressive tightening in 40 years. Inflation came down without (so far) a major recession, which most economists hadn't thought possible.

## Where the argument lives today

- Almost no serious economist is a pure Keynesian or pure Hayekian today. Most are pragmatic mixes, drawing on both depending on the situation.
- But the underlying split is everywhere. When you hear arguments about:
  - **Industrial policy** (CHIPS Act, IRA, EU subsidies): Keynes vs Hayek.
  - **Fed policy** (raise faster, cut sooner): Keynes vs Hayek.
  - **Government debt** ("we can borrow forever" vs "this ends in crisis"): Keynes vs Hayek.
  - **Minimum wage**, **rent control**, **antitrust enforcement**: Keynes vs Hayek.
- **Modern Monetary Theory** (MMT) is essentially an extreme version of the Keynesian view. **Cryptocurrency maximalism** is essentially an extreme version of the Hayekian view.
- Hayek's argument about information in prices got rediscovered by Silicon Valley in the 2000s as part of the case for markets and against central planning, especially during the rise of the internet economy.

## What to carry away

- Keynes was right that markets can get stuck, and government action sometimes prevents catastrophe. The 2008 response and the COVID response, for all their costs, probably prevented two Great Depressions.
- Hayek was right that prices carry information no planner can match, that intervention has costs and unintended consequences, and that government action tends to be sticky once started.
- The honest answer is that both are partially right, and the question is always one of degree and context. Which is why the argument never ends.
- In one sentence: Keynes vs Hayek is the argument over whether markets need active management or active restraint, an argument that started in the 1930s, has changed which side was winning roughly every 30 years, and shows up under different names in basically every economic policy debate of our time.
