# Globalization and supply chains post-pandemic: why nearshoring is everywhere

## What this shift is and why it matters

For roughly 30 years, from the early 1990s to the late 2010s, the world built a single integrated supply chain optimized for cost. Companies made things in whichever country was cheapest, mostly China, and shipped them everywhere else. That era is ending. It isn't being replaced by deglobalization or autarky. It's being replaced by a slower, more political version where governments care about where critical things get made, companies hedge against geopolitical risk, and trade still happens but in patterns that look more like blocs than one big market. The vocabulary that's now everywhere (nearshoring, friend-shoring, China + 1, reshoring) is the language of that shift. It matters because it touches everything from the price of your phone to the wages of factory workers in Vietnam to whether the next war over Taiwan stays cold.

## The era that's ending

- **1995**: the **World Trade Organization** (WTO) is founded, replacing the older GATT system. The rules of global trade get sharper teeth.
- **2001**: **China joins the WTO** under negotiations led by President Clinton. This is the big one. Over the next two decades, China's share of global manufacturing goes from roughly 7% to over 30%.
- The 2000s are the peak of **just-in-time** supply chains, an approach Toyota pioneered decades earlier and the rest of the world adopted. Hold minimal inventory, get parts delivered exactly when you need them, save on warehousing costs. It worked beautifully right up until it didn't.
- **Apple's pivot to Shenzhen** is the iconic story. Tim Cook, then COO, restructured Apple's supply chain around Chinese manufacturing through partners like Foxconn. By 2010 the iPhone was the most successful product in history, built almost entirely in a single Chinese city.
- The peak of pure globalization was probably 2008. Global trade as a share of world GDP hit 61% that year, then plateaued. The 2008 crisis, not COVID, was the first signal that the model had limits.

## The cracks before COVID

- **2016**: Brexit and Trump win in the same year. Both campaigns ran against globalization as it was practiced.
- **2018**: **Trump's tariffs on China** start. First steel and aluminum, then waves of tariffs on Chinese goods. Many of these tariffs survived into the Biden administration and the second Trump term. They didn't reverse.
- **2019**: the US blacklists **Huawei**, cutting it off from American semiconductors and Google's Android services. This is the start of serious **US-China tech decoupling**. The lesson for companies: depending on a single country for critical inputs is now a political risk, not just an economic one.

## When COVID broke the model

- **March 2020**: lockdowns hit globally. **PPE shortages** make it clear that the US, which had outsourced almost all medical mask production, couldn't make a basic protective device at scale. Governments worldwide had the same realization.
- **2020 to 2022**: the **semiconductor shortage** hits the car industry hardest. Automakers had cancelled chip orders early in COVID expecting demand to crater. Demand surged instead. Chip makers (mostly Taiwanese, especially **TSMC**) had reallocated their capacity. Ford, GM, and others left factories idle for months, costing roughly $210 billion in lost auto sales in 2021 alone.
- **2022**: the **baby formula shortage**. A single Abbott factory in Michigan shut down due to contamination, and because the US imports almost no formula, store shelves emptied for months. A perfect illustration of how a hyper-concentrated supply chain looks fine until one node fails.
- **March 23, 2021**: the **Ever Given**, a 1,300-foot container ship, gets wedged sideways in the Suez Canal. It blocks roughly 12% of global trade for six days. Becomes the internet symbol of just-in-time fragility.

## Russia, energy, and food

- **February 24, 2022**: Russia invades Ukraine. Europe had spent two decades getting cheaper gas from Russia via the Nord Stream pipelines, on the theory that economic integration would prevent conflict.
- Within a year, **Germany** had ended that bet. Nord Stream was sabotaged in September 2022. European gas prices spiked, then forced a rapid pivot to American LNG, Norwegian gas, and renewables. Energy security became a top political concern again for the first time since the 1970s.
- Ukraine and Russia together accounted for roughly **30% of global wheat exports** and large shares of fertilizer and sunflower oil. Food prices spiked worldwide. Countries from Egypt to Indonesia faced real food security pressure.
- The lesson governments took: depending on a geopolitical adversary for any critical input (energy, food, chips, drugs) is a strategic mistake, no matter how much cheaper it is.

## The new vocabulary

- **Reshoring**: bring production back to the home country. Expensive, slow, but politically popular.
- **Nearshoring**: move production from far away (China) to nearby (Mexico for the US, Eastern Europe for Western Europe). Cheaper than reshoring, with fewer geopolitical risks.
- **Friend-shoring**: a term **Janet Yellen**, US Treasury Secretary, made popular in 2022. Move production to allied countries (Vietnam, India, Mexico, Poland). The idea is that you don't need everything at home, you just need it out of strategic rivals' reach.
- **China + 1**: the corporate version. Keep your China operations but build a second location somewhere else as a hedge. Apple, Samsung, and most major manufacturers have adopted versions of this.

## Who's winning

- **Vietnam** has been the biggest beneficiary in Southeast Asia. Apple, Samsung, Nike, and many others have moved meaningful capacity there. Vietnam's manufacturing exports more than doubled between 2018 and 2024.
- **India** is making a serious play. **Apple now assembles roughly 14% of iPhones in India** as of 2024, up from near zero five years earlier. The Modi government's "Production Linked Incentive" schemes have been the lever.
- **Mexico** has become the largest US trading partner, passing China in 2023. **Nearshoring to Mexico** is the biggest single beneficiary of the shift, with massive investment in Monterrey and along the US border.
- **Poland and Eastern Europe** have absorbed a lot of European manufacturing moving out of Germany or hedging away from China.

## The big policy moves

- **CHIPS Act (August 2022)**: roughly $52 billion in US subsidies to build semiconductor manufacturing on American soil. TSMC, Intel, and Samsung have all announced multi-billion-dollar US fabs. The goal: stop depending on Taiwan for the most advanced chips.
- **Inflation Reduction Act (August 2022)**: despite the name, mostly a clean-energy industrial policy bill. Massive subsidies for US-made electric vehicles, batteries, and solar. Forced South Korean and European companies to invest in US factories to qualify.
- **EU Critical Raw Materials Act (2023)**: European response. Target of 40% of critical raw materials processing in Europe by 2030, no more than 65% of any single material from one country.
- **Export controls on advanced chips to China (October 2022)**: the Biden administration's most aggressive economic move. The US essentially said: we will not let China get the most advanced chips or the equipment to make them. The Trump administration tightened these further. China has been pouring resources into building a domestic chip industry in response.

## The real question

- The honest answer isn't that globalization is ending. Global trade is still near record highs in dollar terms. Containers still move. Smartphones still get assembled across multiple continents.
- The real shift is that **globalization is splitting into competing blocs**. The US-aligned world (North America, Europe, Japan, Korea, India, Australia) trades freely within itself but increasingly restricts trade with China, Russia, and their partners. China is building parallel infrastructure (Belt and Road, BRICS expansion, yuan settlement) to do the same with friendly countries.
- This is what economists like **Adam Tooze** and **Mohamed El-Erian** mean when they talk about a multipolar economy. Not deglobalization. Re-globalization in blocs.

## What to carry away

- The 30-year experiment of "one global factory optimized for cost" is over, but trade itself isn't ending.
- Companies now build supply chains to be resilient first, cheap second. That's a real change in how businesses operate, and it costs money.
- Governments now treat where things get made as a strategic question, not just an economic one.
- In one sentence: the world spent thirty years building a single integrated supply chain in China, learned during COVID and Ukraine how fragile that was, and is now rebuilding it as a set of overlapping regional blocs that prize resilience and political alignment over pure efficiency.
