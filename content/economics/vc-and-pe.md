# VC and PE: how the money industry actually works

## What VC and PE are and why they matter

Venture Capital and Private Equity are the two big slices of "private capital." Both pool money from large investors, buy stakes in private companies, and aim to sell those stakes later for a profit. Both charge similar fees. But they bet on totally different things. **VC** funds early-stage companies that mostly fail, hoping a few become Google or Stripe. **PE** buys mature, established companies, restructures them, and sells in 5-7 years. Together, these two industries decide which startups get built, which mature companies get reshaped, and increasingly who owns big chunks of the economy. PE alone owns somewhere around 30% of corporate America by some counts. MBAs end up working in or with these worlds constantly, so the basic mental model is worth carrying around.

## Venture capital: the basics

- **What it is.** Equity investment in early-stage private companies, usually tech or other high-growth sectors.
- **The bet.** Most investments fail. A few succeed modestly. One or two return 10-100x and pay for the entire fund. This is power-law math, not normal-distribution math.
- **Origins.** **American Research and Development Corp (ARD)**, founded in 1946 by MIT and Harvard professors, is usually called the first VC firm. Their famous bet was Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) in 1957, which returned about 500x.
- **The Sand Hill Road era.** **Kleiner Perkins** and **Sequoia Capital** both opened on Sand Hill Road in Menlo Park in 1972. That stretch of road became shorthand for the industry. The 1970s tax reforms (lowering capital gains, allowing pension funds to invest in VC via the 1979 ERISA "prudent man rule" reinterpretation) unleashed the modern era.

## How a VC fund actually works

- **Limited Partners (LPs)** put up the money. Pension funds (CalPERS, Yale endowment), university endowments, sovereign wealth funds (Saudi PIF, Singapore GIC), insurance companies, family offices.
- **General Partners (GPs)** are the VCs themselves. They manage the fund, make investment decisions, take board seats.
- **2 and 20.** Standard fee structure: 2% annual management fee on assets, 20% "carried interest" on profits above a hurdle. So on a $500M fund, GPs make $10M/year just to run it, plus 20% of gains.
- **Fund life.** Usually 10 years, with extensions. First 3-4 years deploying capital, rest harvesting returns.
- **Power-law math.** In a typical 30-investment fund, maybe 15 go to zero, 12 return 1-3x, 2 return modest multiples, and 1 returns 50x+. That one investment carries everything. So VCs hunt for "fund returners" and pass on solid-but-not-spectacular bets.

## Power players

- **Andreessen Horowitz (a16z).** Founded 2009 by Marc Andreessen (Netscape) and Ben Horowitz. Pioneered the "platform" model where the firm provides services beyond money. Bet heavy on crypto, mixed results.
- **Sequoia Capital.** Founded 1972 by Don Valentine. Backed Apple, Google, YouTube, WhatsApp, Stripe, Airbnb. Probably the most consistently successful VC firm in history.
- **Founders Fund.** Peter Thiel's firm. Bet on Facebook, SpaceX, Palantir. Contrarian thesis-driven.
- **Y Combinator.** Different model. Started 2005 by Paul Graham. Accelerator: takes ~7% for a small check ($500K), runs 3-month batches, demo days. Alumni include Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, Coinbase, Reddit, DoorDash, Instacart.
- **Tiger Global, Coatue, SoftBank Vision Fund.** Crossover and growth-stage giants who reshaped late-stage VC in the 2020-2021 frenzy by writing huge checks at huge valuations. Many of those bets are now underwater.

## Private equity: the other beast

- **What it is.** Buy mature, profitable (or fixable) companies, often using a lot of debt, restructure them, sell them in 5-7 years.
- **The bet.** Cash flow predictability and operational improvement. Less about explosive growth, more about transformation, financial engineering, and efficiency.
- **Leveraged buyout (LBO).** The signature PE move. Buy a company for $1B using $300M of equity and $700M of debt. The debt sits on the acquired company's balance sheet, not the PE firm's. Pay down debt with the target's cash flow over years. Sell, ideally for $2B+.
- **Origins.** **KKR** (Kohlberg Kravis Roberts), founded 1976, pioneered LBOs. Their 1988 acquisition of RJR Nabisco for $25B was the largest LBO ever at the time, immortalized in *Barbarians at the Gate*.
- **Today.** **Blackstone** is the biggest with over $1 trillion AUM. **Apollo, KKR, Carlyle, Bain Capital** are the other names.

## The PE critique

- **Debt loading.** PE often loads target companies with debt, then pays itself dividends ("dividend recaps") from that debt. The target carries the debt; the PE firm has already pulled the money out.
- **Bankruptcies.** **Toys R Us** went bankrupt in 2017, six years after KKR/Bain/Vornado bought it in 2005. Critics blamed $5B of LBO debt that the toy retailer couldn't service when Amazon eroded margins.
- **Healthcare PE.** Hospital chains, dental groups, nursing homes bought by PE have faced quality complaints and staff reductions.
- **Returns debate.** PE has historically beaten the S&P 500 modestly net of fees. But recent vintages (2018-2022) are looking weak as exits stalled. The 2 and 20 fee model is hard to justify if you're only matching public market returns.

## VC vs PE: the comparison

- **VC**: bets on growth. Equity-funded. Power-law returns. Most companies fail. Bay Area and NYC hub.
- **PE**: bets on transformation and efficiency. Debt-funded. Normal-distribution returns. Most companies survive. NYC, London, global.
- **Hold period**: VC 5-10 years, PE 3-7 years.
- **Risk profile**: VC binary (huge win or zero). PE more modest range.
- **Industries**: VC concentrated in tech/biotech/frontier. PE across consumer, industrials, services, healthcare, software (especially in the last decade).

## Why this matters

- **Startup creation.** VC decides which products get built and which die. A startup that can't raise dies, no matter how good the product.
- **Corporate America.** PE owns roughly 30% of US corporate sector by some counts. Your dentist, your local newspaper, your favorite restaurant chain might be PE-owned and you wouldn't know.
- **Talent flows.** Top MBAs flock to PE; top engineers to VC-backed startups. Both shape the labor market in their orbit.
- **Public market shrinkage.** US public companies have declined from ~8,000 in 1996 to ~4,500 today, partly because PE keeps companies private longer or never lets them go public.

## What people get wrong

- VC isn't "investing in startups." It's a specific, narrow asset class with brutal power-law math. Angel investing and crowdfunding are different.
- PE isn't always asset stripping. Some PE firms genuinely improve operations. The bad actors get the headlines.
- The 2 and 20 fee structure is increasingly under pressure. LPs are demanding lower fees, especially as returns compress.
- "Private credit" (loans from non-banks to mid-size companies) is now bigger than the leveraged loan market and is the next industry to understand.

## What to carry away

- VC: bets on growth, mostly fails, one unicorn covers everything. 2 and 20 fee structure. Sand Hill Road since 1972.
- PE: buys mature companies, often with debt, restructures, sells. KKR invented LBOs. Blackstone is the giant.
- LPs put up the money, GPs manage it, both share carry.
- Power law in VC vs more normal returns in PE.
- The critique of PE (debt-loading, bankruptcies, healthcare quality) is real but partial. The critique of VC (echo chambers, herd behavior, founder mistreatment) is also real.
- In one sentence: VC funds the future, PE reshapes the present, and both run on other people's money.
