# The British Empire: history's largest empire and its long shadow

## What it was and why it matters

At its peak around **1920**, the British Empire controlled roughly **25% of the world's land area** and **23% of its population**, about **458 million people**. It was the largest empire in human history by both measures. The phrase "the empire on which the sun never sets" was literal — somewhere in British territory it was always daytime. It matters now because the world still runs on systems Britain exported: English as the global lingua franca, common law in around 60 countries, the Westminster parliamentary template, and a set of arbitrary borders (India-Pakistan, Israel-Palestine, several African disputes) that still produce conflict today. Half the people Vatsal will sit next to at Wharton come from countries whose modern shape Britain drew.

## The phases

- **First British Empire (1583-1783)**. Focused on the Americas and the Caribbean. Tobacco, sugar, and slaves were the economic engine. Britain lost the **13 American colonies** in the American Revolution and pivoted.
- **Second British Empire (1783-1815)**. After the American loss, Britain turned to Asia and the Pacific. Australia (1788), the consolidation of India under the East India Company, expansion into Southeast Asia.
- **Imperial Century (1815-1914)**. After defeating Napoleon, Britain had no peer competitor for almost a hundred years. This was the peak. **Pax Britannica** — the global order Britain managed through naval supremacy, the gold standard, and free trade.
- **Interwar decline (1918-1939)**. WWI shattered British finances. Independence movements, especially in India, accelerated.
- **Postwar decolonization (1945-1980)**. Britain emerged from WWII bankrupt. It granted or conceded independence to most of its empire within 30 years.

## Key acquisitions

- **India**. The East India Company arrived in 1600. The **1857 rebellion** (called the "Indian Mutiny" in British sources, the "First War of Independence" in Indian ones) led the British Crown to take direct control. The **British Raj** ran from 1858 until **1947**. India was always the empire's economic and strategic centerpiece.
- **Canada (1763)**, after Britain defeated France in the Seven Years' War.
- **Australia (1788)**, founded as a penal colony at Botany Bay.
- **New Zealand (1840)**, via the Treaty of Waitangi with Māori chiefs (which Britain interpreted very differently than the chiefs did, a dispute still being adjudicated).
- **South Africa (1814)**, taken from the Dutch during the Napoleonic Wars.
- **Egypt (1882-1956)**. Never formally a colony, but Britain effectively ran it for 74 years.
- **Singapore, Malaya, Hong Kong** as the chain of Asian trading posts.
- **Most of East and Southern Africa** carved out at the Berlin Conference of 1884-85.

## The instruments of empire

- **Royal Navy supremacy**, from the Battle of Trafalgar in **1805** until roughly 1945. For 140 years the British navy could project force anywhere on Earth.
- **Free trade**, especially after the 1846 repeal of the Corn Laws. Britain was the workshop of the world, exporting manufactures and importing raw materials from the colonies.
- **Technology of empire**. Railways, telegraph cables, and steamships compressed distances and made centralized control possible.
- **The Indian Army**. Indian troops were rotated globally to police the rest of the empire — Egypt, Sudan, China, the trenches of WWI.
- **Divide and rule**. Playing local groups against each other to make British control cheap. The legacy of this is still visible from Northern Ireland to Sri Lanka to India-Pakistan.

## Where the wealth came from

- **India**. Economist **Utsa Patnaik** has calculated that Britain extracted roughly **$45 trillion** in today's value from India over two centuries. The exact figure is contested, but the magnitude isn't seriously disputed by historians anymore.
- **The Caribbean sugar economy and the Atlantic slave trade**. Roughly **3 million Africans** were transported across the Atlantic on British ships before British abolition in **1807**. Sugar from Jamaica and Barbados was a foundation of British wealth.
- **The opium trade**. British merchants sold Indian opium into China against Chinese law, then fought two **Opium Wars (1839-42, 1856-60)** to force China to keep buying it.

## The mythology vs the reality

- **The mythology**: civilizing mission, white man's burden (Kipling, 1899), Pax Britannica, the empire that ended slavery, gave the world railways and parliamentary democracy.
- **The reality, in selected moments**:
  - **Bengal famine of 1943**. Roughly **3 million Indians died** while Britain continued exporting grain from Bengal to feed Allied troops. Churchill's wartime policies are directly implicated. He privately blamed the famine on Indians "breeding like rabbits."
  - **Tasmanian genocide (1820s-1830s)**. The Indigenous population of Tasmania was reduced to almost nothing by settler violence and forced relocation.
  - **Multiple Indian famines** in the 19th century, with contested British policy responsibility but consistent patterns of grain export during famine years.
  - **Boer concentration camps (1899-1902)**. Britain interned roughly **115,000 Boer civilians**, mostly women and children. At least **26,000** died of disease and starvation.
  - **Kenyan detention camps (1950s)**. During the Mau Mau uprising, Britain ran a network of camps where torture and abuse were widespread. Files were destroyed at independence; some were rediscovered in 2011.

## Why the empire ended

- **WWI** exhausted British finances and shattered the moral confidence that underpinned the imperial project.
- **WWII** bankrupted Britain outright. Lend-lease debts to the US weren't fully repaid until 2006.
- **Nationalism** rose globally. Indian independence movements under **Gandhi** and later figures became impossible to suppress without violence Britain could no longer afford or politically sustain.
- **The US emerged as the dominant Western power** and was, by Cold War necessity, against formal empires (while building an informal one).

## The legacy

- **English as global lingua franca**. Roughly **1.5 billion** people speak English today, most as a second language. This is the empire's most durable export.
- **Common law** is the legal system in roughly 60 countries, including the US, India, Australia, Canada, and most of Anglophone Africa.
- **Westminster parliamentary system** is the template for democracies from Canada to Australia to India to Jamaica.
- **The Commonwealth** is the soft-power successor — 54 member states, mostly former British territories.
- **Arbitrary borders that still cause wars**. India-Pakistan partition in 1947 displaced **15 million people** and killed roughly **1-2 million** in the violence. Israel-Palestine is partly the legacy of the British Mandate (1920-1948). Several African border disputes trace to British map-drawing.

## The current reckoning

- **Statue debates** in the UK and former colonies. Cecil Rhodes statues challenged at Oxford and Cape Town. Edward Colston's statue pulled down in Bristol in 2020.
- **Museum repatriation**. The Benin Bronzes, looted from Nigeria in 1897, are being returned by some museums; the British Museum is still holding most of its contested objects, including the Parthenon Marbles.
- **Formal apologies**, slowly. David Cameron apologized in 2013 for the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** of 1919, where British troops killed at least 379 Indians (Indian sources put the figure at 1,000+) at a peaceful gathering.

## What people get wrong

- The empire wasn't simply "bad" or simply "good." It built infrastructure and extracted resources, exported institutions and committed atrocities. The honest reckoning holds both at once.
- The British public has historically been told a sanitized version. School curricula are slowly changing, but the gap between what Britons learn and what former colonies remember is still wide.
- The empire didn't end cleanly in 1947 or 1980. The economic and institutional legacies are still actively shaping outcomes today.

## Why it matters now

- The borders, languages, legal systems, and grievances of around a third of the world's population trace directly to British imperial decisions.
- The "Anglosphere" — US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand — is a working geopolitical bloc built on shared imperial inheritance.
- Modern China's grievance narrative includes the Opium Wars and the "century of humiliation." Modern India's strategic identity is partly defined against British memory.
- In one sentence: the British Empire is the single biggest reason the modern world has the borders, languages, and unresolved arguments it does, and we are still living inside the answers it imposed and the questions it left open.
