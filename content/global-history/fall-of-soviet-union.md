# The fall of the Soviet Union: 1985-1991, the empire that ended without a war

## What happened and why it matters

In six years between 1985 and 1991, the **Soviet Union** went from one of two global superpowers to fifteen separate countries. About **290 million people** went to bed citizens of a communist empire and woke up citizens of new nations. There was no losing war. No invasion. No revolution in the classic sense. The system simply ran out of room and one of its own leaders started reforms he couldn't control. It matters because it ended the bipolar world that had organized international politics since 1945, set the conditions for everything that followed (NATO expansion, the rise of Putin, eventually the war in Ukraine), and stands as one of the few examples in history of a great power dissolving without a major war.

## Why the system was already broken by the 1980s

- The economy had stalled. **GDP growth had fallen to around 2% or less** by the early 1980s, and that was the official figure. Real performance was probably worse.
- The command economy could move steel and tanks but not consumer goods. By the 1980s ordinary Soviet citizens were watching pirated Western television and seeing what they didn't have.
- The **war in Afghanistan (1979-1989)** was draining money, men, and morale. Roughly 15,000 Soviet soldiers died. The war was the USSR's Vietnam.
- **Chernobyl in April 1986** exposed how rotten the system had become. The reactor explosion was followed by days of denial, secrecy, and bureaucratic paralysis. For Soviet citizens watching, this was the moment the official story stopped being credible.

## Gorbachev's bet

- **Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary in **March 1985** at age 54, the youngest member of the Politburo. He believed the system could be saved if it was reformed honestly.
- **Glasnost (openness)**. Press restrictions eased. Banned books got published. Stalin-era crimes became open conversation. Soviet citizens read their own history for the first time, and what they read corroded the system's legitimacy.
- **Perestroika (restructuring)**. Partial economic reforms, limited private enterprise, decentralization. The reforms were too radical for the old guard and too timid to actually work. The economy got worse.
- **Sinatra Doctrine (1989)**. Gorbachev told the Eastern bloc that each country could "do it their way." The Brezhnev Doctrine, which had justified Soviet invasions of Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, was dead.

## 1989: the year Eastern Europe walked out

- **Hungary opened its border with Austria in May 1989**. East Germans started leaving through Hungary, around the wall.
- **Poland elected a Solidarity-led government in June**. The first non-communist government in the Eastern bloc.
- **Berlin Wall fell on November 9**. It came down partly by accident. An East German official misread a memo at a press conference and said the new travel rules took effect "immediately." Crowds rushed the checkpoints and the guards, without orders, let them through.
- The **Velvet Revolution** in Czechoslovakia was bloodless. Romania's was not — **Nicolae Ceaușescu** was overthrown and executed by firing squad on Christmas Day 1989.
- In six months the entire Eastern bloc went from communist to post-communist, mostly without violence.

## The USSR itself unravels

- The Soviet republics had been "republics" on paper but tightly controlled in practice. With glasnost and the Eastern European collapse, that fiction stopped holding.
- The **Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania)** declared sovereignty in March 1990, citing the illegal Soviet annexation in 1940.
- **August 1991 coup attempt**. Hardline Soviet officials placed Gorbachev under house arrest at his Crimea dacha and tried to seize power. **Boris Yeltsin**, then president of the Russian republic, climbed onto a tank outside the Russian White House and defied them. Three days later the coup collapsed.
- After the failed coup, Gorbachev's authority was finished. Yeltsin's was rising. Republics declared independence in waves.
- **December 8, 1991**: leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus met in a forest near Belarus and agreed the USSR no longer existed.
- **December 25, 1991**: Gorbachev resigned on television. The Soviet flag came down from the Kremlin. Eleven of the fifteen republics formed the loose Commonwealth of Independent States. The USSR was formally dissolved the next day.

## What the 1990s did to Russia

- The transition was catastrophic for ordinary Russians. **Shock therapy** economic reform under Yeltsin freed prices and privatized state assets in months rather than years.
- **Hyperinflation** wiped out savings. Pensions became worthless.
- A handful of well-connected operators bought up state assets at fire-sale prices and became the **oligarchs**. By the late 1990s, a small group owned much of what had been national wealth.
- **Life expectancy for Russian men dropped** by roughly five years over the 1990s. Alcoholism, suicide, and untreated disease rose sharply.
- Organized crime exploded. The state was hollow.

## The Putin era

- **Vladimir Putin**, a former KGB officer, became prime minister in August 1999 and acting president on December 31, 1999, after Yeltsin resigned.
- He stabilized Russia in the early 2000s, partly on the back of rising oil prices.
- He also rebuilt central authority, neutralized independent media, brought oligarchs to heel (those who didn't submit lost their assets or their freedom), and gradually made the political system authoritarian.
- He famously called the collapse of the Soviet Union "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the 20th century." That sentence is the key to a lot of what came next.

## Why it matters now

- **NATO expanded eastward** through the 1990s and 2000s. Former Warsaw Pact countries joined. Russia cites this as a betrayal of verbal assurances given to Gorbachev, though no written treaty existed. This is one of the foundational grievances behind Russian foreign policy today.
- **Ukraine became independent in 1991**. Its status remained contested by Russia from the start. The current war is in large part a delayed dispute over what Soviet dissolution actually meant.
- **The "former Soviet space"** — Belarus, Central Asia, the Caucasus — is still a contested zone where Russian influence is being challenged by China, Turkey, the EU, and local nationalisms.
- In one sentence: the USSR's quiet collapse looked at the time like the end of an era of conflict. It turned out to be the start of one whose terms we are still negotiating.
