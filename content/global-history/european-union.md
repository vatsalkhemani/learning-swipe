# The EU: origins, Brexit, and current state

## What the EU is and why it matters

The European Union is the most ambitious peace project in modern history. Six countries that had been at war with each other for most of the previous century bound their economies together in the 1950s to make another European war impossible. That bargain expanded into a 27-country union of 450 million people, a single market, a common currency for 20 of them, and the world's largest trading bloc. It matters because it's the only working example of voluntary supranational integration, because Brexit tested whether you can actually leave, and because Russia's war on Ukraine has forced the EU to do things (joint borrowing, defense coordination, energy independence) it had ducked for decades.

## Why the EU exists

- World War II killed about 40 million Europeans and physically destroyed the continent. The third European war in 75 years between France and Germany.
- The founders made a bet: countries that depend on each other economically don't go to war.
- **Robert Schuman**, French foreign minister, announced the plan on **May 9, 1950** (now Europe Day). Bind French and German coal and steel production so completely that war becomes "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible."

## The slow build, 1951-1992

- **European Coal and Steel Community**, founded 1951. Six members: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg.
- **Treaty of Rome**, March 1957. Created the European Economic Community (EEC), a common market for goods, with the same six countries.
- **First enlargement, 1973**: UK, Ireland, Denmark join. The UK had been blocked twice by **Charles de Gaulle** before he died.
- **Southern enlargement**: Greece in 1981, Spain and Portugal in 1986. All three had recently emerged from dictatorships. EU membership locked in democratic transitions.
- **Single European Act, 1986**. Pushed the common market deeper. Goods, services, capital, people moving across borders without checks.
- **Maastricht Treaty, signed February 1992**. The big leap. Created the European Union (the political layer above the economic), set the rules for a common currency, and introduced EU citizenship.

## The euro

- The **euro** launched as an accounting currency in 1999. Physical notes and coins entered circulation on **January 1, 2002** in 12 countries.
- The structural problem was baked in from day one. The euro is a monetary union without a fiscal union. Countries share a currency but run their own budgets. Works fine in calm weather. Doesn't work in storms.
- Today **20 of 27** EU members use the euro. Sweden and Denmark have opted out. Most newer members are committed to join eventually.

## Eastern enlargement

- **May 1, 2004**: 10 countries joined at once. Eight former communist states (Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania) plus Malta and Cyprus.
- Largest expansion in EU history. Brought the EU to Russia's borders and changed its character.
- Bulgaria and Romania followed in 2007. Croatia in 2013.
- The bet: anchor these new democracies westward, prevent backsliding. Worked for most. **Hungary** under Viktor Orban and **Poland** under PiS (2015-23) tested whether EU rules could be enforced against members that turned authoritarian.

## The constitutional crisis and Lisbon

- 2004: a draft **European Constitution** drawn up to consolidate decades of treaties.
- **2005**: French and Dutch voters rejected it in referendums. Big embarrassment.
- The political class found a workaround: the **Treaty of Lisbon**, signed 2007 and in force from 2009. Most of the constitution's content, repackaged as treaty amendments to avoid referendums. Created the position of **European Council President** and gave the EU a foreign policy chief.

## The sovereign debt crisis, 2010-12

- The 2008 global financial crisis exposed the design flaw in the euro. Markets started questioning whether Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy could repay their debts.
- **Greece** was the worst. About 180% debt-to-GDP by 2011. Bailed out three times. Austerity programs were brutal. Unemployment hit 27%. The Greek crisis cost Greece roughly a quarter of its GDP.
- **Mario Draghi**, then ECB president, in July 2012: "Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do **whatever it takes** to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough." Markets calmed almost overnight. Those four words are credited with saving the euro.

## Brexit

- The UK had always been a reluctant member. Margaret Thatcher famously demanded "I want my money back" in 1984. The UK kept the pound, opted out of Schengen, kept its own working hours rules.
- **David Cameron** promised an in-out referendum in his 2015 election campaign, expecting to win it.
- **June 23, 2016**: **52% Leave, 48% Remain**. The campaign had been ugly. **Jo Cox**, a Labour MP, was murdered by a far-right attacker a week before the vote.
- **Theresa May** spent three years failing to get a deal through Parliament. **Boris Johnson** replaced her in 2019, won a big election, and pushed through a hard Brexit.
- The UK formally left on **January 31, 2020**, and the transition period ended **December 31, 2020**.
- Five years in, the British economy is measurably smaller than it would have been in the EU. Most studies estimate 4-5% lower GDP. Trade volumes with the EU are down. London has held up better than expected as a financial center.
- Nobody else has seriously moved to leave. The lesson European populists took from Brexit was to reform from inside, not exit.

## The current state

- **27 members**, 450 million people, the world's largest single market.
- **EU AI Act** entered force in 2024. First major AI regulation in the world. Tiered risk-based approach. Whether it crushes EU AI competitiveness is the open argument.
- **Russia's invasion of Ukraine, February 2022**, broke a lot of EU paralysis. Energy weaning off Russian gas (painful, especially for Germany), defense spending up across the board, weapons supplied to Ukraine.
- **NextGenerationEU**, July 2020, was the COVID recovery fund. Roughly **750 billion euros**, the first time the EU did joint borrowing at scale. Quiet but important step toward fiscal integration.
- **Sweden** and **Finland** joined NATO (not the EU, but the same direction) after Russia's invasion. NATO unity is unexpectedly strong.

## The open tensions

- **Democratic deficit.** Decisions made in Brussels feel remote. The European Parliament has gained power over time but voter turnout for EU elections is still low.
- **Sovereignty versus integration.** Every crisis pushes toward more centralization. Every centralization push triggers a populist backlash. This is the EU's permanent dialectic.
- **Rule of law.** Hungary and (until recently) Poland tested whether the EU could enforce its values against members who violate them. The answer is sort of, slowly, with leverage from EU funds.
- **Enlargement.** Ukraine, Moldova, Georgia, the Western Balkans all want in. Bringing in Ukraine would be the biggest enlargement ever and would reshape the EU's center of gravity eastward.
- **Defense.** The EU has spent 70 years free-riding on US security. Trump's return makes the free ride less reliable. Building actual EU defense capacity is the project of the next decade.

## What to carry away

- The original move (1951) and the founding bet (bind economies to prevent war) have held. No European war between member states in 80 years.
- The euro is the EU's biggest gamble and its biggest structural vulnerability. Currency without fiscal union still hasn't been fully resolved.
- Brexit proved you can leave. The cost proved most others won't.
- Russia's war did more for EU integration in two years than the previous 20 did.
- One sentence: the EU is a 75-year project to make war between France and Germany impossible, now stress-tested by a real war on its eastern border.
