# African decolonization: how 54 countries emerged from European empires in 30 years

## What this was and why it matters

Between roughly 1951 and 1980, almost every European colony in Africa became independent. **54 modern African countries** trace their statehood to this short window. The continent that had been carved up at the **Berlin Conference of 1884-85** by European powers became, within three decades, a continent of nation-states. The borders mostly stayed where the colonizers had drawn them, even though those borders ran across ethnic groups, languages, and ecologies in ways that have caused conflict ever since. It matters now because Africa has **1.4 billion people**, the youngest population on Earth, the fastest population growth, and is becoming a central arena for US-China competition, climate adaptation, and global migration politics. Understanding how these countries came to be is the starting point for understanding what they are becoming.

## What Africa looked like before WWII

- Almost the entire continent was colonized. Only two places were genuinely independent.
- **Liberia** had been founded in 1847 by freed American slaves resettled by the American Colonization Society. Awkward founding, but independent.
- **Ethiopia** had defeated an Italian invasion in 1896 and stayed independent until Mussolini conquered it in 1936. Italy lost it again during WWII.
- Everywhere else was European territory.

## The colonizers and what they held

- **Britain** had the largest holdings. Egypt, Sudan, Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, Ghana, Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe and Zambia), South Africa.
- **France** held most of West Africa, Algeria, Madagascar, and parts of Central Africa.
- **Belgium** held the **Congo**, where the rule under King Leopold II from 1885 to 1908 was the single most brutal colonial regime on the continent. An estimated **10 million Congolese died** from forced labor, starvation, and violence in the rubber economy.
- **Portugal** held Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau, and Cape Verde.
- **Italy** held Libya and briefly Ethiopia.
- **Germany** lost its African colonies after WWI; they were redistributed to Britain, France, and Belgium.
- **Spain** held Equatorial Guinea and Western Sahara.

## How the wave broke

- **Libya in 1951** was the first sub-Saharan-or-North-African colony to gain independence after WWII.
- **Egypt's 1952 revolution** under Nasser threw out the British-backed monarchy. Egypt had been formally independent since 1922 but effectively a British protectorate.
- **Ghana on March 6, 1957**. **Kwame Nkrumah**'s independence speech set the template — "Africa for Africans." Ghana's flag-raising was the symbolic moment African decolonization became visible to the world.
- **1960: the Year of Africa**. Seventeen countries became independent in a single calendar year, mostly French and Belgian colonies.
- **Algeria in 1962**, after an **eight-year war with France** that killed roughly **1 million Algerians** and displaced millions more. Algeria's war was the bloody counterexample to the relatively peaceful French decolonization elsewhere.
- **Kenya in 1963**, after the **Mau Mau uprising (1952-1960)**, which the British suppressed with detention camps and mass violence.
- **Angola and Mozambique in 1975**, after a leftist revolution in Portugal in 1974 ended the dictatorship at home and the colonial wars abroad.
- **Zimbabwe in 1980**, after a long war against the white-minority Rhodesian government.
- **South Africa in 1994**. Not technically decolonization (white minority rule had run an independent state since 1910), but the end of apartheid and the election of **Nelson Mandela** is usually treated as the closing chapter of African liberation.

## The problems independence inherited

- **Borders drawn by Europeans**. The Berlin Conference of 1884-85 divided Africa with rulers in Berlin who had never set foot on the continent. The borders ignored ethnic groups, splitting some across countries and forcing rivals to share states. Most of these borders are still in place.
- **Weak institutions**. Colonial administration had been thin, extractive, and racially exclusive. Independent governments inherited a shell, not a state.
- **Single-party states and coups**. Many independence leaders consolidated power into one-party rule, citing the need for national unity. Around **80 successful military coups** happened across the continent between the 1960s and 1990s.
- **Cold War proxy battles**. The **Angolan civil war (1975-2002)** ran for 27 years, with the US, USSR, Cuba, and apartheid South Africa all backing different sides. **Mozambique's civil war (1977-1992)** had a similar dynamic.
- **Structural adjustment programs**. The **IMF and World Bank** in the 1980s and 1990s imposed market reforms in exchange for loans. The reforms often cut social spending, opened markets in ways that hurt local producers, and deepened crises rather than solving them.
- **HIV/AIDS**. Sub-Saharan Africa was the epicenter of the global epidemic. At its 2000s peak, life expectancy in some countries dropped by more than 15 years. Antiretroviral access has since transformed the trajectory, but a generation was lost.

## What modern Africa is

- **1.4 billion people**, projected to be **2.5 billion by 2050**.
- **Youngest continent**. Median age in many African countries is under 20.
- **Nigeria** is projected to become the **third most populous country in the world by 2100**, behind only India and China.
- **Urbanization is rapid**. Lagos, Kinshasa, Cairo, and Addis Ababa are among the fastest-growing cities on the planet.
- Outcomes are wildly uneven. **Botswana, Rwanda, Ghana, Senegal** are solid or improving democracies. **Sudan, South Sudan, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Central African Republic** are mired in ongoing conflicts. **Ethiopia** has been a development success and is also a recent civil war.

## What people get wrong

- "Africa" is treated as a single thing. It is 54 countries, more than 2,000 languages, and four major religious traditions. The variance within the continent is larger than the variance across the rest of the world.
- Decolonization is often told as a story of grants. Most of it was won, not given. Some places (Algeria, Kenya, Angola, Zimbabwe) involved long armed struggles.
- The continent is not "behind." It is on a different trajectory shaped by a specific colonial history, a specific commodity dependence, and a specific demographic curve. The framing of "catching up" misses what's actually happening.

## Why it matters now

- **US-China competition** is increasingly playing out in Africa. China is the largest infrastructure investor on the continent. The US is responding with its own initiatives.
- **Climate change** hits African countries hardest first. The Sahel is drying. Coastal cities are flooding. Adaptation is becoming the central political issue in many places.
- **Migration** to Europe and the Gulf is going to be a defining issue of the next 30 years, and it starts in Africa.
- In one sentence: 54 countries got their borders in 30 years from empires that had ruled them for a century, and the next 30 years will decide whether those borders hold the world's youngest continent together as it becomes the world's largest.
