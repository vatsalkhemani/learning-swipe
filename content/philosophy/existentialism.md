# Existentialism: meaning is something you build, not something you find

## What is existentialism and why it matters

Existentialism is a 19th and 20th-century European philosophy that started from one uncomfortable observation. The old sources of meaning (religion, tradition, the natural order) had lost their authority, and no replacement had arrived. The existentialists said that's not a problem to solve by finding new authorities. It's the human condition, and the only honest response is to build your own meaning through how you live. It matters because most modern frameworks for purpose, identity, and authenticity (self-help, therapy, the "find your why" industry) are downstream of existentialist moves, often without knowing it.

## How it started

- **Soren Kierkegaard (1813-1855)** is the usual precursor. A Danish Christian who hated organized Christianity for making faith easy. He argued that real faith required a "leap" past reason into commitment, and that this leap was the only thing that could resolve the dread (he called it *angst*) of being a self that has to choose what it is.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900)** pushed it secular. His famous line "God is dead" wasn't a celebration. It was a warning. The Christian moral order had given Europe its sense of meaning for 1,500 years, and now that the educated class no longer believed in it, nothing was holding the moral architecture up. Either people would invent new values through "will to power," or they'd sink into nihilism. He died insane in 1900, his sister later twisted his work into Nazi propaganda, and the real Nietzsche took decades to recover.
- The label "existentialism" itself only got attached after World War II, in the Parisian cafe scene around **Jean-Paul Sartre** and **Simone de Beauvoir**. They held court at the Cafe de Flore in Saint-Germain, drinking, smoking, arguing, and producing a body of work that defined post-war European thought.
- **Albert Camus** ran in the same Paris circles but rejected the existentialist label, preferring "absurdist." He and Sartre publicly broke in 1952 over politics (Camus opposed Stalinism, Sartre defended it), and never reconciled.

## The key ideas

- **Existence precedes essence.** This is Sartre's slogan, and the whole movement turns on it. A knife has an essence (cutting) before it exists, because the knifemaker designed it for a purpose. Humans don't work that way. You exist first, as a blank, and what you are is the sum of what you do. There's no human nature waiting to be discovered. You're making yourself with every choice.
- **Radical freedom, and the anxiety it brings.** If nothing predetermines you, every choice is yours, and the weight of that is crushing. Sartre called this *anguish*. Kierkegaard called it *angst*. Most people flee from it. Few sit with it.
- **Bad faith.** Sartre's term for the lies you tell yourself to escape that freedom. Saying "I had no choice" when you did. Hiding behind your role ("I'm just a waiter," "I'm just following orders") so you don't have to own who you are. Most of daily life, Sartre thought, was bad faith.
- **The absurd.** Camus' angle. Humans crave meaning. The universe doesn't supply any. That gap is the absurd. His essay *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) opens with a famous line: the only serious philosophical question is whether to commit suicide. His answer is no. The right response to a meaningless universe is revolt: keep living, keep creating, keep loving, in full knowledge that none of it is cosmically justified. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
- **Authenticity.** The existentialist virtue. Not "be yourself" in the Instagram sense. It means living in full awareness of your freedom, owning your choices, refusing the comfort of pretending you had to do what you did.
- **Situated freedom (de Beauvoir's correction).** Sartre's radical freedom sounded great if you were a man with a teaching post. De Beauvoir, in *The Second Sex* (1949), pointed out that women weren't free in the same way, because society had constructed them as "the Other" from birth. Freedom is always exercised inside a situation, and some situations are heavier than others. This move opened existentialism to feminism and post-colonial thought.

## Existentialism is not nihilism

This is the misreading that won't die. Nihilism says there is no meaning, so nothing matters, so do whatever. Existentialism says there is no inherent meaning, therefore you are responsible for creating one, and that responsibility is the most serious thing about being alive. Camus put it sharply: the absurd person doesn't conclude that nothing matters. They conclude that they have to live more fiercely, not less. Nietzsche raged against nihilism his entire career. He saw it as the disease that would eat the modern world if no one built new values to replace the dead ones.

## Modern descendants

- **Viktor Frankl** survived Auschwitz and wrote *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946), arguing that the prisoners who survived were the ones who held onto a personal "why." His logotherapy is existentialism translated into clinical psychology.
- **The "find your why" movement** (Simon Sinek, the whole purpose-economy genre) is a softened, commercial descendant. The existentialists would notice that "finding" your why concedes the game. They'd say you build it.
- **Modern therapy** absorbed huge chunks of existentialist thinking. Irvin Yalom's existential psychotherapy works directly with death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness as the four ultimate concerns clients can't avoid.
- **Identity politics and authenticity culture** owe more to existentialism than most participants realize. The idea that you get to define yourself and that society has been imposing false essences on you (woman, gay, Black, working-class) traces back to de Beauvoir and Sartre.

## What people get wrong

- It's not "everything is permitted." Sartre was clear: when you choose for yourself, you choose for everyone, because you're declaring what a human ought to do. The freedom is heavier than nihilism, not lighter.
- It's not French and depressing. The originals (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Dostoevsky who's often included) were religious or post-religious thinkers wrestling with how to live well. The Paris cafe aesthetic is one chapter.
- It's not the same as absurdism. Camus drew a hard line. Existentialists said you can construct meaning. He said you can't, and you have to live ferociously anyway. Smaller gap than it sounds, but he insisted on it.

## What to carry away

- The starting move: nothing outside you is going to hand you a purpose. The universe didn't issue you one at birth.
- The responsibility that follows: you build meaning through your choices, and you can't outsource that to a religion, a career, a relationship, or a self-help book.
- The discipline: notice the moments you tell yourself you had no choice. That's usually a lie. Sit with the freedom instead.
- In one sentence: existentialism is the philosophy that takes the disappearance of inherited meaning seriously and answers it by making you the author of your own life, with all the weight that implies.
