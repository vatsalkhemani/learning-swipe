# The Ancient Greeks: Socrates, Plato, Aristotle and what they actually invented

## What the Greek trio gave us and why it matters

Almost every move in Western philosophy traces back to three Athenians who lived between 470 and 322 BCE: Socrates, his student Plato, and Plato's student Aristotle. In about 150 years, this teacher-student chain invented systematic argument, ethics as a field, political theory, logic, metaphysics, and most of the questions science later inherited. They didn't agree with each other. Each generation pushed back on the last. That disagreement is what made the tradition compounding instead of dogmatic. It matters because if you've ever asked "what is justice," "what is a good life," "what is real," or "how do I know that," you're using their vocabulary, often without knowing it.

## The Athens they were arguing in

- **Athens, 5th-4th century BCE.** A small Greek city-state of maybe 250,000 people, of which around 30,000 were voting male citizens. The world's first sustained experiment in direct democracy. Citizens voted on laws in the assembly, served as jurors by lot, and rotated through public office.
- The city had just won the Persian Wars (490-479 BCE), then lost the long, exhausting **Peloponnesian War** to Sparta (431-404 BCE). Plague had killed maybe a third of the population in 430 BCE, including their political leader Pericles.
- Out of that turmoil came an intellectual marketplace. Traveling teachers called **Sophists** charged fees to teach the sons of wealthy Athenians how to win arguments in court and politics. Socrates worked the same streets but refused to take money and refused to teach rhetoric. He said he taught people how to think, not how to win.

## Socrates (~470-399 BCE)

- Wrote nothing. Everything we know about him comes from Plato (his student) and Xenophon (another student), with a hostile cameo in the playwright Aristophanes' comedy *The Clouds*.
- Worked as a stonemason, served in the army at three campaigns, was famously ugly, walked around barefoot, married Xanthippe (who reportedly had a temper, fairly, given the husband).
- His method was the **elenchus**, often just called the Socratic method. He'd pick a respected Athenian (a general, a politician, a poet) and ask them to define a virtue they claimed to embody. Then by asking follow-up questions, he'd show their definition contradicted itself. The lesson was almost always: the people who think they know don't, and the first step to wisdom is admitting you don't either.
- The oracle at Delphi told his friend that no one in Greece was wiser than Socrates. Socrates spent years trying to disprove this, interviewing experts. He concluded the oracle was right only in this narrow sense: he knew he didn't know, and everyone else thought they did.
- In 399 BCE, three Athenians charged him with "impiety" and "corrupting the youth." A jury of 500 citizens voted him guilty by a narrow margin. He was sentenced to death by hemlock. He had chances to escape and refused them, arguing that fleeing would betray the city that had made him. Plato's dialogue *Phaedo* gives the death scene.
- What he invented: the idea that ethics is something you investigate by argument, not something you receive from religion or tradition. Also the idea that the unexamined life isn't worth living.

## Plato (~428-348 BCE)

- Aristocratic Athenian, originally trained as a wrestler and poet. Was about 28 when Socrates was executed, and the trauma reshaped his life. He came to distrust democracy permanently after watching it kill the wisest man he knew.
- Around 387 BCE, he founded the **Academy** on the outskirts of Athens, a grove sacred to the hero Akademos. It ran for nearly 900 years, until the Christian emperor Justinian closed it in 529 CE. The word "academic" comes from this grove.
- Wrote in dialogues, almost always featuring Socrates as the main character. Scholars argue (Gregory Vlastos was the clearest) that early dialogues are probably the real Socrates, while later ones use Socrates as a mouthpiece for Plato's own ideas.
- The **Theory of Forms** is his signature move. The chair you're sitting on is an imperfect copy of an ideal, unchanging Form of "Chair" that exists in a non-physical realm. Same for justice, beauty, the good. The visible world is shifting and unreliable. Reality is the world of Forms, accessible only by reason. This is the original split between appearance and reality, and almost every later metaphysics is either defending or attacking it.
- *The Republic* is his most famous work. It builds a thought-experiment city-state to ask what justice is, and ends up with a deeply anti-democratic answer: societies should be ruled by philosopher-kings who have been educated for decades and own no property. The "allegory of the cave" sits in Book 7. Prisoners chained in a cave see only shadows on the wall and think the shadows are reality. The philosopher is the one who turns around, walks out, and sees the sun.
- What he invented: systematic written philosophy, the institutional university, political theory as a discipline, the distinction between appearance and reality, mathematical realism (the idea that numbers exist).

## Aristotle (384-322 BCE)

- Not Athenian. Came from Stagira in northern Greece. His father had been court physician to the king of Macedon. Joined Plato's Academy at 17 and stayed for 20 years.
- Left Athens after Plato died and the Academy passed to Plato's nephew instead of him. Spent years doing biological research on the island of Lesbos, dissecting marine animals. He's the first systematic biologist.
- In 343 BCE, the Macedonian king Philip II hired him to tutor his teenage son, **Alexander**, who would later conquer the Persian Empire and reshape the Mediterranean.
- Returned to Athens around 335 BCE and founded his own school, the **Lyceum**, as a rival to the Academy. His students walked while he lectured, so they were called peripatetics, "the walkers."
- Where Plato looked up to the abstract Forms, Aristotle looked down at the actual world. He thought the Forms were a confused move. The "chair-ness" of a chair isn't in another realm; it's in the chair itself, in the way its matter is organized to serve its function. This empirical instinct made him the patron saint of natural science.
- His range is staggering. He wrote founding texts in logic (the syllogism, "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal"), ethics (the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the basis of virtue ethics), politics, rhetoric, poetics, physics, metaphysics, biology, meteorology. A medieval scholar called him simply "The Philosopher."
- In ethics, his big move was **eudaimonia**, usually translated "flourishing." The good life isn't pleasure (Epicurus' answer) and isn't duty (Kant's, much later). It's the active exercise of your full human capacities in line with virtue, across a complete life.

## How they disagreed

- Socrates wouldn't commit to positive doctrines. He questioned. Plato systematized.
- Plato distrusted the senses and reached for an abstract realm of Forms. Aristotle trusted the senses and built his philosophy from observation.
- Plato thought democracy was mob rule. Aristotle thought it had defects but was viable if balanced. He invented the comparative study of constitutions, having his students collect 158 of them.
- Plato's perfect society is ruled by philosophers. Aristotle's is ruled by a broad middle class with strong civic virtues.

## What people get wrong

- Socrates wasn't a kindly teacher. He was a public nuisance who annoyed powerful Athenians until they killed him. His method was as much theater as instruction.
- Plato wasn't an idealist in the soft modern sense (someone with high hopes). He was an idealist in the technical sense: he believed ideas were more real than physical things.
- Aristotle wasn't a proto-scientist. He believed objects had inherent purposes (final causes), which modern science abandoned in the 17th century. Galileo and Newton built physics by rejecting big chunks of Aristotle.
- They aren't interchangeable "ancient Greek philosophers." The disagreements between them are most of the story.

## What to carry away

- One city, three generations, a teacher-student chain that defined the West.
- Socrates invented argument. Plato invented the university and the abstract metaphysical realm. Aristotle invented empirical investigation and most of the disciplines.
- They disagreed at every step. The tradition's strength is that disagreement compounded into a deeper conversation.
- In one sentence: between 470 and 322 BCE, Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle built the questions Western philosophy has been answering ever since, and the vocabulary to keep asking them.
