# Marxism: the political philosophy that tried to predict history and got half-right

## What is Marxism and why it matters

Marxism is the body of social, economic, and political thought built by Karl Marx in the mid-1800s and developed by his followers ever since. It started as an attempt to explain why industrial capitalism produced such staggering wealth on one side and such staggering misery on the other, and it ended up becoming the ideology of states ruling roughly a third of the world's population in the 20th century. Most of those states are gone. The intellectual tradition is not. It matters because Marx remains the sharpest critic of capitalism anyone has produced, his analytical tools are still in active use by serious economists and historians, and the political vocabulary of the modern left is mostly his.

## How it started

- **Karl Marx (1818-1883)** was a German philosopher and economist from a Jewish family that had converted to Lutheranism. He trained as a Hegelian philosopher, drifted into journalism, got expelled from Germany, France, and Belgium in turn for radical politics, and spent the last 34 years of his life in London, mostly broke.
- His writing was funded by **Friedrich Engels (1820-1895)**, a wealthy textile-factory heir who knew Manchester's slums from the inside because his family owned a mill there. Engels wrote *The Condition of the Working Class in England* in 1845, which gave Marx the empirical ground for his theory.
- The background was the **Industrial Revolution at its most brutal**. Twelve-hour days for children, life expectancy in Manchester of 17 years for working-class men in 1842, no safety net, no vote, no recourse. Marx and Engels were writing inside the worst phase of it.
- They borrowed Hegel's dialectical method (history advances through the clash of opposites and their resolution) and ripped out the idealism. Hegel said ideas drive history. Marx said material conditions drive ideas, and ideas drive history only because they're shaped by who controls the means of production.

## The key ideas

- **Historical materialism.** History is not driven by ideas, religions, or great men. It is driven by who controls the tools and resources of production, and the social arrangements they impose to keep that control. Every other layer (politics, culture, religion) is the superstructure built on that economic base.
- **Dialectical materialism.** Hegel's dialectic of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, but applied to material conditions instead of ideas. Each economic system contains the contradictions that produce the next one. Feudalism produced the bourgeoisie that destroyed it. Capitalism would produce the proletariat that would destroy capitalism.
- **Class struggle.** "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." Famous opening of *The Communist Manifesto*. Under capitalism the fight was between the **bourgeoisie** (owners of capital) and the **proletariat** (workers who sell their labor).
- **Labor theory of value.** The real value of any good comes from the human labor put into producing it. Profit comes from paying workers less than the value they create. Marx called this gap the "surplus value" and treated it as the engine of capitalist exploitation.
- **Alienation.** Workers under capitalism are alienated from the product of their labor (they don't own it), from the act of producing (it's just a job), from other workers (they're competitors), and from their own humanity (they're treated as labor inputs). This was Marx's most psychologically rich idea.
- **Commodity fetishism.** Capitalism makes social relationships between people look like relationships between things. You don't see the worker behind the iPhone. You see a sleek object. The human work is hidden.
- **The prediction.** Capitalism would concentrate wealth in fewer hands, immiserate the working class, and eventually collapse from its own contradictions. The proletariat, having nothing left to lose, would rise. Communism, a stateless and classless society, would follow.

## The key texts

- **The Communist Manifesto (1848)**, written with Engels. Short, hot, a political pamphlet rather than a treatise. Published the year Europe erupted in revolutions, none of which succeeded.
- **Das Kapital, Volume 1 (1867)**. The big one. Dense, exhaustive, the systematic analysis of how capitalism actually worked. Volumes 2 and 3 were assembled by Engels from Marx's notes after Marx died.

## The successors who built states

- **Vladimir Lenin** led the Russian Revolution in **1917** and built the Soviet Union. He adapted Marxism for a country that had barely industrialized, by arguing that a "vanguard party" could push history forward instead of waiting for the proletariat to wake up.
- **Mao Zedong** took power in China in **1949**. His variant emphasized the peasantry over the urban working class, because China barely had an urban working class.
- **Fidel Castro** took Cuba in **1959**.
- **Pol Pot** took Cambodia in **1975** and presided over the deaths of roughly a quarter of the population in his attempt to build agrarian communism.
- The **Soviet collapse in 1989-1991** ended Marxism's run as a serious political program in most of the world. China and Vietnam stayed nominally Marxist while embracing market economics. North Korea and Cuba kept the name. That was about it.

## Where Marx was right and where he was wrong

- **Right about rising inequality.** The wealth concentration he predicted is now empirically documented across the West. **Thomas Piketty's** *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013) revived the topic with two centuries of tax data and is essentially a non-Marxist confirmation of the inequality trend Marx flagged.
- **Right about capitalism's tendency to concentrate.** Markets do tend toward monopoly without active counter-pressure. Antitrust law exists because this turned out to be true.
- **Right about globalization's instabilities.** The 2008 financial crisis was the kind of systemic shock he expected capitalism to produce.
- **Wrong about who would revolt.** The industrial proletariat in advanced economies got richer, not poorer, and turned out to be the least revolutionary class in history.
- **Wrong about planned economies.** Every attempt to centrally plan a complex economy failed catastrophically. The Soviet famines, the Chinese Great Leap Forward (1958-1962, around 30 million dead), the Cambodian collapse.
- **Wrong about capitalism's adaptability.** The welfare state, labor unions, and progressive taxation defanged most of capitalism's worst tendencies in the rich world without revolution.

## What's still alive

- The **critique of capitalism** as an analytical tool. Even non-Marxists use Marx's vocabulary on inequality, alienation, and concentration.
- **Labor movements** and trade union thought worldwide.
- **World-systems theory (Immanuel Wallerstein)**, which analyzes the global economy as a structured core-periphery system.
- **Marxist literary and cultural criticism**, which reads texts for the class interests they encode.
- **Dependency theory** in development economics, which explains why poor countries stay poor through their structural position in the global system.

## Why MBAs need to know this

- The critiques of business, inequality, and concentration that get aimed at you in school and after, especially in international politics, are mostly Marxist in lineage even when the speaker doesn't know it.
- The analytical lens is useful even if you reject the prescriptions. Asking "whose interests does this arrangement serve, and who is paying for them?" is a Marx-shaped question, and it cuts through a lot of corporate fog.
- A serious manager understands the strongest critique of the system they work inside. Marx is that critique.

## What to carry away

- Marxism is the most influential failed prediction in modern thought, and that combination (powerful diagnosis, wrong prescription) is exactly why it won't go away.
- The honest read: take the analytical tools, set aside the political program, and keep reading the critics. They are usually pointing at something real.
- In one sentence: Marxism is a 175-year-old critique of capitalism that misread how the system would end but called most of its pathologies correctly, and that tension is why it keeps coming back.
