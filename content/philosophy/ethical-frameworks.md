# Utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics: the three families MBAs argue with

## What normative ethics asks and why it matters

Normative ethics is the branch of philosophy that tries to answer the question "how should I act?" Three families of answers have dominated the conversation for the last 250 years: **utilitarianism** (judge actions by their outcomes), **deontology** (judge actions by whether they follow correct rules), and **virtue ethics** (judge actions by what kind of person they make you). They disagree fundamentally about where moral weight sits. Every real ethical argument at work, from a layoff decision to whether to ship a feature, is implicitly one of these three families trying to overrule the others. It matters because if you don't know which framework you're using, you'll lose the argument to someone who does.

## Utilitarianism: outcomes are what matter

- Founded by **Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832)**, an English legal reformer who wanted to put ethics on a scientific basis. His move: the right action is the one that produces the greatest sum of happiness across everyone affected. He called this the "principle of utility."
- Bentham was serious about quantifying happiness. He proposed a "felicific calculus" with seven dimensions (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent) and thought future legislators would use it to evaluate laws. He also designed a prison called the Panopticon, willed his body to be preserved as an "auto-icon" at University College London (it's still there in a glass case), and was 200 years ahead of his time on gay rights, women's suffrage, and animal welfare.
- **John Stuart Mill (1806-1873)** softened Bentham. Mill argued some pleasures are higher quality than others ("better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a pig satisfied") and added that individual liberty has a special place: society can only restrict your freedom to prevent harm to others. His essay *On Liberty* (1859) is still the cleanest defense of free speech and personal autonomy ever written.
- **Peter Singer (born 1946)**, the most influential living utilitarian, extended the framework hard. If suffering is what matters, the suffering of animals counts, which made him the philosophical father of the modern animal rights movement (*Animal Liberation*, 1975). And if distance doesn't matter morally, a child drowning in a pond five feet from you and a child dying of malaria 5,000 miles away should pull on you equally, which makes most middle-class Westerners morally negligent. His arguments helped seed the effective altruism movement.
- The criticisms.
  - **The minority-rights problem.** If five people get a huge benefit from torturing one person, utilitarianism seems to endorse the torture. The math wins, the rights lose. Most utilitarians try to dodge this by saying that rules protecting individuals produce better long-run utility, but the structural problem doesn't fully go away.
  - **Trolley problems.** The famous thought experiments (do you divert a runaway trolley to kill one person instead of five) show how quickly utilitarian math conflicts with most people's gut sense of what's allowed.
  - **The demandingness objection.** If you should always maximize utility, you should give away almost all your money, organs, and time to people poorer than you. Most utilitarians live nothing like this, which is either a problem for them or a problem for the theory.

## Deontology: rules are what matter

- The grandfather is **Immanuel Kant (1724-1804)**, a German philosopher who lived such a regimented life in Konigsberg that neighbors set their watches by his daily walk. He spent decades trying to ground morality in pure reason, independent of feelings or consequences.
- His core test is the **categorical imperative**. Most famous formulation: "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." Before you do something, ask whether you could coherently want everyone to do it. Lying fails the test because if everyone lied, the institution of language would collapse and lying would stop working, so the maxim contradicts itself.
- Second formulation, even more famous: "Treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means." Using people as instruments, full stop, is wrong. You can pay someone to do a job (they're a means, but also an end-in-themselves with their own consent and dignity), but you can't manipulate or coerce them.
- Kant's universe is one where consequences don't change the verdict. If you have to choose between telling a lie that saves a life and telling the truth that costs the life, Kant infamously said you tell the truth. Critics have been hammering this since 1797, when **Benjamin Constant** asked Kant about the murderer at the door looking for your friend hiding upstairs.
- Modern deontology is more flexible. **W.D. Ross** in the 1930s argued for "prima facie duties" that can be weighed against each other (keep promises, but break the promise if needed to save a life). **T.M. Scanlon's** contractualism (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998) reframes the test: act only on principles no one could reasonably reject.
- The criticisms.
  - **Rule-fetishism.** When following the rule causes catastrophe, refusing to bend it can look more like vanity than virtue.
  - **No way to settle conflicts between rules.** Kant's system gives you absolute duties that can collide, and the original framework has no rigorous way to choose.
  - **Cold.** Deontology can feel arid. It judges actions, not the texture of moral life. Bernard Williams accused both utilitarianism and Kantianism of having "one thought too many" when someone asks why you saved your drowning spouse instead of a stranger.

## Virtue ethics: character is what matters

- The original framework, predating the other two by over 2,000 years. **Aristotle's** *Nicomachean Ethics* (~340 BCE) is the foundational text. His move: ethics isn't a checklist of actions or a rulebook. It's about what kind of person you are.
- The aim is **eudaimonia**, often translated "flourishing" or "living well." It's not pleasure or duty. It's the active life of a human being whose capacities (reasoning, friendship, courage, justice, self-control) are exercised in their full form.
- A virtue is a **stable disposition to feel and act well**, sitting between two vices. Courage is the mean between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity sits between stinginess and wastefulness. The mean isn't a midpoint on a number line; it's a sensitivity to the situation. The courageous person knows when to fight and when to flee.
- You become virtuous by habituation. You aren't born brave; you become brave by doing brave things, badly at first, until the disposition gets baked in. This is why Aristotle thought ethics couldn't be taught from a book to a 20-year-old. You need years of practice and good role models.
- Virtue ethics nearly died as a serious framework in the modern era. **Elizabeth Anscombe**, a British philosopher, revived it in a 1958 paper called "Modern Moral Philosophy," arguing that both utilitarianism and Kantianism had become hollow, and that you couldn't have "morality" without something like the older virtue tradition.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre's** *After Virtue* (1981) extended the revival. He argued that modern moral language is in disrepair because we've inherited fragments of virtue ethics without the social context (communities, traditions, shared purposes) that gave them meaning. His diagnosis influenced communitarian political thought and the slow-living movement.
- The criticisms.
  - **Vagueness.** "What would the virtuous person do?" is a less actionable test than "maximize utility" or "follow the rule." Critics say it just kicks the question down the road.
  - **Cultural relativism risk.** Different societies admire different virtues. Without an outside standard, how do you criticize a culture's vices?
  - **Doesn't scale.** Virtue ethics evolved for small communities where everyone knew everyone. It struggles to give clear answers about strangers, institutions, and global problems.

## Real cases, three answers each

- **Whistleblowing on your employer.**
  - Utilitarian: weigh the harm of the wrongdoing against the harm to you, your colleagues, the company. If exposure produces more good than silence, blow the whistle.
  - Deontological: if you have a duty to truth and to people being harmed, you blow the whistle regardless of consequences. Loyalty to a company doesn't override duties to people.
  - Virtue: ask what the courageous, just, honest person would do. Usually they speak up, but only after exhausting reasonable internal channels, because part of virtue is practical wisdom (*phronesis*) about how to act.
- **Layoffs.**
  - Utilitarian: layoffs are justified if they produce more total welfare than the alternatives, after weighing the harm to those let go.
  - Deontological: the question is whether people are being treated as ends or as disposable means. Layoffs aren't necessarily wrong, but the manner (notice, severance, honesty) matters morally, not just operationally.
  - Virtue: what would a manager of good character do? They'd own the decision, look people in the eye, give them real notice and real help, and accept the moral cost instead of dressing it up in HR language.
- **AI ethics.**
  - Utilitarian: build whatever produces the most welfare across the affected population, weighing risk of catastrophic harm seriously. This is the framework behind effective altruism's interest in AI safety.
  - Deontological: don't deploy systems that violate consent, dignity, or autonomy, even if the aggregate benefit looks attractive. This frames the privacy and consent conversation in EU regulation.
  - Virtue: what habits of attention, honesty, and care does this technology cultivate or erode in its users and its builders? This frame is rarer in industry but increasingly common in academic AI ethics.

## Why an MBA needs all three

- Every framework breaks in characteristic ways. Pure utilitarianism approves of horrors as long as the math works. Pure deontology gets paralyzed when rules collide. Pure virtue ethics struggles to give crisp guidance in unfamiliar institutional contexts.
- The actual practice of moral reasoning at work is using all three as cross-checks. A decision that looks fine on utility but treats people as means should worry you. A decision that follows the rules but is one a person of bad character would happily make should worry you too.
- The people who get into the worst ethical trouble in business are usually those who don't know they have a framework. They default to one, get caught off-guard by a situation where it breaks, and confuse "this is uncomfortable for me" with "this is the right call."

## What to carry away

- Three families. Outcomes (utility), rules (deontology), character (virtue).
- Each one is strongest where the others are weakest. Use them as a triangulation, not as competitors picking a winner.
- The trolley problem isn't a trick question. It's a stress test for which framework you're running.
- In one sentence: every hard ethical decision is a fight between an outcomes-maximizer, a rule-follower, and a character-grower, and your job is to hear all three before you act.
