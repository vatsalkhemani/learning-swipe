# Liberalism (the actual philosophy, not the US political label)

## What is liberalism and why it matters

Liberalism is the political-philosophical tradition that built the modern West. It emerged in the 1600s and 1700s in response to wars of religion and absolute monarchy, and it argued that individuals have rights the state cannot violate, that government legitimacy comes from consent of the governed, and that the law should treat everyone equally. Most of the institutions we now take for granted (constitutional government, free press, independent courts, free markets, universal suffrage) are downstream of liberal arguments. It matters because liberalism is the implicit operating system of the international order (UN, WTO, IMF, the EU, the post-1945 Western alliance), and a lot of current global politics is the fight over whether that operating system holds.

## Important: this is not "liberal" in the American sense

- In American politics, "liberal" means roughly "left of center, pro welfare state, pro civil rights, pro regulated capitalism." That usage stuck after **Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal** in the 1930s and got cemented during the civil rights era.
- In the rest of the world, and in serious political philosophy, "liberal" means something closer to the original: individual rights, limited government, free markets, rule of law. In European politics a "Liberal Party" is usually center-right.
- The American word "libertarian" was invented in the mid-20th century precisely because "liberal" had been captured by the New Deal coalition, and free-market thinkers needed a new label.
- The confusion is real and runs constantly. The philosophical tradition this card is about is the broader one, of which both modern American "liberals" and "libertarians" are branches.

## How it started

- **John Locke (1632-1704)** is the foundational figure. English, lived through the English Civil War, the execution of Charles I, and the Glorious Revolution. In his *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) he argued that humans have natural rights to life, liberty, and property that exist before government, that government's only legitimate purpose is to protect those rights, and that citizens have the right to overthrow a government that violates them. Thomas Jefferson copy-pasted Locke into the **Declaration of Independence** in 1776, swapping "property" for "the pursuit of happiness."
- **Adam Smith (1723-1790)**, Scottish, gave liberalism its economic arm. *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that markets, left mostly alone, would coordinate human activity better than any planner. The "invisible hand" line is from this book. He was a moral philosopher first and an economist second, and his earlier book *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) is a serious work on ethics that most readers of *Wealth* skip.
- **John Stuart Mill (1806-1873)**, English, the great Victorian liberal. *On Liberty* (1859) gave the tradition its sharpest defense of individual freedom. His **harm principle** said the only legitimate reason for restricting someone's behavior was to prevent harm to others. He was also a utilitarian, a feminist (rare for the period), and a Member of Parliament.
- **Isaiah Berlin (1909-1997)**, born in Riga, settled at Oxford. His essay **"Two Concepts of Liberty"** (1958) split freedom into two kinds. **Negative liberty** is freedom from interference (nobody's stopping you). **Positive liberty** is freedom to actually do things (you have the resources, education, and capability to act). The distinction matters because most modern political disagreements are arguments over which kind counts more.
- **John Rawls (1921-2002)** rebuilt liberalism for the 20th century. *A Theory of Justice* (1971) is the most influential work of political philosophy of the last hundred years. His central tool was the **original position** behind a **veil of ignorance**: imagine designing society without knowing your race, gender, talents, or wealth. What rules would you accept? Rawls argued you'd accept basic liberties for all, and inequalities only if they benefited the worst-off. This became the philosophical foundation for modern welfare-state liberalism.

## The core commitments

- **Individual liberty.** The individual is the primary unit, and their right to live as they choose is the starting point.
- **Limited government.** State power has to be constrained by law, divided across branches, and accountable to citizens. The state is necessary but dangerous.
- **Rule of law.** Laws apply equally to everyone, are knowable in advance, and bind the government as well as the governed.
- **Equality before the law.** Whatever your birth, wealth, religion, or status, the law treats you the same.
- **Free markets, mostly.** Private property and voluntary exchange as the default for organizing economic life. Liberals disagree fiercely on how much state intervention is acceptable.
- **Tolerance.** People are allowed to live, worship, and think differently. The state doesn't enforce a single conception of the good life.
- **Separation of religion and state.** Born from the European wars of religion, the conviction that political authority and religious authority must be kept apart.

## How it differs from its rivals

- **Socialism** prioritizes economic equality over individual property rights and is willing to use the state to enforce it. Liberalism prioritizes liberty and accepts more inequality as a result.
- **Conservatism** prioritizes tradition, community, and inherited institutions over individual choice. A liberal asks "what do I freely choose?" A conservative asks "what have we built together that I should not lightly tear up?"
- **Marxism** sees liberal rights as a mask for class power. The freedom of speech is great if you own the printing press. Liberals respond that imperfect rights are still better than no rights.

## The internal split

- **Classical liberalism** = small state plus free markets plus individual rights. Closer to what Locke and Smith actually wrote. Modern descendants include **Friedrich Hayek (1899-1992)** and **Milton Friedman (1912-2006)**.
- **Modern liberalism** (American sense) = welfare state plus regulated markets plus expanded civil rights. The New Deal lineage, the Rawlsian frame.
- **Libertarianism** = the market-fundamentalist branch, sharper than classical liberalism. **Robert Nozick's** *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) was a direct response to Rawls arguing that any redistribution beyond a minimal night-watchman state was theft.

## The serious critiques

- **Communitarians** (**Alasdair MacIntyre**, **Michael Sandel**) argue liberalism over-individualizes humans. We are not free-floating choosers. We are embedded in families, communities, and traditions, and a political philosophy that ignores that produces lonely, atomized societies.
- **Marxists** argue liberal rights mask class power. Equality before the law is hollow if one party has lawyers and the other does not.
- **Postmodernists** (**Foucault**, **Derrida**) argue liberal universalism is Western particularism in disguise. The "universal human" of liberal theory looks suspiciously like a 19th-century European man.
- **Feminists and post-colonial thinkers** point out that the "individual" of liberal theory was historically a propertied white man, and the project of extending it to everyone else has been slow, contested, and incomplete.

## Why this matters

- Liberalism is the dominant political philosophy of the West and the implicit framework for most international institutions.
- The current global politics fight (rise of illiberal democracy in Hungary, Turkey, India under Modi, China's authoritarian model, the populist right in the US and Europe) is largely a contest over whether the liberal order is durable or already cracking.
- Understanding liberalism is understanding the default frame against which everything else is positioned. You cannot read modern politics or international affairs intelligently without it.

## What to carry away

- The word means three different things in three different rooms (US politics, European politics, political philosophy). Always check which room you are in.
- The deepest liberal move: nobody has a monopoly on truth, so the state's job is to protect the space in which people can live, argue, and choose differently, not to enforce one answer.
- The honest read: liberalism's universalist claims are contested, its rights are unevenly enforced, and its critics often have a point. None of that means the alternatives have worked better.
- In one sentence: liberalism is the 350-year-old tradition that built the modern West around individual rights, limited government, and rule of law, and the current crisis is about whether that frame survives the 21st century.
