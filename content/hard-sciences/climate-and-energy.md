# Climate and energy: the real tradeoffs between solar, nuclear, and oil

## What this is and why it matters

Climate change is settled science, the energy transition is not. Almost every argument in the public conversation conflates these two. Whether human emissions are warming the planet is no longer in serious scientific dispute. What to do about it (which energy sources to build, at what speed, who pays, who carries the risk) is a real tradeoff conversation with no easy answer. It matters because energy is the input to civilization, and getting this transition wrong in either direction (too fast and you crash economies, too slow and you cook the planet) compounds for centuries.

## Why the climate science is settled

- The **greenhouse effect** has been understood since **John Tyndall** measured it in 1859. Some gases (CO2, methane, water vapor) trap infrared radiation. More of them in the atmosphere means more trapped heat. This is not a model, it's a lab measurement.
- **Svante Arrhenius**, the Swedish chemist, calculated in 1896 that doubling atmospheric CO2 would warm the planet by roughly 5°C. Modern climate models give numbers in the same ballpark (2.5-4°C). A 19th-century calculation that holds up is a good sign.
- **Ice cores** drilled from Antarctica and Greenland record 800,000 years of atmospheric composition trapped in air bubbles. Pre-industrial CO2 hovered around 280 parts per million through every ice age and warm period. It is now 425 ppm and rising about 2-3 ppm per year. This is well outside the natural range.
- The **IPCC** (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change), set up in 1988, synthesizes thousands of peer-reviewed papers every few years. The 2021 AR6 report stated with "unequivocal" confidence that humans have warmed the climate, and that warming is happening faster than any episode in the last 2,000 years.
- Where there is real scientific debate: the exact magnitude of feedback loops (clouds, permafrost methane, ice albedo), regional impacts, the speed of tipping points. The basic mechanism and direction are not debated by anyone publishing in the field.

## The energy trilemma

Every energy source has to be judged on three axes, and no source is good on all three.

- **Low-carbon.** Does it emit greenhouse gases per unit energy produced?
- **Affordable.** What does it cost to build, operate, and pay for over its lifetime?
- **Reliable.** Can you turn it on when you need it, including at 3 a.m. in February when demand spikes and the wind isn't blowing?

The honest framing is that you usually get two of three. Solar and wind are clean and increasingly cheap but not reliable. Nuclear is clean and reliable but expensive. Coal, oil, and gas are cheap and reliable but not clean. The transition is the work of finding combinations that hit all three at scale.

## Solar and wind

- **The cost story is the big one.** Solar photovoltaic cost has dropped about **90% from 2010 to 2024**, faster than almost anyone predicted. Wind has dropped roughly 70% over the same period. In sunny places, solar is now the cheapest electricity humans have ever generated.
- **The intermittency story is the other big one.** Solar produces nothing at night and not much on cloudy days. Wind produces nothing on calm days. A grid running on 100% solar and wind needs either massive battery storage, massive transmission moving power across regions, or backup generation that sits idle most of the time. All three add cost.
- **Battery storage** has fallen about 90% in cost since 2010 too, mostly thanks to lithium-ion scaling from electric cars. But grid-scale storage for days or weeks of low wind and sun is still expensive. **Germany's Dunkelflaute** ("dark doldrums") in winter, when the country can go days with almost no wind or solar output, is the standard example of why this matters.
- **Land use.** Utility-scale solar needs roughly 5-10 acres per megawatt. Wind farms spread across much more land but only use a small fraction of it directly. Public opposition to siting (NIMBY) is a serious constraint, especially for transmission lines.
- **Mineral supply chains.** Solar panels, wind turbines, and batteries need lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and rare earths. **China** controls 60-90% of refining for most of these. This is the new oil-equivalent strategic dependency.

## Nuclear

- A single 1 gigawatt nuclear reactor produces about **1,000 times the energy density** of a solar farm at the same physical footprint. It runs 24/7 at about 90% capacity factor, the highest of any source.
- **The build cost is the issue.** Western nuclear construction has become slow and expensive. The **Vogtle 3 and 4** reactors in Georgia, the only new US nuclear in 30 years, cost over $30 billion and took 15 years. **France's Flamanville 3** was 12 years late and 4x over budget.
- **South Korea, China, and the UAE** have built nuclear on time and on budget recently. **South Korea's APR-1400** design, built in the UAE's Barakah plant, came in roughly on schedule at competitive cost. The problem in the West is not the technology, it's the regulatory and construction environment.
- **Safety statistics.** Counting deaths per unit energy produced, including Chernobyl and Fukushima, nuclear is one of the safest energy sources, comparable to wind and solar, and far safer than coal or natural gas. The **Fukushima** disaster in 2011 killed one confirmed person from radiation. The earthquake and tsunami it followed killed over 18,000. Public perception of nuclear risk is wildly disconnected from the data.
- **Waste.** Spent fuel is real but it's solid, contained, and small in volume. The US has produced about 90,000 metric tons of spent fuel over 60 years, all of it stored on reactor sites. The **Yucca Mountain** repository was scientifically approved and politically killed.
- **Small modular reactors (SMRs)** are the current bet. Smaller, factory-built reactors that could in theory be cheaper and faster to deploy. **NuScale**, **TerraPower** (Bill Gates), **X-energy**, and several others are racing. None operating at commercial scale yet.

## Oil and natural gas

- **Hydrocarbons still provide roughly 80% of global primary energy.** Despite 20 years of renewable scale-up, this share has barely moved because total energy demand keeps growing.
- **Why they're so hard to displace.** Energy density (a gallon of gasoline carries the energy of about 130 lithium-ion battery cells of equivalent weight), fungibility (you can ship oil anywhere on a tanker), reliability (it sits in a tank until you need it), and a century of built infrastructure.
- **Natural gas** burns roughly half as carbon-intense as coal per unit of energy. The US shift from coal to gas via fracking in the 2010s did more to reduce American emissions than any policy.
- **Methane leakage** is the gas-industry caveat. Methane is 80x more potent than CO2 as a greenhouse gas over 20 years. If more than about 3% of gas leaks during production and transport, the climate benefit over coal evaporates. Real leak rates are contested and probably higher in some regions than reported.
- **The geopolitical layer.** The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, and the resulting European energy crisis, was a reminder that energy and geopolitics are inseparable. Countries that need to import energy carry strategic risk.

## How long transitions actually take

- Wood to coal took roughly **50 years** to become dominant globally.
- Coal to oil took another **50 years**.
- The **Vaclav Smil** (energy historian, Bill Gates's favorite author on this) point: energy transitions are slow not because of technology but because of capital stock. Power plants last 40 years. Cars last 15. Buildings last 80. You can't replace infrastructure faster than it depreciates without writing off trillions of dollars.
- The current transition is moving faster than past ones, but probably not fast enough to keep warming below 1.5°C, the **Paris Agreement** stretch target. 2°C is plausible. 2.5-3°C is the trajectory if current policies continue without acceleration.

## The China dynamic

- China builds more solar capacity in a single year than the rest of the world combined. It also burns more coal than the rest of the world combined, and is still building coal plants.
- China dominates the manufacturing of solar panels (~85% of global supply), batteries (~75%), and rare earth refining (~85%). The Inflation Reduction Act in the US (2022) and the European Green Deal are largely about not being permanently dependent on Chinese supply chains for the energy transition.
- The decarbonization math doesn't work without China cutting emissions, and China's cuts depend on continued economic growth. This is the global Prisoner's Dilemma of the century.

## What people get wrong

- **"Renewables can do it alone."** They can't yet at most latitudes, because of intermittency. They might in 20 years with cheap enough storage and overbuilt capacity.
- **"Nuclear is too dangerous."** The data says the opposite. Public fear is doing real damage to decarbonization.
- **"Oil companies are blocking the transition."** Some are, but the deeper truth is that 8 billion people want cheap, reliable energy, and the world's energy infrastructure is enormous. Both demand and supply inertia are the bigger forces.
- **"Climate change is fake."** It's not. The argument worth having is about costs and tradeoffs of responses, not about the underlying science.

## What to carry away

- The science of warming is settled. The policy of energy is not.
- Every energy source has tradeoffs across clean, cheap, and reliable. No source wins all three.
- Solar and wind are now the cheapest electricity ever, but they need storage and transmission to displace baseload. Nuclear works but the West has forgotten how to build it. Oil and gas are sticky because they're dense, portable, and stored capital we already paid for.
- Realistic decarbonization is "all of the above" with different mixes by region. Anyone telling you it's one source or none is selling you something.
- The pace is constrained by capital stock turnover, not by technology. Patience and urgency at the same time.
