# Climate models and the feedback loops that scare scientists

## What climate models are and why this matters

A climate model is a simulation of Earth's physical systems, run on a supercomputer, that lets scientists test how the planet responds to different conditions. The headlines focus on the temperature trajectory: roughly 1.2°C of warming above pre-industrial levels already, possibly 2.5 to 3°C by 2100 on the current trajectory. Those numbers are sobering but they hide the more important story. The thing scientists worry about most isn't the smooth temperature curve. It's the **feedback loops**, the parts of the system where a small change in one place triggers a much larger change somewhere else. Some of those loops, once they start, may not be reversible on any human timescale. That's where the real risk lives, and it's the part of climate science that doesn't translate well into a thermometer reading.

## How climate models actually work

- A climate model is a system of **thousands of equations** representing physical processes: the atmosphere, oceans, land surfaces, ice sheets, vegetation, and the carbon cycle. The earth is sliced into a 3D grid of cells, typically tens to hundreds of kilometers wide and stacked vertically through the atmosphere and oceans.
- For each grid cell, the model calculates how energy, water, and gases move between it and its neighbors, time step by time step. A typical century-long simulation requires weeks of supercomputer time.
- **Syukuro Manabe** at Princeton built the first credible 3D atmospheric climate model in the **1960s and 70s**. His model predicted, decades in advance, that doubling CO2 would raise global temperatures by 2 to 3°C, which is right in the range modern models still produce. He won the **2021 Nobel Prize in Physics** along with **Klaus Hasselmann** and **Giorgio Parisi**.
- The 1980s climate projections, including **James Hansen's 1988 testimony to Congress**, turned out to be remarkably close to what actually happened over the next 35 years. The models are not perfect, but their core predictions have held up.

## The IPCC and where the consensus actually sits

- The **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)** was founded in **1988** by the UN. It does not do its own research. It synthesizes thousands of peer-reviewed climate studies into periodic assessment reports.
- The current scientific consensus, expressed in the IPCC's **AR6 report (2021-2023)**:
  - Humans have caused about **1.2°C of warming** above the pre-industrial baseline (roughly 1850-1900).
  - The warming is unequivocally human-caused, primarily from CO2 emissions from burning fossil fuels.
  - On current policies the world is headed toward roughly **2.5 to 3°C of warming by 2100**.
  - **1.5°C** was the aspirational Paris Agreement target. We will likely cross it in the early 2030s. **2°C** was the harder limit. Crossing it is still possible to avoid but requires faster cuts than current policy delivers.

## The feedback loops that matter

Feedbacks are the part of the system that can turn a controlled change into a runaway one. A **positive feedback** amplifies warming. A **negative feedback** dampens it. Most of the major identified climate feedbacks are positive.

- **Ice-albedo feedback.** Ice and snow are bright and reflect sunlight back to space. Open ocean and bare land are dark and absorb it. As Arctic sea ice melts, more solar energy gets absorbed, which warms the region further, which melts more ice. This is the cleanest example of a positive feedback. Arctic warming is happening **3 to 4 times faster than the global average**.
- **Permafrost methane.** The Arctic's frozen soil contains roughly **1,500 billion tons of carbon**, twice the carbon currently in the atmosphere. As it thaws, microbes break down the organic matter and release CO2 and methane. **Methane is roughly 25 times more potent than CO2 over a 100-year timescale, and about 80 times more potent over 20 years.** How fast this carbon releases is one of the biggest uncertainties in climate modeling.
- **Amazon dieback.** The Amazon rainforest currently absorbs more carbon than it emits. As warming reduces rainfall and droughts intensify, and deforestation continues, the system can flip. Recent papers suggest the eastern Amazon is already a net carbon source. A full dieback would convert the world's largest carbon sink into a major emitter.
- **AMOC slowdown.** The **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation** is the system of currents (including the Gulf Stream) that pulls warm water north and cold water south across the Atlantic. It's been slowing, measurably, since at least the 1950s. A full shutdown would dramatically cool northwestern Europe (counterintuitively, in a warming world), shift monsoons in Africa and South America, and raise sea levels along the US east coast. Recent papers have raised concerns it may be closer to a tipping point than previously thought, though the timing is debated.
- **Cloud feedbacks.** Clouds are the largest source of uncertainty in climate models. Low clouds tend to cool by reflecting sunlight. High clouds tend to warm by trapping heat. Whether a warmer world produces net more cooling clouds or more warming clouds is still being worked out. The latest models lean toward warming clouds, which means previous models may have understated future warming.

## Tipping points

- A 2008 paper by **Tim Lenton** and colleagues, updated in 2022, identified **16 climate tipping elements**. These are parts of the climate system that, once pushed past a threshold, change state in ways that don't reverse on human timescales.
- The 2022 update found that **5 of the 16 may already be near or past their tipping points** at the current 1.2°C of warming. These include the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, low-latitude coral reefs, the boreal permafrost, and the Barents Sea ice.
- A tipping point is not the same as a cliff edge. Crossing one doesn't trigger immediate collapse. It commits the system to a slow but unstoppable change over decades to centuries. The Greenland ice sheet, for instance, could be committed to full melt long before it actually finishes melting.

## What climate models still don't capture well

- **Clouds and aerosols** are the largest source of model uncertainty.
- **Regional precipitation** is harder to predict than global temperature. Where exactly the droughts and floods land is messy.
- **The exact thresholds** for tipping points are not well constrained. Scientists are confident the points exist; they're not confident where they sit.
- **Compound risks** when multiple stressors combine, like a heat wave plus a drought plus a wildfire, are hard to model from first principles.

## Why this matters more than the temperature average

- The temperature average is just the smoothed top-line number. The damage happens at the extremes and at the tipping points.
- **Food and water security** are the through line. A 2°C world doesn't just have 2°C warmer everything. It has more intense droughts in some places, harder rainfall in others, shifting monsoons, collapsing fisheries, and large changes in where crops will grow.
- Most catastrophic scenarios in the climate literature don't come from gradual warming. They come from feedbacks that accelerate the warming or from tipping points that lock in change.

## What to carry away

- Climate models are physics-based simulations of Earth. They've been remarkably accurate where they've been tested. They are imperfect on regional detail and exact tipping points.
- The temperature curve is the simple version of the story. The feedback loops are the actual story.
- Once a tipping point is crossed, the change is mostly locked in. The question isn't whether the system reverses. It's whether you stop pushing it past more thresholds.
- The science is settled on the basics. The uncertainty is in the timing and magnitude of feedbacks, which is exactly where the policy decisions need to be made.
