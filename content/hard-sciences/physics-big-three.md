# Relativity, quantum mechanics, entropy: the three physics ideas you should know cold

## What these three are and why they matter together

Modern physics rests on three pillars. **Relativity** rewrote space and time. **Quantum mechanics** rewrote what matter is. **Entropy** explains why anything happens at all. They were each weird when proposed, and 100 years later they're still weird, but they run the universe and they run your life. GPS satellites would drift miles per day without relativity corrections. Every transistor in your phone is a quantum device. Entropy is why your coffee cools, why you age, and why you can remember yesterday but not tomorrow. You don't need the math to use these ideas. You need the pictures.

## Relativity: space and time are not what you thought

- **Albert Einstein** published special relativity in 1905, during his "miracle year," while working as a patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland. He was 26. Same year he also published three other historic papers including the one on the photoelectric effect that won him the Nobel.
- The setup: in the 19th century, physicists believed light traveled through a medium called the **luminiferous ether**. The famous **Michelson-Morley experiment** in 1887 tried to detect Earth's motion through the ether and got nothing. Light moved the same speed in every direction. This was a crisis.
- Einstein's answer was to throw out the ether and accept that the speed of light is constant for every observer, no matter how they're moving. Once you swallow that, the rest cascades.
- **Simultaneity is relative.** Two events that look simultaneous to you may not look simultaneous to someone moving past you. There is no universal "now."
- **Time dilates.** Clocks moving relative to you tick slower. Confirmed in the 1971 **Hafele-Keating experiment** where atomic clocks flown around the world disagreed with stationary ones by hundreds of nanoseconds, exactly as predicted.
- **Length contracts.** Moving objects are shorter along the direction of motion. Real, just unnoticeable at human speeds.
- **E=mc².** Mass and energy are convertible. A small amount of mass equals a huge amount of energy because c² is a huge number. This is what powers the sun (fusion) and atomic bombs (fission).

### General relativity

- Einstein finished **general relativity** in 1915, ten years later. Special relativity handled constant-speed motion. General handled acceleration and gravity.
- The central idea: **gravity is not a force, it's the geometry of spacetime.** Mass curves spacetime, and things follow the straightest available path through that curved geometry. The Earth orbits the sun for the same reason a ball rolls toward a dip in a trampoline.
- **First confirmation:** Einstein predicted general relativity would explain a tiny anomaly in **Mercury's orbit** that Newton's laws couldn't, the precession of Mercury's perihelion. It did, exactly.
- **Second confirmation:** a 1919 solar eclipse expedition led by **Arthur Eddington** measured starlight bending around the sun, exactly as general relativity predicted. The *New York Times* headline: "Lights All Askew in the Heavens." Einstein became the first global science celebrity overnight.
- **Modern confirmations:** GPS satellites correct for both special (their motion) and general (lower gravity at altitude) relativity, totaling about 38 microseconds per day. Without those corrections GPS would be useless within hours. In 2015 LIGO directly detected **gravitational waves** from two merging black holes, a prediction Einstein made in 1916 and didn't think we'd ever measure.

## Quantum mechanics: the universe is granular and weird

- The crack opened in **1900** when **Max Planck**, trying to fix a problem with how hot objects emit light, proposed that energy comes in discrete packets called quanta. He called the assumption "an act of desperation." He was right anyway.
- **Einstein** extended it in 1905 (yes, same year) by showing the **photoelectric effect** could be explained if light itself came in particle-like packets called photons. This won him the Nobel in 1921.
- **Niels Bohr** built the first quantum model of the atom in 1913. Electrons live on discrete energy levels and jump between them. He ran the school in Copenhagen where most of quantum mechanics was hammered out in the 1920s.
- **Werner Heisenberg** wrote down matrix mechanics in 1925, age 23, while recovering from hay fever on the island of Helgoland. He proved his **uncertainty principle** two years later: you cannot simultaneously know a particle's exact position and exact momentum. It's not a measurement problem, it's the structure of reality.
- **Erwin Schrödinger** wrote his equation in 1926 from a ski cabin in the Swiss Alps with a mistress whose identity historians still debate. His wave equation gave the same answers as Heisenberg's matrices, and was easier to use. Combined with **Max Born's** interpretation, the wave function gives you probabilities of where you'll find a particle.
- **Wave-particle duality.** Electrons, photons, and even atoms behave as waves when not observed and as particles when measured. The **double-slit experiment**, especially the single-electron version run in 1989, is the cleanest demonstration. The textbook reaction is supposed to be acceptance, but if you don't find it disturbing you're not paying attention.
- **Einstein hated it.** Not the math, the interpretation. He believed the universe was deterministic underneath. "God does not play dice," he wrote to Born in 1926. Bohr's reply: "Stop telling God what to do." Einstein and Bohr argued about this for decades. The 1964 **Bell's theorem** and subsequent experiments (especially **Alain Aspect's** 1982 work, Nobel 2022) showed Einstein was wrong. The universe is genuinely probabilistic at small scales.
- **Modern applications.** Lasers, transistors, MRI machines, solar panels, LEDs, the entire semiconductor industry. Quantum computing is the next layer, using entanglement and superposition to compute in ways classical machines can't.

## Entropy and the second law of thermodynamics

- **Sadi Carnot**, a French engineer, started this in 1824 by analyzing steam engines. **Rudolf Clausius** named the concept entropy in 1865 and stated the second law: in an isolated system, entropy always increases.
- **Entropy** is, loosely, a measure of how disordered or spread out a system is. More precisely (this is **Ludwig Boltzmann's** 1877 statistical interpretation) it counts the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same macroscopically. There are vastly more disordered arrangements than ordered ones, so disorder is overwhelmingly more likely.
- A broken egg has many more arrangements of yolk and shell than an unbroken egg. Eggs break easily and don't spontaneously reassemble not because reassembly is impossible but because the odds are unfathomable.
- **Why time has a direction.** The fundamental laws of physics are mostly time-symmetric. Run a movie of two billiard balls colliding backward, it still looks like physics. Run a movie of an egg breaking backward, it looks wrong. The asymmetry is entropy. The early universe was in a remarkably low-entropy state, and the universe has been "running down" ever since. Time's arrow points from low entropy to high entropy.
- **Boltzmann** drilled the statistical foundation so hard he was attacked for it by the physics establishment and died by suicide in 1906. His equation, S = k log W, is engraved on his tomb in Vienna.
- **Why this scales out.** Entropy isn't only about gases and engines. **Claude Shannon** in 1948 used the exact same math to define information entropy, the foundation of all digital communication and compression. **Erwin Schrödinger** in his 1944 book *What Is Life?* argued biology runs on local entropy reductions paid for by exporting entropy outward (we eat low-entropy food and excrete high-entropy waste and heat). Cosmologists use entropy to track the long-term fate of the universe (the "heat death" scenario is a maximum-entropy endpoint).

## Why these three matter

- **Relativity** runs at the cosmic and high-speed scale. Without it: no GPS, no understanding of black holes, no cosmology.
- **Quantum mechanics** runs at the atomic and subatomic scale. Without it: no transistors, no lasers, no chemistry as we understand it, no modern medicine.
- **Entropy** runs at every scale. Without it: no explanation for why time moves forward, why machines wear out, why information theory works, or why life is locally improbable but possible.
- The unsolved frontier is reconciling relativity (which is smooth and continuous) with quantum mechanics (which is granular and probabilistic). The candidate frameworks (string theory, loop quantum gravity) are 50 years old and still unverified. Whoever cracks it gets the next Einstein-level slot.

## What to carry away

- Space and time aren't absolute. They're geometry, and the geometry bends.
- Reality at small scales isn't deterministic and isn't intuitive. Get used to probabilities.
- Entropy is why time moves forward, why your coffee cools, and why life is a local exception that has to be paid for.
- The phones in your pocket and the GPS in your car and the structure of your body all depend on these three. Even if the math is beyond you, the pictures aren't.
