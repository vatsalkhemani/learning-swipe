# DNA and CRISPR: the genetic code and how we learned to edit it

## What is DNA and why it matters

DNA is the chemical that stores the instructions for building and running every living thing. Every cell in your body carries the same copy, written in a four-letter alphabet, folded into a structure so elegant it tells you how it copies itself just by looking at it. For most of human history we knew nothing about it. In the last 70 years we figured out its shape, read the whole human version, and then learned to edit it letter by letter. The pace from "we have no idea" to "we can rewrite a gene that causes sickle cell disease" is one of the fastest knowledge gains in any field, ever. That is why it matters. The tools that came out of this story will reshape medicine, agriculture, and the ethics of what counts as a "natural" human in the next 30 years.

## How we figured out the structure

- **DNA's double helix structure was solved in 1953** by **James Watson** and **Francis Crick** at Cambridge, building on X-ray diffraction images taken by **Rosalind Franklin** at King's College London. Franklin's Photo 51 was the critical evidence. Watson and Crick saw it without her clear permission. She died of ovarian cancer in 1958 at age 37, before the Nobel was awarded in 1962.
- The structure itself is two strands wound around each other, like a twisted ladder. The rungs are pairs of four chemical bases: **adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T)**, and **guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C)**. That pairing rule is why DNA can copy itself faithfully. Unzip the strands and each side becomes a template for a new partner.
- The human genome has about **3 billion base pairs** packed into 23 chromosomes. Stretched out, the DNA in one cell would be about 2 meters long. You have about 30 trillion cells.

## Genes, the genome, and what actually codes for things

- A **gene** is a stretch of DNA that codes for a specific protein or function. Humans have roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, fewer than a grain of rice.
- Only about **1.5% of human DNA codes for proteins**. This protein-coding portion is called the **exome**. The rest used to be dismissed as "junk DNA." It is not junk. Much of it regulates when and how genes turn on.
- The **Human Genome Project** ran from **1990 to 2003**, cost about **$3 billion**, and gave us the first full read of human DNA. A full sequence today costs under $200 and takes a day. That cost crash, faster than Moore's Law, is what made everything downstream possible.

## CRISPR-Cas9: how editing works

- **CRISPR** stands for Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats. It is a bacterial immune system. Bacteria store snippets of virus DNA from past infections, then use those snippets to recognize and cut the virus next time.
- **Jennifer Doudna** at UC Berkeley and **Emmanuelle Charpentier** then at Umea published the breakthrough paper in **2012**, showing the system could be programmed to cut any DNA sequence in any organism. They won the **2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**, the first all-women science Nobel.
- How it works in practice: you design a short **guide RNA** matching the DNA sequence you want to edit. The guide RNA leads the **Cas9** enzyme to that exact spot. Cas9 cuts both DNA strands. The cell's repair machinery patches the cut, and you can use that repair process to disable a gene, insert a new one, or correct a single letter.
- It is cheap, fast, and works in essentially any organism. A grad student can edit a gene for a few hundred dollars in an afternoon. Before CRISPR, gene editing was slow, expensive, and only worked in a few species.

## What it is being used for

- **Sickle cell disease cure.** The FDA approved **Casgevy** in **December 2023**, the first CRISPR therapy. It edits a patient's own bone marrow cells to switch a backup hemoglobin gene back on. List price: about $2.2 million per patient. Trials have shown durable remission.
- **Cancer immunotherapies.** CRISPR is being used to engineer a patient's T-cells to recognize and attack their tumors. Several CAR-T variants are in late-stage trials.
- **Agriculture.** Drought-resistant crops, longer-shelf-life mushrooms, hornless cattle. The regulatory rules differ by country. The US treats most CRISPR-edited crops the same as conventional breeding. The EU treats them like GMOs.
- **Other diseases in the pipeline.** Beta thalassemia, hereditary blindness, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, hereditary high cholesterol. Trials are ongoing.

## The ethics crisis

- In **November 2018**, Chinese scientist **He Jiankui** announced he had used CRISPR to edit the embryos of twin girls, **Lulu and Nana**, to make them resistant to HIV. The edits were poorly done. The babies were born. The scientific community condemned it almost universally. He was sentenced to three years in prison in China.
- The line that case crossed was **germline editing**. There are two kinds of edits:
  - **Somatic editing** changes the cells of one person and dies with them. Casgevy is somatic.
  - **Germline editing** changes egg, sperm, or embryo DNA, so the change passes to every descendant. The He Jiankui twins are germline.
- The "designer babies" question is mostly about germline editing for non-medical traits like height, eye color, or theorized intelligence gains. The science is not there yet for most traits. The ethics is not settled for any.
- Most countries ban or heavily restrict germline editing in humans. There is broad scientific consensus that we should not deploy it until we understand it much better, but the technical barrier keeps dropping.

## Where it goes from here

- **Base editing and prime editing**, developed by **David Liu** at the Broad Institute, are newer versions that can change single letters of DNA without cutting both strands. Safer and more precise. Already in trials.
- mRNA delivery, lipid nanoparticles, and AI-designed guide RNAs are making CRISPR cheaper and more targeted every year.
- The pipeline of monogenic diseases, those caused by a single broken gene, is the near-term prize. Maybe a few hundred conditions where one edit could be curative.
- The harder questions are coming for polygenic traits, the ethics of enhancement, and how we govern a technology where the means of production keeps getting smaller.

## What to carry away

- DNA is a four-letter chemical instruction book. We solved its shape in 1953, read the human copy by 2003, and learned to edit it in 2012.
- CRISPR works because bacteria already invented it. We borrowed their immune system and pointed it at the problems we cared about.
- The first cures are real. The first ethics violations are real. Both happened within five years of each other. That is the rhythm to expect.
