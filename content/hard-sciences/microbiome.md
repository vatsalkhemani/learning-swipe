# The microbiome: the trillions of microbes that run your body

## What the microbiome is and why it matters

Your body is home to about **38 trillion microbial cells**, roughly matching the **30 trillion human cells** that make up you. Most of them live in your gut. They digest food you can't digest on your own, train your immune system, make neurotransmitters that affect your mood, and protect you from invaders. For most of medical history we thought of bacteria as something that made you sick. In the last 20 years it became clear that the right ones, in the right balance, are doing most of the unglamorous work that keeps you alive. The field is young, the science is still mostly correlational, and the supplement industry has rushed ahead of the data. But the underlying shift is real. Your gut is an organ, and you have been ignoring it.

## What's actually in there

- The **gut microbiome** contains roughly **1,000+ bacterial species**, plus viruses, fungi, and archaea. The collective genome (the **metagenome**) has about 100 to 150 times more genes than your own.
- Most of the action happens in the **large intestine**. Different sections of the gut, plus the mouth, skin, lungs, and vagina, each host their own distinct microbial communities. The gut is the densest and the most studied.
- Two bacterial families, **Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes**, dominate most healthy adult guts. The ratios shift with diet, age, geography, and disease.
- Your microbiome is partly inherited (from your mother during birth and breastfeeding), partly built by what you eat and what antibiotics you've taken, and partly shaped by where you live and who you live with. Identical twins raised apart have noticeably different microbiomes.

## What it actually does for you

- **Digestion.** Humans cannot digest **dietary fiber** on our own. Gut microbes ferment it and produce **short-chain fatty acids** (acetate, propionate, butyrate). Butyrate is the main fuel for the cells lining your colon. Without it, gut lining health degrades.
- **Immune training.** About **70% of your immune system tissue is in or near the gut**. Microbes teach immune cells what's a threat and what's not. Kids raised in extremely sterile environments have higher rates of allergies and autoimmune disease, the leading explanation for the "hygiene hypothesis."
- **Neurotransmitter production.** Roughly **90% of your serotonin is made in the gut**, not the brain. The microbiome is in two-way conversation with the brain through the **vagus nerve** and through chemicals it releases into the bloodstream. This is the **gut-brain axis**.
- **Vitamin production.** Gut bacteria make vitamin K and several B vitamins.
- **Pathogen defense.** A healthy microbiome physically crowds out harmful bacteria and produces antimicrobial compounds.

## When it goes wrong (dysbiosis)

When the microbial balance gets thrown off, the term is **dysbiosis**. It's been linked, though usually correlationally rather than causally, to a long list of conditions:

- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** and **inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)**, which includes Crohn's and ulcerative colitis.
- **Obesity and type 2 diabetes.** Lean and obese people have measurably different gut compositions. Mouse studies suggest the microbiome can causally affect weight gain. The human picture is less clean.
- **Depression and anxiety.** Strong correlational data. Causality unclear. Probiotic strains called "psychobiotics" are being studied.
- **Autism.** Controversial. People with autism often have distinctive gut compositions and high rates of GI issues. Whether this is cause, effect, or coincidence is not settled.
- **Clostridioides difficile (C. diff) infections.** This is the cleanest example. C. diff causes severe diarrhea, usually after antibiotics wipe out the protective gut community. Recurrent C. diff is hard to treat with more antibiotics.

## The big research push

- The **Human Microbiome Project** ran from **2007 to 2016**, funded by the NIH for about $215 million. It produced the first reference catalog of microbes in healthy humans and set the technical standards for the field.
- Sequencing technology cost crashes (the same ones that enabled the genome revolution) made it suddenly feasible to read what's in a gut sample.
- The field exploded after 2010. Tens of thousands of papers per year now.

## Fecal microbiota transplants (FMT)

- For recurrent C. diff infections, the treatment is often a **fecal microbiota transplant**, where stool from a healthy donor is transferred into the patient's gut. The cure rate is around **90%**, far better than antibiotics.
- The **FDA approved Rebyota** in **2022** and **Vowst** in **2023**, the first standardized stool-based therapies. Before that, FMT was a procedure done in hospitals from screened donor stool.
- Outside C. diff, FMT trials for IBD, depression, autism, and obesity are ongoing. Most early results are mixed.

## Antibiotics, diet, and the things that move the needle

- **Antibiotics** are a sledgehammer. A single course wipes out a large share of your gut bacteria. Recovery takes months, and the microbiome often does not return to its exact pre-antibiotic state. Repeated childhood antibiotic exposure is correlated with higher rates of asthma, allergies, and obesity.
- **Diet is the lever most under your control.** Diverse fiber from fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and nuts feeds a diverse microbiome. The general rule from researchers like **Tim Spector** at King's College London is **30 different plants per week**.
- **Ultra-processed food** is associated with less diverse microbiomes, more inflammation markers, and worse health outcomes. The exact mechanism is still being mapped.
- **Fermented foods** (yogurt with live cultures, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut) appear to boost microbial diversity, possibly more reliably than off-the-shelf probiotic supplements.

## Probiotics: the supplement caveat

- Most off-the-shelf probiotic supplements have **weak evidence** behind their general claims. Many of the bacteria don't survive stomach acid. Many of the strains are not the ones with research backing.
- **Specific strains for specific conditions have stronger evidence.** *Saccharomyces boulardii* for antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Certain Lactobacillus strains for vaginal infections. *Bifidobacterium infantis* for infant gut development.
- The general "take a probiotic for gut health" pitch is mostly marketing. The specific clinical applications are real.

## What to carry away

- You're roughly half microbe by cell count, and the half that's not microbe relies heavily on the half that is.
- The gut microbiome digests fiber, trains the immune system, makes neurotransmitters, and is in constant chemical conversation with the brain.
- The strongest single move you can make for it is to eat a wider range of plants. Most supplement claims outrun the evidence.
- This is a young, fast-moving field. Expect a lot of what you read about it today to look naive in 10 years. The basic story, that an internal microbial ecosystem matters more than we used to think, will hold.
