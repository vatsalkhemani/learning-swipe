# How vaccines work and why they matter

## What vaccines are and why they matter

A vaccine is a controlled preview of a threat that trains your immune system to recognize and destroy that threat fast, before it can make you sick. The principle is simple. The execution is one of the great achievements of modern medicine. Vaccines have eradicated smallpox, nearly eliminated polio, and prevented somewhere between two and three million child deaths every year. They are also the most cost-effective medical intervention ever measured. The catch is that they are a public good. They work best when most people use them, and they have always lived in tension with the human instinct to mistrust something injected into a healthy person. That tension is older than vaccines themselves and is the reason the politics of vaccination keeps recurring.

## The origin story

- **Edward Jenner, 1796.** An English country doctor noticed that milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild disease, never seemed to get smallpox, which killed roughly 30% of those it infected. He took pus from a milkmaid's cowpox sore, scratched it into the arm of a boy named James Phipps, and then later exposed the boy to smallpox. The boy did not get sick. This was the first vaccine. The word itself comes from *vacca*, Latin for cow.
- **Louis Pasteur, 1880s.** Expanded the principle beyond cowpox. He showed that you could deliberately weaken pathogens in the lab and use the weakened version as a vaccine. He made vaccines for chicken cholera, anthrax, and rabies. The framework moved from one lucky observation to a general method.
- **Smallpox eradicated, 1980.** The World Health Organization's global vaccination campaign wiped out smallpox entirely. The last natural case was a Somali cook named **Ali Maow Maalin** in 1977. It is the only human disease ever eradicated by humans.

## How they actually work

- Your immune system has two main parts. The **innate** system reacts fast and non-specifically. The **adaptive** system is slow on first exposure but learns. It builds **antibodies** against a specific pathogen and **memory B and T cells** that remember it for years or life.
- A vaccine introduces a safe version of the pathogen, or just a piece of it (called an **antigen**), to the adaptive immune system. The body builds antibodies and memory cells without ever having to fight the real disease.
- When the real pathogen shows up later, the immune system recognizes it within hours instead of weeks. The infection is shut down before it can spread or cause symptoms.

## Types of vaccines

- **Live attenuated.** A weakened version of the actual virus. Strong, long-lasting immunity, usually from one or two doses. Examples: MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), yellow fever, oral polio, chickenpox.
- **Inactivated.** The virus or bacterium is killed entirely. Safer in immunocompromised people but weaker, often needs boosters. Examples: flu shot, hepatitis A, rabies, inactivated polio.
- **Subunit and protein.** Just a piece of the pathogen, usually a specific protein. Very safe but immune response can be weaker. Examples: hepatitis B, HPV, shingles.
- **Toxoid.** When the disease is caused by a bacterial toxin, you immunize against the toxin itself. Examples: tetanus, diphtheria.
- **mRNA.** A new platform. You inject a strand of messenger RNA that tells your own cells to briefly make a piece of the pathogen, which your immune system then learns to recognize. Pfizer and Moderna's COVID vaccines (2020) were the first widely used mRNA vaccines. **Katalin Kariko** spent decades on mRNA research, mostly underfunded and ignored, before her work became the foundation of the COVID vaccines. She and **Drew Weissman** won the **2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**.
- **Viral vector.** Use a different harmless virus to deliver genetic instructions for the target antigen. Examples: Johnson and Johnson's COVID vaccine, AstraZeneca's COVID vaccine, the Ebola vaccine.

## Herd immunity and the schedule

- **Herd immunity** is the threshold at which enough people in a population are immune that the pathogen can no longer find new hosts and outbreaks fizzle. Anyone who cannot be vaccinated, like newborns or the immunocompromised, is protected by the people around them.
- The threshold depends on the disease's **R0**, its base contagiousness. **Measles** has an R0 around 12 to 18, so you need about 95% immunity for herd protection. **Polio** is around 5 to 7, threshold around 80%. **COVID** original strain was around 2 to 3, but variants like Omicron raised it well past 10.
- The **CDC and WHO childhood schedules** include vaccines against roughly 14 to 16 diseases by age 6, including measles, polio, whooping cough, tetanus, diphtheria, hepatitis B, chickenpox, and HPV.

## Risks and benefits

- Vaccines have side effects. Sore arm, fatigue, sometimes a low fever. Serious adverse reactions exist but are extremely rare, usually in the range of one in a million.
- The diseases vaccines prevent are massively more dangerous than the vaccines themselves. Measles kills around 1 in 1,000 unvaccinated children in developed countries and more in poorer ones. The MMR vaccine has roughly one in a million risk of a serious reaction.
- **Vaccine hesitancy** has a long history. The modern movement gained huge momentum from a single fraudulent study. **Andrew Wakefield's 1998 paper** in The Lancet claimed a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. The paper was based on fabricated data. It was retracted in 2010 and Wakefield lost his medical license. But the fear it planted is still with us. MMR vaccination rates in the US and UK dropped, and measles outbreaks returned in places where the disease had been eliminated.

## The mRNA breakthrough and what comes next

- The COVID pandemic compressed what was normally a 10 to 15 year vaccine development cycle into about 11 months. The mRNA platform was a big reason. Once you know the genetic sequence of a target protein, you can design and manufacture an mRNA vaccine in weeks.
- That same platform is now being adapted for **cancer vaccines**. The approach is personalized. You sequence a patient's tumor, identify proteins (called **neoantigens**) unique to that tumor, and design an mRNA vaccine that trains the immune system to attack only those. Moderna and Merck have a melanoma vaccine in late-stage trials showing strong early results.
- The platform is also being applied to flu, RSV, HIV, malaria, and Zika.

## What to carry away

- A vaccine is a training exercise for your immune system. It teaches the body to recognize a threat without paying the price of actually getting sick.
- The benefit is enormous. The risk is real but usually tiny. The trade is overwhelmingly worth it for the diseases we vaccinate against today.
- COVID's mRNA breakthrough was not a one-off. The same machinery is now being pointed at cancer and at the diseases we have not yet beaten.
- The political and emotional resistance is older than the science. It is also part of the science of public health.
