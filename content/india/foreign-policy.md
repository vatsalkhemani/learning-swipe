# India's foreign policy: from non-alignment to multi-alignment

## What Indian foreign policy is and why it matters

India's foreign policy is the most successful hedging operation in modern geopolitics. After independence, **Jawaharlal Nehru** refused to pick sides in the Cold War and founded the Non-Aligned Movement. After the Cold War, India quietly leaned toward the US while keeping Russia close. Under **Narendra Modi**, the framing became explicit: "multi-alignment." India works with the US, Russia, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Israel at the same time. The Russia question after Ukraine has tested it. It has held. Understanding the doctrine is how you understand why India keeps surprising Western foreign policy commentators who expect it to act like an ally and then wonder why it doesn't.

## The Nehru era: non-alignment (1947-1964)

- After independence, **Nehru** rejected the Cold War binary. India would join neither the US camp nor the Soviet camp. The doctrine became **non-alignment**.
- The **Non-Aligned Movement** formally launched in 1961 in Belgrade. Nehru's co-founders: **Josip Broz Tito** of Yugoslavia, **Gamal Abdel Nasser** of Egypt, **Sukarno** of Indonesia. The movement reached over 120 member countries at its peak.
- The reality was more complex than the rhetoric. India accepted Soviet military aid, leaned on USSR diplomatic support against Pakistan, and signed a Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with Moscow in 1971.
- **The 1962 war with China** was the trauma. Border skirmishes in the Himalayas escalated into a Chinese invasion across the Aksai Chin and the McMahon Line. India lost decisively in about a month. Nehru's health and his worldview both broke. He died in 1964, partly from the shock.

## Hardening through the wars

- **1965 Pakistan war**. Brief, inconclusive, ended by Soviet mediation at Tashkent. Lal Bahadur Shastri, the PM, died of a heart attack the night the agreement was signed.
- **1971 Bangladesh war** was decisive. Pakistan was committing mass atrocities in East Pakistan (now Bangladesh). Ten million refugees flooded into India. **Indira Gandhi** moved. The Indian Army won in 13 days. Bangladesh became independent. **93,000 Pakistani soldiers** surrendered, the largest military surrender since WWII.
  - The US under **Nixon** and **Kissinger** sent the Seventh Fleet into the Bay of Bengal in support of Pakistan. India remembered. The "tilt" became a permanent reference point in how India reads US intentions.
- **1974 nuclear test** ("Smiling Buddha") at Pokhran. India joined the nuclear club without signing the NPT. Sanctions followed, including the formation of the **Nuclear Suppliers Group** explicitly to keep India out of the nuclear trade. India spent the next 24 years in nuclear isolation.
- **1998 nuclear tests**. **Atal Bihari Vajpayee** of the BJP ordered five tests at Pokhran in May 1998. Pakistan tested two weeks later. Heavy sanctions. The bet: ride them out, build deterrence, force the world to deal with a nuclear India. It worked.

## The shift toward the US

- The **post-Cold War realignment** started under **P V Narasimha Rao** in the early 1990s. Economic liberalization opened the door. The Clinton-Vajpayee era warmed it up.
- **Bill Clinton's visit to India in March 2000** was the symbolic turning point. He spent 5 days in India, six hours in Pakistan. The asymmetry was deliberate.
- The **2008 US-India Civil Nuclear Agreement** was the landmark. President **George W Bush** and Prime Minister **Manmohan Singh** struck a deal that let India trade in civilian nuclear technology without signing the NPT. The Nuclear Suppliers Group granted India a one-time waiver. Effectively, the world accepted India as a nuclear power outside the treaty framework.
- Defense ties followed. India has signed the four "foundational agreements" with the US (LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA, GSOMIA) that interoperate the militaries. Annual joint exercises with the US Army, Navy, and Air Force. Major arms purchases shifted from Russia toward the US, France, and Israel, though Russian equipment still dominates the existing stock.

## The Modi-era doctrine: multi-alignment

- **S Jaishankar**, External Affairs Minister since 2019 and a former Foreign Secretary and Ambassador to the US and China, articulates the doctrine. His framing: India hedges and works with everyone in its interest. India will not be asked to pick teams.
- **The Quad** (US, Japan, Australia, India) was formalized in 2017. It's a strategic grouping aimed at the Indo-Pacific and, less politely, at China. Leaders' summits became regular from 2021.
- **BRICS** (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) expanded in 2024 to add Iran, Egypt, Ethiopia, UAE. India sits in BRICS and the Quad at the same time. The contradiction is the point.
- **G20 presidency 2023** was a foreign policy showcase. India ran a year of meetings across 60+ cities, hosted the leaders' summit in New Delhi, used it to push Global South priorities. Got the **African Union admitted as a permanent G20 member**.
- Bilateral relationships kept multiplying.
  - **Israel**: ambassador-level since 1992, strong defense partnership (drones, missiles, intelligence).
  - **UAE and Saudi Arabia**: surge of Gulf investment into India. CEPA trade agreement with UAE in 2022.
  - **Japan**: ODA partner for decades, the Mumbai-Ahmedabad bullet train.
  - **France**: defense partner, Rafale fighter purchase, Macron as chief guest at 2024 Republic Day.
  - **Iran**: Chabahar Port investment as the workaround to Pakistani territory for Central Asia trade.

## The China question

- The **Galwan Valley clash** in the Ladakh sector in June 2020 killed 20 Indian soldiers and an unknown number of Chinese soldiers in hand-to-hand fighting at 14,000 feet. First combat deaths on the border in **45 years** (since 1975). Confidence-collapsing event.
- India responded with both military buildup along the Line of Actual Control and a economic decoupling push.
  - Banned **TikTok**, **WeChat**, and over 200 Chinese apps in 2020.
  - Tightened FDI rules for investments from neighboring countries (read: China).
  - PLI manufacturing schemes partly aimed at shifting supply chains out of China.
- Tensions eased somewhat by late 2024 with new disengagement agreements at Demchok and Depsang. The relationship is still cold.

## The Russia question after Ukraine

- When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the West expected India to condemn it. India didn't.
- India **abstained on UN resolutions** condemning the invasion. Bought **discounted Russian oil** at scale, becoming one of Russia's largest buyers along with China. Refused to join Western sanctions.
- The Western frustration was real and visible. The Indian position was internally consistent.
  - Russia remains India's largest weapons supplier by stock. Spare parts, training, maintenance all depend on Moscow.
  - India needed cheap oil to manage inflation. Russian crude at $60 a barrel mattered.
  - Multi-alignment as a doctrine means India doesn't take sides on others' wars when its interests are mixed.
- The position was awkward for the West but it didn't break the broader US-India relationship. Both sides treated the disagreement as one item on a wide agenda, not a deal-breaker.

## What to carry away

- Non-alignment under Nehru was about not picking Cold War sides. Multi-alignment under Modi is about working with everyone in parallel.
- The 1962 China loss and the 1971 US tilt toward Pakistan are formative memories that still shape Indian foreign policy.
- The Quad and BRICS sitting in the same Indian foreign policy is the doctrine working as intended, not as a confusion.
- The Russia stance after Ukraine is the cleanest test of the doctrine and it has held.
- In one sentence: India hedges by design, treats foreign policy as a portfolio not a team membership, and that's the doctrine the world is still catching up to.
