# The 1991 liberalization and after: how India became an economy

## What 1991 changed and why it matters

For 44 years after independence, India ran a planned economy with industrial licensing, state-owned dominance, and tariff walls. Growth averaged 3.5%, which economists called the "Hindu rate of growth" with no respect intended. In July 1991, a foreign exchange crisis forced India to open up almost overnight. The reforms that followed reshaped what kind of country India would be. The IT boom, the telecom revolution, the rise of the middle class, the move from aid-recipient to G20 host all trace back to that pivot. Understanding what worked, what didn't, and what India is betting on now is the difference between treating India's growth story as inevitable and treating it as a contested project.

## Pre-1991: the License Raj

- After independence, **Jawaharlal Nehru** set up a Soviet-inspired planned economy. Five Year Plans, large public sector, heavy industry first.
- **Industrial licensing** meant you needed government permission to start a factory, expand capacity, change products, or import equipment. Approval times of 2-3 years were normal.
- **Import substitution** kept foreign goods out behind tariff walls of 80-150% on many items. Indian-made was protected from competition and therefore mostly bad.
- The **"Hindu rate of growth"** of around 3.5% from 1950 to 1980 was a phrase coined by economist Raj Krishna. Population grew at 2%. Per capita income barely moved.
- By 1991 the system was failing. Gulf War spiked oil prices. Remittances from the Gulf collapsed. Foreign exchange reserves fell to about **$1.2 billion**, enough for three weeks of imports.
- The **RBI airlifted 47 tons of gold** to the Bank of England and Union Bank of Switzerland as collateral for emergency loans. National humiliation moment.

## The reform itself

- **P V Narasimha Rao** became Prime Minister in June 1991 after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination. He picked **Manmohan Singh**, a former RBI Governor and Oxford-trained economist, as Finance Minister.
- Singh's **July 24, 1991 budget speech** quoted Victor Hugo: "no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come." Translation: the License Raj was dead.
- The reform package was sweeping for one summer.
  - **Industrial delicensing** for all but 18 sectors.
  - **Currency devaluation** of the rupee by about 19% across two moves.
  - **FDI opened** with automatic approval up to 51% in many sectors.
  - **Tariffs cut** from peak rates of 150% toward 25-30%.
  - **MRTP Act loosened**, removing limits on large business expansion.
- The reforms passed in part because the **IMF loan** was conditional on them. Rao used the external pressure as political cover for changes the Indian left would otherwise have killed.

## What grew

- **IT services** took off from the late 1990s. **Y2K** opened doors. Indian engineers were cheaper and English-fluent. **Infosys** went public on NASDAQ in 1999, the first Indian company listed on a US exchange. **TCS**, **Wipro**, and later **HCL** built the model: large body-shopping contracts with Western banks and corporates.
- **Telecom** was the second wave. Mobile penetration exploded through the 2000s. Then **Reliance Jio**, launched by **Mukesh Ambani** in September 2016, dropped 4G data prices by about 90% in one move. India went from one of the most expensive data markets to one of the cheapest. Smartphone-first internet adoption reached 800+ million users.
- **Pharmaceuticals**. Indian generics manufacturers became the global supplier to developing-country health systems. India makes 60% of global vaccine doses. **Serum Institute** in Pune is the largest vaccine maker in the world.
- **Auto and auto components**. **Maruti Suzuki** (1983 joint venture) showed that India could build cars at scale. Tata, Mahindra, and the Korean and Japanese majors followed.
- A **demographic dividend** is the structural tailwind. About 65% of Indians are under 35. Median age is 28, compared to China's 39 and Japan's 49.

## What hasn't worked

- **Manufacturing share** of GDP has been stuck at around 16% for two decades. China's is 28%. Vietnam's is climbing fast. India tried to skip manufacturing and go from agriculture to services. That worked for the educated tenth. It didn't work for the rest.
- **Formal employment** is still small. Roughly 90% of Indian workers are in the informal sector. No contracts, no social security, no protections. The IT boom employs around 5 million people directly out of a 500-million workforce.
- **Agriculture** still employs about 45% of the workforce but produces only 17% of GDP. The arithmetic alone tells you how poor most Indian farmers are.
- **The jobs gap** is the live political issue. India adds roughly 12 million people to the workforce every year. The economy creates far fewer formal jobs than that. Underemployment masks unemployment.

## Why China pulled ahead

- China and India had similar GDP per capita in 1980. By 2024 China's was about five times India's.
- **Infrastructure speed**. China built more highway, rail, and port capacity in 15 years than India built in 50. Bulldozing villages for projects is faster in an authoritarian system. India's land acquisition law and democratic process make it slower.
- **Manufacturing focus**. China bet on coastal export manufacturing in the 1980s and 90s. India bet on import-substitution longer, then on services.
- **Authoritarian decisiveness for better and worse**. China can mandate at scale. India has to negotiate every reform through 28 state governments, opposition parties, courts, and civil society. The cost is speed. The benefit is durability.

## Current bets

- **PLI schemes** (Production Linked Incentives) launched 2020 across 14 sectors. Cash subsidies for manufacturers who hit volume targets. Apple suppliers like Foxconn, Pegatron, Wistron have shifted iPhone assembly into Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. iPhone exports from India crossed $14 billion in FY2024.
- **Semiconductor mission**. A $10 billion incentive package. **Tata** broke ground on a fab in Gujarat in 2024 with Taiwan's Powerchip. First Indian-made chips are 28nm, not cutting edge, but a real start.
- **Digital Public Infrastructure** is India's most exported policy product.
  - **Aadhaar** (2009-): biometric ID for over 1.3 billion people. Identity layer.
  - **UPI** (Unified Payments Interface, 2016): real-time interbank transfers. Processed over 17 billion transactions per month in 2024. Free for users. Now being adopted by France, UAE, Singapore.
  - **CoWIN** ran the COVID vaccination rollout for over a billion doses.
- The **services export** story keeps compounding. Global Capability Centers (GCCs) of multinational firms in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune now employ ~2 million people doing higher-end work than the body-shopping era.

## What to carry away

- 1991 was the inflection. Everything after, including the IT boom, traces to that summer.
- Growth came from services and consumption, not manufacturing. That's the asymmetry to fix.
- The demographic dividend is a window, not a guarantee. If formal jobs don't catch up, the window closes ugly.
- DPI is the surprise export. UPI and Aadhaar are now studied globally as models for digital public goods.
- In one sentence: India became an economy in 1991, grew on services for 30 years, and is now trying to add manufacturing before the demographic clock runs out.
