# Indian classical traditions: music, dance, art across centuries

## What it is and why it matters

India's classical traditions are not one thing. They are multiple parallel systems, each with its own rules, lineages, and centuries of accumulated knowledge. Two distinct classical music traditions. At least eight recognized classical dance forms. Painting schools that predate European oil painting. Sanskrit literature that the Enlightenment philosophers were still discovering when they shaped modern thought. Most of this was nearly lost during British rule and was revived in the 20th century by a small set of obsessive practitioners. Understanding it is how you understand what Indians mean when they talk about "culture," and why even very modern Indians treat the classical world with a respect Western moderns rarely give their own.

## The two classical music systems

- **Hindustani** is the North Indian system. More Persian and Central Asian influence (Mughal courts patronized it for 300 years), longer raga elaboration where a single raga can unfold over an hour, improvisation-heavy. Instruments include the **sitar** (Ravi Shankar made it globally famous), **tabla** (the two-drum percussion set), **sarod** (fretless lute), **sarangi** (bowed string instrument), and **bansuri** (bamboo flute). Vocal giants of the 20th century: **Bhimsen Joshi**, **Kishori Amonkar**, **Pandit Jasraj**, **Kumar Gandharva**.
- **Carnatic** is the South Indian system. Less invaded, more continuously Hindu, more composition-driven. The structure is built around **kritis** (devotional compositions), with improvisation layered around a fixed core. Instruments: **veena**, **mridangam** (the main percussion), **violin** (adopted from Europe in the 18th century and absorbed completely), **flute**. Vocal greats: **M.S. Subbulakshmi** (first musician to receive the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian honor), **Balamuralikrishna**, **T.M. Krishna** (also a public intellectual).
- **What they share.** Both run on the **raga** system: roughly 200+ named ragas, each a melodic framework with prescribed notes, characteristic phrases, mood, and (in many cases) a time of day or season it belongs to. Both run on the **tala** system: rhythmic cycles of 3 to 16+ beats with internal structure.
- **The gharanas.** In Hindustani music, knowledge passed down through family-like lineages called gharanas (literally "households"): **Gwalior**, **Kirana**, **Patiala**, **Mewati**, **Jaipur-Atrauli** and others. Each had its own stylistic stamp. Carnatic has parampara (teaching lineages) but less of the gharana branding.

## The classical dance forms

- **Bharatanatyam** (Tamil Nadu). Geometric, sharp, temple origins. The most globally recognized Indian dance. Revived in the early 20th century by **Rukmini Devi Arundale**, who pulled it out of the devadasi temple-dancer tradition and reframed it as respectable art.
- **Kathak** (North India). Mughal-influenced, footwork-heavy, famous for fast spins (chakkars) and storytelling. The most rhythmically intricate of the classical dances.
- **Kathakali** (Kerala). Elaborate green-and-red face makeup, masks, dramatic eye movement, all-night temple performances. The dancer is almost always male and the training takes a decade or more.
- **Odissi** (Odisha). Sculptural, soft, derived from temple sculpture at Konark and Bhubaneshwar. Revived in the mid-20th century from near-extinction.
- **Manipuri** (Manipur). Gentle, devotional, Vaishnav-inflected, no harsh lines.
- **Kuchipudi** (Andhra Pradesh). Originally male brahmin dance-drama from a village called Kuchelapuram, now performed by all genders.
- **Mohiniyattam** (Kerala). The other Kerala form, feminine, slow, in white-and-gold costume.
- **Sattriya** (Assam). Added to the official list of classical dances only in 2000. Born in 15th-century Vaishnav monasteries.
- All have ancient roots, all were marginalized under British rule, all were codified and revived in the 20th century by named individuals doing slow, careful, archival work.

## Visual arts and painting

- **Cave temples.** **Ajanta** (2nd century BCE-6th century CE, Buddhist murals and sculpture), **Ellora** (6th-10th century CE, Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain in the same complex). The Ajanta paintings predate European fresco technique by centuries.
- **Temple architecture and sculpture.** **Khajuraho** (10th-11th century, Chandela dynasty, famous for explicit sculpture as part of a larger philosophical program), **Konark Sun Temple** (13th century, Odisha), **Brihadeeswarar Temple** at Thanjavur (11th century Chola, a 66-meter tower built without modern engineering).
- **Mughal miniature painting** (16th-19th century). Persian technique adapted to Indian subjects. Court scenes, hunting, portraits, Mughal emperors as patrons. **Akbar's** atelier produced the **Hamzanama**, a 1,400-folio illustrated epic.
- **Rajput painting.** Hindu court traditions in Rajasthan reacting to and absorbing Mughal style. Romantic, mythological, Krishna-themed.
- **Pahari schools.** Painting from the Punjab Hills (Kangra, Basohli, Guler). Kangra style (18th century) is famous for its lyrical Krishna paintings.
- **Tanjore painting.** Tamil Nadu, gold leaf on wood, religious subjects, the kind of painting your grandmother probably had at home.
- **Bengal School revival.** Early 20th century. **Abanindranath Tagore** (Rabindranath's nephew) led a movement to reject European academic painting and recover Indian visual idioms.
- **Modernists.** The **Progressive Artists' Group** formed in Bombay in **1947**: **M.F. Husain**, **S.H. Raza**, **F.N. Souza**, **Tyeb Mehta**, and others. They synthesized Indian subjects with global modernism. Now blue-chip globally: Husain paintings have crossed **$1 million** at auction, Mehta crossed **$3 million**. An entire global market for Indian modern art barely existed 30 years ago.

## Sanskrit literature and architecture

- **Sanskrit epics.** The **Mahabharata** (composed roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE) is the longest poem in world literature, ~10x the length of the Iliad and Odyssey combined. The **Ramayana** (~7th-5th century BCE), shorter, more focused, the model story of ideal kingship.
- **Kalidasa** (5th century CE). The Shakespeare of Sanskrit. Plays like *Shakuntala* and the lyric poem *Meghaduta* (Cloud Messenger).
- **The Bhagavad Gita.** 700 verses embedded in the Mahabharata. Krishna's battlefield dialogue with Arjuna. The single most influential Hindu philosophical text. Influenced Emerson, Thoreau, Gandhi, and Oppenheimer (who quoted it at Trinity).
- **Indo-Saracenic architecture.** British-era hybrid that mixed Mughal domes and arches with Victorian Gothic. Mumbai's **CST station** (built 1888, UNESCO World Heritage Site) is the iconic example.
- **The Taj Mahal.** Completed 1653 by **Shah Jahan** for his wife Mumtaz Mahal. White marble, Persian symmetry, Mughal craftsmanship at its peak. Roughly 20,000 workers, 22 years to build.

## What people get wrong

- These traditions are not frozen. Ragas evolve, dancers experiment, painters argue with the canon. The classical world has its own internal modernism.
- The revival narrative matters. Without specific individuals doing patient work in the early 20th century (Rukmini Devi for Bharatanatyam, Vishnu Digambar Paluskar for Hindustani music, Abanindranath Tagore for painting), many of these forms would be in much worse shape today.
- It is largely elite-consumed today. Classical concerts struggle for audiences outside a small urban set. Government support is uneven. The economics are real.

## What to carry away

- Two music systems, at least eight classical dances, multiple painting schools, ancient architecture, Sanskrit literature. They are parallel inheritances, not one.
- All of them were close to lost under colonial rule and were rebuilt by named, traceable individuals in the 20th century.
- The traditions still have working practitioners today. They are not museum pieces, even if they are increasingly niche.
