# Indian economic history pre-1991: the License Raj era and what we got wrong

## What it is and why it matters

For the first 44 years after independence in 1947, India ran a planned, inward-looking, heavily licensed economy. The government decided who could produce what, how much, where, and at what price. The system built some genuinely world-class institutions and fed the country, but it also throttled growth and bred corruption. By 1991 the foreign exchange reserves were down to three weeks of imports, and India had to mortgage gold to the Bank of England. The reforms that followed are the founding event of modern Indian capitalism. Anyone over 35 in India remembers the old world. Every "ease of doing business" push since references it.

## How it started

- **Independence in 1947** left India poor, partition-scarred, and skeptical of the colonial trading model that had drained the country. The intellectual mood favored planning, state-led industrialization, and self-reliance.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru's vision** was a socialist-influenced mixed economy. The state would hold the "commanding heights": steel, energy, banking, heavy industry, defense. The private sector existed but was tightly regulated.
- **The Five-Year Plans** were modeled on the Soviet system. The Planning Commission set production targets and allocated capital. The Second Plan (1956-61), drafted by statistician **P.C. Mahalanobis**, doubled down on heavy industry over consumer goods.
- The licensing regime grew on top of this. By the 1960s a businessman wanting to start an industrial project needed roughly **80 separate licenses** from various ministries. Capacity additions, new products, imports, foreign exchange, even where to locate a factory all required permission.
- **C. Rajagopalachari**, founder of the free-market Swatantra Party, coined the phrase **"permit-license-quota Raj"** in 1959. It stuck. License Raj became how the era is remembered.

## What worked

- **Institution building.** India built **IIT**, **IIM**, and **AIIMS** during this period. State capacity to produce engineers, managers, and doctors at scale was the era's most durable gift. Today's Silicon Valley CEO pipeline runs through institutions Nehru's government founded.
- **The Green Revolution (1960s-70s).** Agricultural scientist **M.S. Swaminathan** working with American agronomist **Norman Borlaug** introduced high-yield wheat and rice varieties. India went from importing food aid under PL-480 to food grain self-sufficiency by the late 1970s. Punjab and Haryana became the breadbasket. Famine, a regular feature of Indian life, ended.
- **Strategic capability.** ISRO was founded in 1969 (Vikram Sarabhai). India tested its first nuclear device in **1974** ("Smiling Buddha"). The defense industrial base, however inefficient, existed.
- **Public sector behemoths.** SAIL (steel), ONGC (oil), BHEL (heavy electricals), Indian Railways, the nationalized banks. Inefficient by global standards, but they built physical infrastructure and gave hundreds of thousands of families their first salaried jobs.

## What didn't

- **The "Hindu rate of growth."** Economist **Raj Krishna** coined this in the 1970s to describe India's stubborn ~3.5% annual GDP growth. The phrase had nothing to do with religion. It just meant slow. Compared to East Asian peers like South Korea and Taiwan growing at 8-10%, India was falling behind.
- **The private sector was strangled.** The Tata, Birla, and Bajaj groups existed but couldn't expand without permission. Antitrust rules (MRTP Act 1969) capped how big a private firm could grow. Innovation was punished, not rewarded.
- **Consumer choice was a joke.** Buying a Bajaj scooter meant a **7-year waiting list**. The Premier Padmini car was based on a 1950s Fiat design and was still being sold in the 1990s. The Hindustan Ambassador was a 1956 Morris Oxford with minor modifications. Two phone companies (state-run MTNL, BSNL). Two TV channels (Doordarshan and DD2).
- **Bank nationalization in 1969.** Indira Gandhi nationalized 14 large private banks overnight. The move was politically popular but turned banks into government employment programs and lending vehicles for politically chosen projects.
- **FERA (1973)** made foreign companies dilute their stake to 40% or leave. **IBM** and **Coca-Cola** both walked out of India in 1977 rather than comply. India lost a generation of foreign technology and capital.
- **Corruption became structural.** When bureaucrats have discretion over licenses worth crores of rupees, bribes are not a bug. They are the system. The license officer became one of the most powerful and most paid-off figures in the economy.

## The 1991 crisis and the reforms

- **The trigger.** A combination of the 1990 Gulf War oil price spike, the collapse of the Soviet Union (a major trading partner), and years of fiscal deficits left India with **$1.2 billion in foreign exchange reserves**, enough for about three weeks of imports. The government physically airlifted **47 tons of gold** to the Bank of England as collateral for an emergency IMF loan.
- **The team.** Prime Minister **P.V. Narasimha Rao**, an unlikely reformer who had been part of the old Congress establishment, brought in **Manmohan Singh** as finance minister. Singh was an Oxford-trained economist, technocratic, soft-spoken, and politically uninteresting. That was the point.
- **The reforms (July 1991).** Industrial licensing abolished for most sectors. The rupee devalued in two steps by about 20%. Foreign direct investment opened up to 51% in many sectors. Customs duties slashed. Capital markets opened. The Planning Commission's role shrank.
- **The famous Manmohan Singh budget speech** quoted Victor Hugo: "no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come."

## What endured and what changed

- The institutions Nehru built (IITs, IIMs, ISRO, defense labs, public sector banks) survived and in many cases now power the post-1991 economy.
- The licensing apparatus mostly died. Some sectors (defense, atomic energy, retail until recently) stayed protected.
- The mental model took longer to die. "Ease of doing business" is still a constant policy push 30+ years later because the bureaucratic instinct to control hasn't fully left.
- The post-1991 economy grew at 6-8% for two decades. The middle class went from ~50 million to ~300 million. The unicorns, the IT services giants, the consumer brands all date from after 1991.

## What to carry away

- Pre-1991 India was not stupid. It was building institutions, feeding itself, and protecting strategic capability under genuine constraints.
- It was also slow, corrupt, and consumer-hostile in ways that became indefensible once East Asia showed a better path.
- The 1991 reforms were not ideological. They happened because the country was nearly bankrupt. The crisis made the politics possible.
- Every Indian reform program since references the License Raj. It is the founding "before" picture of modern Indian capitalism.
