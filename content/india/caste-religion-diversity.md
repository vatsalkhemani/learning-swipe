# Caste, religion, regional diversity: the honest answer

## What this is and why it matters

India holds 17% of humanity inside a single country. About 1.43 billion people, 22 official languages, around 700 mother tongues, 6 major religions native to the subcontinent, and 28 states that each feel like a small country in food, dress, and political tradition. The diversity isn't a tagline. It's the constraint inside which the political system operates. Caste, religion, and region are the three axes most foreigners get wrong. The polite version glosses too much; the angry version misses how much actually works. The honest answer holds both that India has made huge progress on most metrics and that the fault lines are still live.

## The scale

- **1.43 billion people**, more than China since around 2023. China's population is now falling. India's is still growing, expected to peak around 1.65 billion in the 2060s.
- About **600 million** live in the **Hindi belt** (UP, Bihar, MP, Rajasthan, Haryana, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Delhi). This is the political heartland.
- **22 official languages** under the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution. Hindi and English are the official languages of the Union government, but neither is "national." India deliberately doesn't have a single national language.
- The state of **Uttar Pradesh** alone has about 240 million people, larger than every country except China, India itself, the US, and Indonesia.

## Religion

- About **80% Hindu**, **14% Muslim**, **2.3% Christian**, **1.7% Sikh**, **0.7% Buddhist**, **0.4% Jain**, plus smaller Parsi (Zoroastrian) and Jewish communities. The percentages are from the 2011 Census. The next census is overdue.
- India's Muslim population at around **200 million** is the largest Muslim minority anywhere in the world. Larger than the entire population of Pakistan, Bangladesh, Iran, Saudi Arabia, or Egypt. The third largest Muslim population of any country, behind only Indonesia and Pakistan.
- **Hinduism** itself isn't a single thing. It's an umbrella for traditions that span the Vedic schools, devotional Bhakti, philosophical Vedanta, Shaivism, Vaishnavism, Shaktism, plus thousands of folk practices. There's no central religious authority, no equivalent of a Pope.
- **Sikhism** was founded by **Guru Nanak** around 1500 CE in Punjab. Monotheistic, rejects caste in principle, sees itself as distinct from Hinduism. Punjab is the heartland; large diaspora in the UK, Canada, US.
- **Christianity** in India is older than in most of Europe. **Saint Thomas the Apostle** is believed to have landed in Kerala in 52 CE. Indian Christianity isn't a colonial import; the colonial conversions came on top of an older base.
- **Jainism** and **Buddhism** both emerged in the 6th-5th century BCE in what is now Bihar. Jainism stayed inside India and stayed small but influential, especially in trade and business. Buddhism declined in India around 1200 CE and spread across Asia.

## Caste, honestly

- Caste is a hierarchical social structure with origins in the Vedic period (~1500-500 BCE), formalized in later texts into four **varnas**: Brahmins (priests, teachers), Kshatriyas (warriors, rulers), Vaishyas (traders, farmers), Shudras (laborers, service castes).
- Outside the varna system entirely were **Dalits**, formerly called "untouchables." They did the work considered ritually polluting: handling dead bodies, leather, sewage. **B R Ambedkar**, the architect of the Indian Constitution, was Dalit. He famously converted to Buddhism in 1956 with hundreds of thousands of followers as a protest against caste.
- The varnas split into thousands of **jatis**, sub-castes that are the actual operative unit. Your jati shapes who you marry, what last name you carry, what village you came from, what your grandparents did.
- The 1950 Constitution abolished untouchability and outlawed caste discrimination. **Article 17** is explicit. In practice, the system persists in marriage patterns (over 90% of marriages still within caste), in rural occupational patterns, in political identity.
- **Reservations** are India's affirmative action. Seats and jobs reserved for **SC** (Scheduled Castes, the Dalit category), **ST** (Scheduled Tribes, India's indigenous peoples), and **OBC** (Other Backward Classes). The percentages: 15% SC, 7.5% ST, 27% OBC in central government jobs and central university admissions. Plus 10% for **EWS** (Economically Weaker Sections among the general category), added 2019.
- The honest contradiction: reservations have visibly created a Dalit and OBC middle class with real political power, especially in states like Tamil Nadu, Bihar, UP. They've also calcified caste as a category, since you need a caste certificate to claim the seat. The debate isn't whether reservations work; it's whether they should expand, contract, or change shape.

## Regional differences

- **South India** (Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Kerala) feels like a different country in many ways.
  - Speaks **Dravidian languages** (Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam), structurally unrelated to the Indo-European Hindi family.
  - **Better human development indicators**. Kerala's literacy rate is about 96%; Bihar's is 62%. Tamil Nadu's per capita income is more than twice Bihar's.
  - **Lower fertility**. Most southern states are now below replacement level. The North is still above.
  - **Less Hindi acceptance**. The 1965 anti-Hindi agitations in Tamil Nadu turned violent and shaped the Dravidian political parties (DMK, AIADMK) that have dominated the state ever since.
  - The South contributes more to federal taxes than it gets back. This grievance is live and growing.
- **The Northeast** (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura) is ethnically and culturally closer to Southeast Asia than to mainland India. Predominantly Christian in several states. Connected to the rest of India by the narrow **Siliguri Corridor**, sometimes called the "Chicken's Neck," about 22 km wide. The region has felt distant and underrepresented for most of independent India's history. **Manipur** has been in serious ethnic conflict since 2023.
- The **Hindi belt** (the North) is more politically dominant in Parliament because of population. It's also poorer on average and has worse health and education outcomes. This North-South tension drives federalism debates.

## The honest contradiction

- India has lifted around 400 million people out of extreme poverty since 1990. Literacy went from 18% at independence to 77% now. Life expectancy from 32 to 70. By every macro indicator, the country is unrecognizably better than at independence.
- The fault lines have not disappeared.
  - Hindu-Muslim tensions periodically turn into riots; the 2002 Gujarat violence, the 2020 Delhi riots, ongoing communal flashpoints.
  - Caste discrimination in marriage and employment is still routine.
  - Regional resentments toward the Center are growing in the South.
  - Manipur is in slow-burn ethnic conflict.
- Both things are true at once. The progress is real. The fault lines are real. Anyone who tells you only one half is selling you something.

## What to carry away

- Diversity is the constraint the political system was built around, not a tagline. The constitution's federal structure, the language policy, and the reservation system are all responses to it.
- Caste was constitutionally abolished, still socially operative. Reservations are the lever that's actually moved things.
- The North-South split is becoming the most consequential cleavage of the next two decades, not Hindu-Muslim, because of demography and money flows.
- The Muslim population of India is larger than most Muslim-majority countries. Forgetting this number is how people misread Indian politics.
- In one sentence: India is a country where huge progress and live fault lines coexist, and the system holds because it was designed to hold both at once.
