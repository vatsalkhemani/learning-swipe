# Buddhism: the religion that started as a meditation technique and conquered Asia

## What is Buddhism and why it matters

Buddhism is the world's fourth-largest religion, with about 520 million followers, roughly 7% of humanity. It was founded in the 5th century BCE in northern India by **Siddhartha Gautama**, known as **the Buddha** ("the awakened one"). At its core it isn't about gods. It's a method for ending suffering by understanding the mind and reality clearly. From India it spread across Asia, taking different shapes in different cultures: monastic and rigorous in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, devotional and elaborate in China and Japan, mystical and tantric in Tibet. In the last 70 years it has spread to the West, where its meditation techniques have been extracted and sold as "mindfulness." It matters because it offers one of humanity's most rigorous psychological systems, and because half a billion people order their lives around it.

## How it started

- **Siddhartha Gautama (~563-483 BCE)** was born a prince in what is now southern Nepal, near the town of Lumbini. His father was the ruler of a small kingdom of the Shakya clan.
- A sage predicted at his birth that he would either become a great king or a great spiritual teacher. His father, wanting the king option, kept him inside palace walls and shielded him from all suffering.
- At age 29 he went out and encountered the **four sights**: an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering monk. The first three showed him that life ends in suffering. The fourth showed him a possible way out.
- That night he left his sleeping wife and newborn son, shaved his head, and became a wandering ascetic. This is called the **Great Renunciation**.
- He studied with the leading meditation teachers of his day, mastered their methods, and found them insufficient. He then tried extreme asceticism (fasting almost to death) for six years and found that insufficient too.
- He sat under a fig tree (later called the **Bodhi tree**) in **Bodh Gaya** and resolved not to get up until he understood. After what tradition says was 49 days, he achieved enlightenment at age 35.
- He spent the next 45 years walking northern India and teaching, building a community of monks and nuns (the **sangha**). He died at age 80 in Kushinagar.

## Core teachings

- **The Four Noble Truths**, the foundation of all Buddhism:
  - Life involves **dukkha** (suffering, unsatisfactoriness, friction).
  - The cause of dukkha is **tanha** (craving, clinging, thirst for things to be other than they are).
  - Dukkha can end.
  - The way to end it is **the Eightfold Path**.
- **The Eightfold Path**: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. Often grouped into three: wisdom (view, intention), ethics (speech, action, livelihood), mental discipline (effort, mindfulness, concentration).
- **The Three Marks of Existence**:
  - **Anicca**: everything is impermanent. Nothing lasts. Trying to hold on to anything causes suffering.
  - **Dukkha**: that impermanence means we can never get lasting satisfaction from anything we cling to.
  - **Anatta**: there is no permanent self. What we call "I" is a flow of perceptions, sensations, and thoughts, not a fixed soul. This is the most radical Buddhist claim and the one that separates it most sharply from Hinduism.
- **Nirvana**: the goal. The extinction of craving and the end of the rebirth cycle. Not "heaven." Not a place. The cessation of the engine that produces suffering.
- **Karma and rebirth**: similar in shape to Hindu versions but reframed. Without a permanent self, what gets reborn is a stream of causes and conditions, not a soul.

## Sacred texts

- **The Pali Canon (Tripitaka, "three baskets")**: the oldest complete Buddhist scriptures, written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE in the Pali language. Three sections: the **Sutta Pitaka** (Buddha's discourses), the **Vinaya Pitaka** (rules for monks), and the **Abhidhamma Pitaka** (systematic philosophy).
- **The Mahayana sutras**: a later wave of texts (1st century BCE to ~500 CE) that became central to Mahayana Buddhism. Includes the **Heart Sutra**, the **Lotus Sutra**, the **Diamond Sutra**.
- **Tibetan canon**: includes everything above plus tantric texts unique to Vajrayana, such as the **Tibetan Book of the Dead**.
- **Zen** in Japan downplays scriptures in favor of direct experience, but still uses koan collections like the **Mumonkan**.

## Major branches

- **Theravada ("the way of the elders," ~35% of Buddhists, ~190 million)**: the older, more conservative form. Centered on monastic discipline and individual liberation through meditation. Dominant in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos. Closest to the earliest teachings.
- **Mahayana ("greater vehicle," ~55%, ~290 million)**: emerged around the 1st century BCE. Centered on the **bodhisattva ideal**: instead of seeking liberation only for yourself, you vow to help all beings reach it. More devotional, more focused on cosmic Buddhas like Amitabha (the Buddha of infinite light). Dominant in China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan. Includes:
  - **Pure Land Buddhism**: faith in Amitabha Buddha; recite his name and you'll be reborn in his paradise. The most popular form in East Asia.
  - **Zen (Chinese: Chan)**: stripped-down, direct, often paradoxical. Sit, breathe, observe. Reached Japan around 1200 CE. Most exported branch in the West.
  - **Nichiren**: founded in 13th-century Japan, centers on chanting the title of the Lotus Sutra.
- **Vajrayana ("diamond vehicle," ~6%, ~30 million)**: tantric Buddhism that uses visualization, mantras, ritual, deity yoga to accelerate the path. Dominant in Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia, parts of Nepal. The **Dalai Lama** is the most famous Vajrayana figure.

## Demographics and geography

- ~99% of Buddhists live in Asia.
- **China**: largest by absolute number (~250 million, though estimates vary widely because Chinese folk religion blends Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism).
- **Thailand**: ~95% Buddhist.
- **Myanmar**: ~88%.
- **Sri Lanka**: ~70%.
- **Japan**: ~67% claim some Buddhist identity, often alongside Shinto.
- **India**: ~0.7%, despite being Buddhism's birthplace. Buddhism was marginalized by Hindu revival and Muslim invasions by ~1200 CE. The Dalit Buddhist movement, started when **B. R. Ambedkar** converted with 500,000 followers in 1956, has grown since.
- **United States**: ~1% (~3 million). Roughly half Asian-American (Vietnamese, Chinese, Korean immigrants and descendants), half Western converts.

## Practices

- **Meditation**: the core practice. Theravada uses techniques like **anapanasati** (mindfulness of breathing) and **vipassana** (insight meditation watching the rise and fall of phenomena). Zen uses zazen (just sitting) and koans. Tibetan Buddhism uses elaborate visualization.
- **Monasticism**: a defining institution. Theravada countries still have visible monastic life, with young men often spending months or years in robes before returning to lay life.
- **Chanting** of sutras and mantras.
- **Offerings** at altars: incense, food, flowers, candles. Not because the Buddha needs them but as practices of generosity and gratitude.
- **Vesak**: the most important Buddhist holiday, marking the Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and death (held to be the same lunar day).
- **Vegetarianism**: common but not universal. Most Mahayana monks are vegetarian. Theravada monks often eat whatever is offered, including meat, on the principle that begging means you don't choose.
- **Refuge**: becoming Buddhist traditionally means taking refuge in the Three Jewels: the Buddha, the Dharma (teaching), and the Sangha (community).

## Modern controversies

- **Buddhist nationalism and violence**. The image of peaceful Buddhism took a hit when Buddhist monks in Myanmar led mobs in the 2017 **Rohingya genocide**, in which ~700,000 Muslims fled to Bangladesh and thousands died. Buddhist-Hindu tensions in Sri Lanka fueled a 26-year civil war (1983-2009). Buddhism is not categorically pacifist.
- **The commodification of mindfulness in the West**. Apps, corporate retreats, "McMindfulness." Critics like Ronald Purser argue that mindfulness stripped of Buddhist ethics becomes a productivity tool that helps people tolerate exploitative work rather than question it.
- **The Dalai Lama and Chinese government**. The current Dalai Lama (Tenzin Gyatso, born 1935) fled Tibet in 1959 and has lived in exile in Dharamshala, India. The Chinese government claims the right to approve his next reincarnation, a position most Tibetan Buddhists reject. Succession is an open question.
- **Sexual abuse scandals in Western convert sanghas**. Several prominent teachers (Chögyam Trungpa, Osel Tendzin, Sogyal Rinpoche) were implicated. Western Buddhists are still working through how to handle teacher misconduct in traditions that emphasize devotion.
- **Buddhism without rebirth**. Secular Buddhists in the West often discard rebirth and karma as literal claims while keeping meditation. Asian Buddhists generally find this puzzling.

## What people get wrong

- The Buddha is not a god. Statues show a teacher, not a deity. Some Mahayana traditions later elevated him toward divine status, but the original framing was a human who figured something out.
- Buddhism doesn't necessarily require believing in rebirth literally. Many Western practitioners treat it as metaphor; traditional Asian Buddhism treats it as fact.
- "Zen" is a Japanese form of Mahayana, not the whole religion. The vast majority of Buddhists are not Zen.
- Buddhism isn't categorically nonviolent. Monks have led genocides. The teachings are about ending suffering, not always about pacifism.
- "Nirvana" doesn't mean "bliss" in the popular sense. It literally means "blowing out" (like a candle), the extinction of the fire of craving.

## What to carry away

- A prince renounced his palace, sat under a tree, and worked out a system for ending suffering. That's the founding story.
- The Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path are the operating manual.
- Three branches: Theravada (older, monastic, Southeast Asia), Mahayana (devotional, East Asia, includes Zen), Vajrayana (tantric, Tibet).
- Anatta (no permanent self) is the most distinctive idea and the sharpest break from Hinduism.
- Buddhism is shrinking or stagnant in much of Asia but the only major religion seeing significant Western convert growth.
- In one sentence: Buddhism is a 2,500-year-old psychological technology for ending suffering by understanding that nothing, including you, is permanent.
