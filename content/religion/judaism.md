# Judaism: the small religion whose influence is wildly disproportionate to its size

## What is Judaism and why it matters

Judaism is the world's smallest major religion by population, with about 15 million followers, roughly 0.2% of humanity. It is also the oldest of the three Abrahamic religions and the parent of both Christianity and Islam. Without Judaism, neither of those exists. The Western moral inheritance (one God, ethical law, the Sabbath, the dignity of every person made in the divine image) is Jewish in origin. Jews have also produced an outsized share of the modern world's intellectual output: Freud invented psychoanalysis, Einstein rewrote physics, Marx reframed economics, Spinoza secularized philosophy, the founders of Hollywood and the recording industry were largely Jewish, ~25% of Nobel Prize laureates have been Jewish despite Jews being ~0.2% of the population. Judaism matters not because of size but because of leverage.

## How it started

- Jewish tradition traces the religion to **Abraham**, who is said to have made a covenant with God around 2000 BCE. Historians generally date the recognizable Israelite religion to ~1200 BCE, when a distinct people called Israelites appears in the archaeology of Canaan (modern Israel/Palestine).
- The foundational narrative: God promised Abraham a land and a lineage. Abraham's grandson Jacob was renamed **Israel** ("one who wrestles with God"). His twelve sons became the twelve tribes.
- The tribes ended up enslaved in Egypt. **Moses** led them out (the **Exodus**, traditionally dated ~13th century BCE), received the **Torah** at **Mount Sinai**, and led them toward the promised land.
- **The United Kingdom of Israel (~1000-930 BCE)** under Saul, David, and Solomon. David made Jerusalem the capital. Solomon built the **First Temple**.
- After Solomon the kingdom split into Israel (north) and Judah (south). Israel was destroyed by Assyria in 722 BCE. Judah and the First Temple were destroyed by Babylon in **586 BCE**. The elite were deported (the **Babylonian Exile**).
- They returned 50 years later, rebuilt the **Second Temple** (516 BCE). For the next ~600 years Judah was ruled by Persians, then Greeks (the Maccabean revolt against Greek rulers in 167 BCE is what Hanukkah commemorates), then Romans.
- **70 CE**: Rome crushed a Jewish revolt and destroyed the Second Temple. Another revolt (the Bar Kokhba revolt, 132-135 CE) was also crushed; Jerusalem was renamed and Jews were largely expelled from Judea.
- The result was the **Diaspora**: Jews scattered across the Mediterranean, then Europe, North Africa, the Middle East, eventually the Americas. Without a temple or a homeland, the religion reorganized around the synagogue, the rabbi, and the text.
- **1948**: the modern State of Israel was founded after the **Holocaust** in which the Nazis murdered roughly 6 million Jews, ~two-thirds of European Jewry. For the first time in ~1,900 years there was a Jewish state.

## Core beliefs

- **Strict monotheism**. The most famous Jewish prayer is the **Shema**: "Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one." No Trinity. The single most important theological line in Judaism.
- **Covenant**. God chose the descendants of Abraham as a people in special relationship to him. "Chosen" doesn't mean superior; it means assigned a specific mission (to live ethically and bring God's presence into the world).
- **Torah as God's revealed law**. Not just stories, but law. The **613 commandments (mitzvot)** structure daily life.
- **Messianic age yet to come**. Jews are still waiting for the Messiah. This is the core theological break with Christianity, which says he came already.
- **Tikkun olam ("repairing the world")**. The idea, especially strong in liberal Judaism, that humans partner with God to make the world better.
- **No detailed afterlife doctrine**. Judaism focuses heavily on this life and how to live it; the next life is mentioned but not elaborated.

## Sacred texts

- **The Tanakh**: the Hebrew Bible. An acronym for its three sections: **Torah** (the first five books, also called the Pentateuch), **Nevi'im** (Prophets), **Ketuvim** (Writings). Christians call this the Old Testament; Jews don't.
- **The Torah**: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The foundational five books.
- **The Talmud**: rabbinic interpretation of the Torah, compiled between ~200 and 500 CE. Two versions: the **Jerusalem Talmud** (shorter, less authoritative) and the **Babylonian Talmud** (vastly more influential). The Talmud is the foundational text of post-Temple Judaism. It is not a single argument but a centuries-long recorded conversation.
- **The Mishnah**: oral law written down ~200 CE, forms the core of the Talmud.
- **Midrash**: rabbinic commentary and storytelling on biblical texts.

## Major branches

- **Orthodox (~10% of Jews globally, higher in Israel)**: strict observance of Jewish law as traditionally interpreted. Includes Modern Orthodox (engaged with secular society) and **Haredi** ("ultra-Orthodox," including Hasidic groups), which separate more.
- **Conservative (~18%)**: mid-traditional. Accepts that Jewish law can evolve with historical scholarship, but emphasizes continuity.
- **Reform (~35%)**: the most liberal major branch. Originated in 19th-century Germany. Treats Jewish law as inspiring but not binding. Female rabbis, LGBTQ inclusion, vernacular services.
- **Reconstructionist**: smaller, American-founded, treats Judaism as an evolving civilization rather than a revealed religion.
- **Secular / cultural Jews**: the largest category in many places, including Israel. Identify as Jewish ethnically and culturally without religious practice. Many Israeli Jews are functionally secular but deeply Jewish in identity.

## Demographics and geography

- ~15 million Jews worldwide.
- **Israel**: ~45% of world Jewry (~7 million Jews out of ~9 million total population). The only Jewish-majority country.
- **United States**: ~40% (~6 million), the world's largest Jewish diaspora. Heavily concentrated in New York/New Jersey, California, Florida. Has produced disproportionate influence in academia, finance, entertainment, technology.
- Smaller communities in France (~440,000), Canada (~390,000), the United Kingdom (~290,000), Argentina, Russia, Germany.
- **India**: ~5,000 Jews (~0.001%), mostly remnants of ancient communities in Kerala (Cochin Jews) and Mumbai (Bene Israel), with most having emigrated to Israel.

## Practices and life cycle

- **Shabbat**: the Sabbath, from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown. No work, no driving (for the strictly observant), no electronics. A weekly pause that has shaped the structure of the entire Western week.
- **Kosher (kashrut)**: dietary laws. No pork. No shellfish. Meat and dairy can't be eaten together. Meat must be slaughtered ritually.
- **Prayer**: three times daily for the observant (morning, afternoon, evening). Synagogue services particularly on Shabbat.
- **Brit milah**: circumcision of baby boys on the 8th day.
- **Bar mitzvah / bat mitzvah**: coming-of-age at 13 (boys) or 12-13 (girls), marked by reading from the Torah publicly.
- **Marriage**: traditionally under a chuppah (canopy), with the breaking of a glass at the end (interpretations vary, but a common one is remembering the destruction of the Temple even in joy).
- **Mourning**: shiva, seven days of intense mourning at home, with the community visiting; structured stages stretching for months.
- **High Holy Days**: **Rosh Hashanah** (Jewish New Year, autumn) and **Yom Kippur** (Day of Atonement, the holiest day, a 25-hour fast).
- **Passover (Pesach)**: spring, retells the Exodus from Egypt over a structured family meal called a **seder**.
- **Hanukkah**: the eight-day festival in December commemorating the Maccabean victory and the Temple rededication. Marketed up in the US because of proximity to Christmas; theologically a minor holiday.
- **Sukkot, Shavuot, Purim**: other major festivals.

## Disproportionate influence

- Jews are ~0.2% of the world's population. Yet:
  - ~25% of Nobel Prize laureates have been Jewish (over 200 individuals).
  - Founders of psychoanalysis (Sigmund Freud), modern physics (Albert Einstein), much of modern philosophy (Spinoza, Wittgenstein), American comedy (a list too long to fit), Hollywood (the major studio founders), computing (von Neumann), modern economics (Marx, Friedman, Samuelson, Krugman).
- Why? Heavily debated. Common factors include:
  - Cultural emphasis on literacy and textual study (Jews achieved near-universal male literacy ~1500 years before the rest of Europe).
  - Centuries of urbanization (Jews were often forced into cities and into mobile professions like trade, finance, medicine).
  - Selection pressure from persecution (those who survived often were the ones who could move fast and learn fast).
  - Concentration in modernity-friendly places (Vienna, Berlin, New York, Tel Aviv) at the right moments.
- No serious scholar argues for genetic explanations; the data on group IQ differences is widely contested and doesn't explain the magnitude.

## Modern controversies

- **Israel-Palestine**. The most charged ongoing political conflict involving Judaism. Founded in 1948 in part as a response to the Holocaust, Israel's establishment displaced ~700,000 Palestinians (**the Nakba**). The conflict has shaped global politics ever since. The 2023-2024 Gaza war, following the **October 7, 2023** Hamas attack that killed ~1,200 Israelis, has further polarized global opinion.
- **Resurgent antisemitism**. Post-October 7 saw record spikes in antisemitic incidents in the US, Europe, Australia. Universities, especially elite American ones, have been a particular flashpoint.
- **Orthodox vs secular tensions in Israel**. The ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) have historically been exempt from military service while receiving state subsidies; this is increasingly unpopular with secular Israelis who serve and pay taxes.
- **Who is a Jew?** Traditionally defined matrilineally (Jewish if your mother is). Reform Judaism accepts patrilineal descent. Conversion rules vary by branch. Israel's "Law of Return" gives citizenship to anyone with one Jewish grandparent, broader than religious definition.
- **Zionism vs anti-Zionism within Judaism itself**. A growing minority of Jews, especially younger and more progressive, distinguish Jewish identity from support for the Israeli state.

## What people get wrong

- Judaism is an ethnicity, a religion, and a culture, all at once. A person can be ethnically and culturally Jewish without being religiously practicing. This is hard to translate into Christian or Muslim categories where religion is purely belief-based.
- Jews don't proselytize. Conversion is possible but historically discouraged (you have to ask three times). This is the opposite of Christianity and Islam and is part of why Judaism stayed so small.
- The Old Testament is not "the Jewish Bible." It's the Christian framing. Jews call it the Tanakh, and it ends differently.
- Israel is not Judaism. About half of all Jews live outside Israel. Conflating the state with the religion is one of the most common errors in current discourse.
- Hasidic and ultra-Orthodox Jews are a small (though visible) minority. Most Jews globally are not strictly observant.
- Antisemitism is not "racism toward Jews" in the way American racism is usually framed. It is older, more conspiratorial, and tends to portray Jews as both controlling elites and racial inferiors at the same time. Theodor Adorno called this "the rumor about the Jews."

## What to carry away

- ~15 million people. World's smallest major religion. Parent of Christianity and Islam.
- Strict monotheism, Torah as law, 613 commandments, no afterlife focus, Messiah yet to come.
- ~1,900 years of Diaspora reshaped the religion around text, synagogue, and rabbi instead of temple and priest.
- Roughly half of all Jews live in Israel today, roughly 40% in the US.
- Three big modern branches: Orthodox, Conservative, Reform. Plus a huge secular-cultural Jewish identity that doesn't map onto religion.
- Judaism's influence in modern intellectual and cultural life is wildly out of proportion to its numbers, for reasons historians still debate.
- In one sentence: Judaism is a 3,000-year-old monotheistic religion of a small people whose ideas became the foundation of most of the modern Western world.
