# Climate zones and biomes: why some places are deserts and others jungles

## What climate zones are and why they matter

Climate zones are the broad patterns of temperature and rainfall that shape where you can grow food, where people live, and what an ecosystem looks like. They are not random. They follow physics: solar angle, atmospheric circulation, ocean currents, and the position of mountains. Once you see the pattern, the world stops looking like a scatter of jungles and deserts and starts looking like a system. The same physics explains why the **Sahara**, the **Arabian desert**, the **Sonoran**, the **Atacama**, the **Kalahari**, and the **Australian Outback** all sit at roughly the same latitude. They are the same phenomenon, repeated.

## The driver: sun angle plus circulation

- The Earth's tilt and curvature mean the sun hits the equator most directly and the poles most obliquely. The equator absorbs more energy. The poles lose more than they gain.
- Hot air at the equator rises. As it rises, it cools and dumps its moisture, which is why the equator runs a belt of rainforest: the **Amazon**, the **Congo**, the islands of **Indonesia**.
- That risen air spreads north and south at altitude. It sinks back down at about **30 degrees latitude**, dry and warm. Sinking dry air kills clouds. This creates the world's deserts.
- This circulation is called a **Hadley cell**, named after the 18th-century English meteorologist George Hadley.
- The pattern: wet at the equator, dry at 30 degrees, then a different pattern at higher latitudes.

The 30-degree desert belt is the cleanest illustration in all of geography. Sahara, Arabian, Thar (northwest India), Sonoran (US Southwest and northern Mexico), Atacama (northern Chile), Kalahari (southern Africa), Australian Outback. All sitting on the same parallel, all caused by the same atmospheric mechanism.

## What happens at other latitudes

- **0 to 10 degrees (equatorial).** Hot, wet, year-round. Tropical rainforest. Diverse ecosystems. Hard agriculture because the soil leaches nutrients quickly and pests thrive.
- **10 to 30 degrees (tropical to subtropical).** Mostly hot and dry, with monsoon exceptions. The desert belt sits here.
- **30 to 60 degrees (mid-latitudes).** Westerly winds dominate. Most of the world's developed economies sit in this band: the US, Europe, China, Japan, Korea, Argentina, Chile, Australia, New Zealand. Temperate climate. Distinct seasons. Reliable rainfall in most places. Good agriculture.
- **60 to 90 degrees (polar).** Cold all year. Tundra and ice. Almost no population.

## Why coasts and continental interiors differ

- Water heats and cools much more slowly than land. Oceans moderate temperature.
- **London** and **Moscow** sit at about the same latitude (51 and 55 degrees N). London winters average 5 to 8 degrees Celsius. Moscow winters average minus 6 to minus 10. The difference is the ocean.
- Coastal cities have mild winters and cool summers. Continental interiors have brutal winters and hot summers. Chicago and Beijing both sit deep in continental landmasses and both swing 30+ degrees between summer and winter.

## Mountain rain shadows

- Mountains force air upward. Rising air cools and dumps rain on the windward side. The leeward side gets the dry leftovers.
- The **Himalayas** catch the monsoon coming north from the Indian Ocean. North of the Himalayas sits the **Gobi desert** and the dry Tibetan Plateau, in the rain shadow.
- The **Cascade Mountains** in the Pacific Northwest catch wet Pacific air. Seattle and Portland are famously rainy. Drive two hours east over the Cascades and you hit Eastern Washington, which is dry grassland and high desert.
- Same physics in the **Andes** (dry Patagonia in the rain shadow), the **Sierra Nevada** (the Great Basin desert behind it), and the **Atlas Mountains** (the Sahara extends from their southern flank).

## The major biomes you should recognize

- **Tropical rainforest.** Amazon, Congo basin, Southeast Asian islands (Indonesia, Borneo, New Guinea). Hot, wet year-round. Highest biodiversity on the planet.
- **Tropical savanna.** Grass with scattered trees. Wet and dry seasons. The Serengeti, much of central Africa, parts of India and Brazil. Big herbivores, big predators.
- **Desert.** The 30-degree belt described above, plus the polar deserts.
- **Mediterranean.** Wet winters, dry summers. Found in five places on Earth: the **Mediterranean basin** (southern Europe, North Africa, the Levant), **California**, central **Chile**, the southwestern tip of **South Africa** (Cape Town), and southwestern **Australia** (Perth). Olives, citrus, wine, and disproportionately pleasant cities.
- **Temperate forest.** Eastern US, most of Europe, parts of East Asia. Deciduous trees, four seasons, dense human population, the place industrial economies grew up.
- **Grassland.** The American Great Plains, the Russian and Ukrainian steppe, the Argentine Pampas, the Hungarian Puszta. Wheat, cattle, and historically the empires of horse nomads.
- **Taiga (boreal forest).** A continuous band of evergreen forest across northern Canada, Scandinavia, and Russia. Cold, dark winters. Largest biome by area on Earth.
- **Tundra.** Treeless, frozen subsoil, just enough summer thaw for moss and short grass. Northern Alaska, Canada, Siberia, Greenland.

## Why this matters in practice: India's monsoon

The clearest example of climate driving everything else is the **South Asian monsoon**.

In summer, the Indian landmass heats up faster than the Indian Ocean. Hot rising air over land pulls in moist air from the ocean. That moist air slams into the Himalayas, has to rise, cools, and dumps rain. From roughly June through September, this delivers about **75% of India's annual precipitation**. More than 600 million Indians farm rain-fed land. The monsoon is not a weather story. It is a food security story, a GDP story, and a political story.

A weak monsoon means crop failure, food inflation, and rural distress. A strong monsoon means surplus grain and reservoirs that carry the country to the next year. Climate change is now making the monsoon less predictable: shorter, more intense rainfall events instead of steady seasonal rain. The same physics that feeds India also makes it vulnerable.

## What to carry away

- The geography of climate is physics, not randomness. Sun angle plus atmospheric circulation plus oceans plus mountains.
- The 30-degree desert belt is the single best illustration: same latitude, same mechanism, same outcome on six different continents.
- Coastal climates are mild, continental climates are extreme, mountains create wet sides and dry sides.
- Where people live, what they grow, and how their economies developed mostly sit downstream of these patterns. India's monsoon is the clearest example: a single climate feature feeds 600 million people.
- Climate change is now stressing every one of these patterns, which is why geography is becoming news in a way it hasn't been for a century.
