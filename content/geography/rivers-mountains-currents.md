# Rivers, mountains, and currents: the geography under geopolitics

## Why these matter

Three physical features quietly run the world: rivers, mountains, and ocean currents. Rivers gave civilizations their start and still carry most of the food we eat. Mountains decide where borders sit and where armies stop. Ocean currents move heat around the planet and decide which coasts are habitable and which are deserts. Once you see these layers, the daily news makes more sense. Why does **Russia** keep fighting over **Ukraine**? Why is **Taiwan** so contested? Why does **Iran** sit on a choke point that the US Navy guards? All of these are downstream of the same three features.

## Rivers as civilization engines

Every early civilization grew up along a river. The pattern is so consistent it is almost a law.

- **Tigris and Euphrates.** Mesopotamia, modern Iraq. The first cities (Ur, Uruk, Babylon), the first writing (cuneiform, around 3200 BCE), the first law codes (Hammurabi, around 1750 BCE).
- **Nile.** Egypt. The longest river in the world at about 6,650 km. The annual flood deposited fertile silt every year, which is why Egyptian civilization stayed in one place for 3,000 years.
- **Indus.** The Indus Valley Civilization, around 2600 to 1900 BCE, in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. Cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro had urban planning and drainage that European cities would not match for another 3,000 years.
- **Yellow River and Yangtze.** Chinese civilization. The Yellow River, "China's sorrow," flooded so often it changed course completely several times. The Yangtze is the third longest river in the world and carries most of China's internal shipping.
- **Ganges.** The sacred river of Hindu India. Supports about 600 million people in its basin, the most populated river basin on Earth.
- **Amazon.** Not a civilization engine in the ancient sense, but the largest river by water discharge by a wide margin. Carries about 20% of all freshwater that reaches the world's oceans.

## Why rivers still run trade

Rivers are not just history. They are the cheapest way to move heavy goods.

- The **Mississippi River system**, including its tributaries, drains about 40% of the continental US. It carries roughly 60% of US grain exports out through New Orleans to the world. That is one reason the US has been a food superpower for 150 years: the world's best farmland with a natural conveyor belt built into it.
- The **Rhine** runs from Switzerland through Germany and the Netherlands to the North Sea, and is the busiest waterway in Europe. The German industrial economy was built along it.
- The **Danube** flows through 10 countries from Germany to the Black Sea. It has been a trade route and a contested border for 2,000 years.
- Rivers also make borders. The **Rio Grande** is the US-Mexico border for about half its length. The **Mekong** is part of the border between Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and Vietnam.

## Mountains as barriers

Mountains stop armies, divide cultures, and isolate populations.

- The **Himalayas** are the highest range on Earth, with **Mount Everest** at 8,849 meters. They separate the Indian subcontinent from China. Tibet sits on a high plateau north of them, isolated for most of history. Indian and Chinese armies have only fought one significant war (1962), partly because moving troops over the Himalayas is brutally hard.
- The **Andes** run almost the entire length of South America, about 7,000 km. They separate the wet Amazon basin from the dry Pacific coast. The Inca Empire used the Andean spine as its corridor.
- The **Alps** cut Europe in two, separating Italy from northern Europe culturally and linguistically. Hannibal famously crossed them with elephants in 218 BCE. Napoleon and the Romans both built passes through them.
- The **Rocky Mountains** divide the US into the moister east and the drier west.
- The **Atlas Mountains** separate the Mediterranean coast of North Africa from the Sahara.

Mountain passes are where the action happens. The **Khyber Pass** between Afghanistan and Pakistan has been the invasion route into India for 3,000 years (Alexander, the Mughals, the British). The **Brenner Pass** between Italy and Austria has been the main Alpine crossing since Roman times. Holding a pass mattered more than holding the mountains.

## Ocean currents and why London is not Moscow

Ocean currents move enormous amounts of heat around the planet. They are the climate system's plumbing.

- The **Gulf Stream** is a warm current that flows from the Gulf of Mexico north along the US East Coast, then bends across the Atlantic to Western Europe. It is the reason **London** (51 degrees N) has mild winters while **Moscow** (55 degrees N) freezes. **New York City** (41 degrees N, much further south) has colder winters than London because the Gulf Stream warms Europe instead of the US East Coast at that latitude.
- The **Kuroshio Current** is the Pacific equivalent, a warm current flowing north past Japan. It warms Japan and feeds the rich fisheries off its coast.
- The **Humboldt Current** is a cold current flowing north along the Pacific coast of South America. It cools the air above it, which means the air cannot hold much moisture, which means little rain falls on the coast. This is one major reason the **Atacama desert** in northern Chile is the driest place on Earth outside the polar regions. The same current feeds the cold, nutrient-rich waters around the **Galapagos Islands**, which is why Galapagos has penguins almost on the equator and the biodiversity that helped Darwin write the *Origin of Species*.

## Choke points: where global trade narrows

A choke point is a narrow waterway that a huge share of global trade must pass through. These are where geography becomes geopolitics.

- **Strait of Hormuz.** Between Iran and Oman. About 20% of the world's oil passes through here. The US Navy patrols it constantly. When Iran threatens to close it, oil prices jump.
- **Bab-el-Mandeb.** Between Yemen and Djibouti, the southern entrance to the Red Sea. Houthi attacks here in 2023 and 2024 disrupted global shipping for months.
- **Suez Canal.** Connects the Mediterranean to the Red Sea. About 12% of world trade passes through. When the *Ever Given* container ship blocked it for six days in March 2021, global supply chains felt it for months.
- **Strait of Malacca.** Between Malaysia and Indonesia, connecting the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea. About a quarter of world trade passes through, including most of China's oil imports. This is the Malacca Dilemma: China is dependent on a strait the US Navy could close.
- **Bosphorus.** The strait through Istanbul, connecting the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Russia's main warm-water access. Turkey controls it under the 1936 Montreux Convention.
- **Panama Canal.** Connects Atlantic to Pacific without sailing around South America. Currently under stress because of drought reducing the freshwater needed to operate the locks.

## Why "Prisoners of Geography" landed

Tim Marshall's 2015 book *Prisoners of Geography* became a global bestseller because it argued, with concrete examples, that physical geography sets the default options for nations more than most modern political analysis acknowledges. Russia's defensive paranoia, China's Pacific obsession, America's lucky two-ocean position, Africa's poor harbor situation, all sit on top of geographic facts that don't change when leaders do.

The argument is not that geography determines everything. It is that geography sets the floor and the ceiling of what is possible, and political leaders work within that. Reading the daily news without the geography layer is reading the surface.

## What to carry away

- Rivers built civilizations and still carry the world's food and goods.
- Mountains decide where borders sit and where armies stop, and the passes through them are where history happens.
- Ocean currents move so much heat that London is warmer than New York at a far higher latitude.
- A handful of choke points carry most of global trade, and whoever controls them has leverage over everyone else.
- The next time the news mentions Hormuz or Malacca or the Bosphorus, you will know what is at stake.
