# Canonical US TV: the prestige era and what got it there

## What prestige TV is and why it matters

For most of the 20th century, American TV was the lesser cousin of film. Sitcoms, soaps, procedurals. Then in 1999, HBO aired the first episode of *The Sopranos*, and within a decade TV had become where the most ambitious American storytelling lived. The novelistic, long-form, morally messy shows that followed earned the label "prestige TV," and they're now the cultural shorthand that conversations about storytelling, masculinity, capitalism, and family all run through. If someone says "it's a real Succession dynamic" or "Walter White energy" and you don't know what they mean, half the joke is gone. Below is the canon.

## How prestige TV started: 1999 to 2010

- **The Sopranos (HBO, 1999-2007).** Created by **David Chase**. **James Gandolfini** as Tony Soprano, a New Jersey mob boss who goes to therapy for panic attacks. The first show where audiences accepted a protagonist who was actively, repeatedly bad and stayed bad. The finale cut to black mid-scene and became one of the most-debated endings in television history. Without this show, nothing else on this list exists.
- **The Wire (HBO, 2002-2008).** Created by **David Simon**, a former Baltimore Sun reporter. Each of the five seasons focuses on a different American institution: the drug trade (season 1), the docks and working-class decline (2), city politics (3), the school system (4), and the news media (5). Often called the greatest TV show ever made. Modest viewership at the time. Now considered a structural critique of American urban life as serious as anything in literature.
- **Mad Men (AMC, 2007-2015).** Created by **Matt Weiner**, a former Sopranos writer. Set inside a 1960s NYC ad agency. **Don Draper** (Jon Hamm) is a self-invented man whose secret past slowly catches up with him. Won the Emmy for Best Drama four years running. Lower viewership than its prestige reputation suggests. Defined the visual language of 2010s style.
- **Breaking Bad (AMC, 2008-2013).** Created by **Vince Gilligan**. **Bryan Cranston** as Walter White, a high school chemistry teacher diagnosed with terminal cancer who starts cooking meth. **Aaron Paul** as his former student Jesse Pinkman. The Pitch Vince Gilligan gave was "Mr. Chips becomes Scarface." The transformation arc is the cleanest example of a character study in modern TV.

## The HBO peak and the streaming wave

- **Game of Thrones (HBO, 2011-2019).** Based on **George R. R. Martin**'s novels. The biggest cultural moment in TV history. Sunday-night appointment viewing for almost a decade. The final season collapsed under its own weight and the petition to remake it gathered 1.7 million signatures. Even the failure was a cultural event.
- **Succession (HBO, 2018-2023).** Created by **Jesse Armstrong**. **Logan Roy** (Brian Cox) and his four children fight over who inherits a Murdoch-style media empire. **Jeremy Strong**'s method acting as Kendall Roy became its own discussion topic. Peak prestige TV of the late era. Won Best Drama three times. The finale of season 3 ("ludicrously capacious bag") and the death episode in season 4 ("Connor's Wedding") are the most-discussed single episodes of the 2020s so far.
- **Stranger Things (Netflix, 2016-present).** The first Netflix show that became a true mass-culture phenomenon. 80s nostalgia, kids on bikes, Demogorgons. Made **Millie Bobby Brown** a household name. Cracked the door open for streaming as a prestige home.
- **The Crown (Netflix, 2016-2023).** Six seasons covering Queen Elizabeth II's reign. Multiple actresses (Claire Foy, Olivia Colman, Imelda Staunton) playing her across decades. Made the British royal family a Netflix-driven cultural fixture in America.
- **Ted Lasso (Apple TV+, 2020-2023).** **Jason Sudeikis** as an American football coach hired to coach a struggling English Premier League soccer team. Premiered in the pandemic. Became the comfort show of the early 2020s.
- **Severance (Apple TV+, 2022-present).** Created by **Dan Erickson**. Office workers undergo a procedure that splits their work selves from their home selves. The best workplace allegory since Mad Men. The season 2 finale in 2025 was the most-discussed TV moment of that year.
- **The Bear (FX/Hulu, 2022-present).** **Jeremy Allen White** as a Michelin-starred chef who returns to Chicago to run his late brother's beef sandwich shop. Won the Emmy for Best Comedy three years running, despite barely qualifying as comedy. The episode "Fishes" (Christmas dinner with the family) is a single-episode classic.

## The sitcom tradition

Prestige drama dominates the canon, but American sitcoms shaped daily speech and humor more than anything else:

- **Seinfeld (NBC, 1989-1998).** Created by **Jerry Seinfeld** and **Larry David**. Marketed as "the show about nothing." Four friends in NYC navigating petty social grievances. Reshaped American comedy structure. Lines like "yada yada yada" and "not that there's anything wrong with that" are in common speech.
- **The Office (NBC, 2005-2013).** US adaptation of the British show, which Ricky Gervais created. Initially seen as inferior to the UK original. By season 2, Steve Carell's Michael Scott and a deep writers' room had pushed it past. Now the most-streamed sitcom on Netflix and Peacock, particularly with Gen Z, decades after release.
- **Parks and Recreation (NBC, 2009-2015).** Created by **Greg Daniels** and **Michael Schur**. Amy Poehler as Leslie Knope, a relentlessly optimistic mid-level government employee in fictional Pawnee, Indiana. The comfort sitcom of the 2010s.
- **Community (NBC/Yahoo, 2009-2015).** Created by **Dan Harmon**. A study group at a community college that becomes a meta-comedy laboratory. Cult following. Donald Glover, Alison Brie, and Joel McHale all came through it.

## Why the prestige era happened

- **HBO and then AMC** found that subscribers and ad buyers would tolerate slower, darker, more novelistic storytelling than broadcast TV ever could. The economics changed.
- **The writers' room** as the creative engine, modeled on Sopranos and refined by Breaking Bad and Mad Men, became the standard structure for serious TV.
- **The streaming era (post-2013)** expanded the buyers and changed the economics again. Netflix released full seasons at once. Binge-watching reshaped how stories got built.
- The **algorithmic streaming era** of the late 2020s has fragmented the audience. There's no single show everyone is watching the way they were watching Sopranos or Game of Thrones. The cost of that is fewer shared cultural moments.

## What to carry away

- The prestige era runs roughly 1999 to today and is anchored by a handful of HBO and AMC shows that taught TV to be ambitious.
- The four shows you should know cold: **The Sopranos**, **Breaking Bad**, **Mad Men**, **Succession**. The rest builds on those.
- Sitcoms quote into everyday speech more than dramas do. *Seinfeld* and *The Office* lines pop up constantly.
- In one sentence: American TV got serious in 1999, the prestige era ran for 25 years, and the canonical shows are the cultural reference frame American conversation runs on.
