# US music history: blues to jazz to rock to country to hip-hop, the through-line

## What this map is and why it matters

American popular music is the most influential cultural export the country has produced. Almost every popular genre on the global charts today traces back to a few founding traditions in the American South and East. The single most important fact: most of those traditions came from Black American musicians, and almost every subsequent genre, including the ones that get coded as white, evolved from that root or borrowed heavily from it. Below is the through-line from the Mississippi Delta in the 1890s to the streaming charts in 2025, with the names and dates worth knowing.

## Blues: the root

- Born in the **Mississippi Delta** in the **late 1800s**, fused from Black American spirituals, work songs sung in cotton fields, and West African musical traditions carried over through slavery.
- The form is mostly **12-bar blues**: a chord pattern simple enough that anyone can play it and infinite enough that no two songs sound the same.
- **Robert Johnson (1911-1938)** is the legend at the center. Mississippi guitarist who recorded only 29 songs before dying at 27. The myth says he sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads for his guitar ability. He shaped every rock guitarist who came later.
- **Muddy Waters** electrified the blues in 1940s Chicago, turning acoustic Delta blues into the electric urban sound that became rock's direct parent.

## Jazz: the sophistication

- Started in **New Orleans around 1900**, a port city where Black, Creole, French, Spanish, and African traditions mixed. The brass-band tradition and the blues collided.
- **Louis Armstrong (1901-1971)**, born in poverty in New Orleans, became the single most influential figure in jazz. His trumpet phrasing taught generations how to swing. He was also one of the first American Black musicians to become a global star.
- **Duke Ellington** led the most famous big band of the 1930s and 1940s and composed thousands of pieces. Took jazz from dance music to concert hall.
- **Bebop** emerged in the 1940s, faster and harder to dance to. **Charlie Parker** on saxophone and **Dizzy Gillespie** on trumpet led it. Jazz became cerebral.
- **Miles Davis** is the towering figure of mid-century jazz. The album *Kind of Blue* (1959) is the best-selling jazz record ever and the one almost every Boomer claims to own. Davis reinvented his sound every decade: cool jazz in the 50s, modal jazz in the late 50s, fusion in the 70s.

## Country: the rural cousin

- Came out of **Appalachian folk music**, English and Scots-Irish ballads, and the blues. Fused in the **1920s** in the South.
- **Jimmie Rodgers**, the "Singing Brakeman," cut some of the first commercial country records in 1927.
- **Hank Williams** in the late 1940s wrote the songbook modern country still draws from. Died at 29 in the back of a car on the way to a New Year's show.
- **Johnny Cash** brought country to a mainstream audience in the 1950s and 60s. The Folsom Prison concert in 1968 is the canonical recording.
- **Dolly Parton** is the genre's living matriarch. Wrote "I Will Always Love You" (the song Whitney Houston later turned into the best-selling single by a female artist ever) and Jolene the same week.
- The current moment: **Morgan Wallen** is the biggest-selling country artist of the 2020s. **Zach Bryan** brought a more acoustic, lyrical version back to the mainstream. **Beyonce**'s 2024 album *Cowboy Carter* opened a conversation about country's Black roots that had been pushed out of the genre's marketing for half a century.

## Rock and roll: the fusion

- **Rock and roll** is what happened when **blues** and **country** collided in the **1950s**. The first true mass youth genre in American history.
- **Chuck Berry** and **Little Richard** are the originators, both Black, both writing the rock template that white artists copied.
- **Elvis Presley** took the sound mainstream in 1956. The cultural force was enormous and the racial dynamics of credit-taking are still debated.
- **The British Invasion (1964)** brought the sound back to America through **The Beatles** and **The Rolling Stones**, both bands of British kids obsessed with American blues and R&B.
- The 1970s gave **arena rock** (Led Zeppelin, Pink Floyd), **funk** (Parliament-Funkadelic, Sly Stone), and the first wave of **punk** (The Ramones, Television in NYC, then The Clash and Sex Pistols in London).
- The 1980s were the **MTV era**. Michael Jackson, Madonna, Prince, Bruce Springsteen.
- The 1990s gave **grunge** (Nirvana, Pearl Jam, Soundgarden, all from Seattle). **Kurt Cobain**'s death in 1994 closed an era. After that, rock as the dominant cultural genre slowly faded.

## Soul and R&B: the parallel river

- **Motown Records** founded by **Berry Gordy** in Detroit in 1959 was the assembly line that produced **The Supremes**, **The Temptations**, **Marvin Gaye**, **Stevie Wonder**, and **Smokey Robinson**.
- **Aretha Franklin** is the voice. The 1967 recording of "Respect" is the high-water mark of the genre.
- Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On* (1971) turned soul political and remains one of the most-covered albums in any genre.
- Soul and R&B never disappeared. They evolved into 80s and 90s R&B (Whitney Houston, Mariah Carey, Boyz II Men), into 2000s neo-soul (D'Angelo, Erykah Badu), and into the current generation (SZA, Daniel Caesar, Beyonce in her solo work).

## Hip-hop: the dominant genre

- Born **August 11, 1973**, at a back-to-school party in the Bronx where **DJ Kool Herc** extended the instrumental breaks of funk records by switching between two turntables. That's the founding moment.
- **The Sugarhill Gang**'s "Rapper's Delight" (1979) was the first hip-hop record to chart.
- **Run-DMC** and **LL Cool J** in the mid-80s brought hip-hop to MTV.
- **N.W.A.** (1988) and **Public Enemy** (late 80s) made hip-hop overtly political and racially confrontational.
- The 1990s were the **East Coast / West Coast** era: **Tupac Shakur** in LA, **The Notorious B.I.G.** in Brooklyn. Both were murdered (Tupac in 1996, Biggie in 1997). Their rivalry shaped a generation of rap.
- **OutKast** out of Atlanta in the 2000s shifted the center of gravity South. *Speakerboxxx/The Love Below* (2003) won Album of the Year at the Grammys, the first hip-hop album to do so.
- **Kanye West**'s 2004-2013 run (College Dropout to Yeezus) is the most influential creative arc in hip-hop. Everything after has been reacting to it.
- **Kendrick Lamar** is the artistic standard-bearer of the 2010s and 2020s. *To Pimp a Butterfly* (2015) is widely considered the most important hip-hop album of the decade. His 2024 beef with Drake produced "Not Like Us," which became the song of the year and won five Grammys.
- **In 2017**, hip-hop became the **#1 streamed genre** in the US, passing rock for the first time. It has stayed there.

## The through-line

- Blues created jazz, jazz fed rock, rock split into pop and metal, soul became R&B, R&B fed hip-hop, hip-hop ate everything.
- Black American musicians sit at the origin of almost every genre on this map. Country is the closest exception, and even there the banjo is West African and the form borrows directly from blues.
- The borrowing across racial lines, with credit often denied, is one of the most-discussed dynamics in American cultural history. It's also why "American music" as a category is so global. The roots ran in every direction.

## What to carry away

- The chain runs **blues → jazz → R&B → rock → soul → hip-hop**, with country running alongside and crossing over constantly.
- The single most important figure across the whole map is whoever you ask: blues people say Robert Johnson, jazz people say Miles Davis, soul people say Aretha, hip-hop people say Kendrick Lamar.
- In one sentence: American music is mostly the long downstream of Black American innovation, and once you see the through-line, every pop song you hear becomes legible.
