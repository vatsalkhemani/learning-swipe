# The computing arc: from transistor to AI, in one sweep

## What the computing arc is and why it matters

The computing arc is the seventy-five year run from a single switched device to machines that talk back. It starts with a glass vacuum tube the size of a fist and ends with neural networks that pass the bar exam. Most people experience the arc one era at a time, as if each shift were permanent. The pattern says otherwise. Mainframes felt like the final form. So did the PC. So did the web. So did mobile. Each was a platform that mattered enormously, and each was eventually swallowed by what came next. Understanding the arc matters because we are living inside another shift right now, and the temptation is again to call it the destination.

## The vacuum tube era

- The first general-purpose electronic computer was **ENIAC**, switched on at the University of Pennsylvania in **1946**. Eighteen thousand vacuum tubes, thirty tons, paid for by the US Army to compute artillery firing tables.
- Tubes were the bottleneck. They burned out constantly. They ran hot. A serious machine needed an air-conditioned room and a maintenance team.
- The era's logic was simple. If you wanted more computing, you bought more tubes. There was no Moore's Law yet, no doubling. Just larger rooms.

## The transistor

- In December **1947** at **Bell Labs** in New Jersey, three physicists demonstrated a tiny solid-state switch. **John Bardeen**, **Walter Brattain**, and team leader **William Shockley**. They won the **Nobel Prize in 1956** for it.
- A transistor did what a tube did. Switched current on and off. But it was thousands of times smaller, used a fraction of the power, and did not burn out.
- Bell Labs licensed it widely on government pressure, which is the single most important reason the technology spread. The science of computing was about to become a manufacturing problem.

## The integrated circuit

- The next move was to put many transistors on one piece of silicon. Two engineers solved it in parallel.
- **Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments** built the first working integrated circuit in **1958**. A few transistors on a sliver of germanium, wired by hand.
- **Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor** built a cleaner version in **1959** using silicon and a planar process that could be manufactured at scale. Noyce's design is the ancestor of every chip you have ever used.
- Both are usually given joint credit. Kilby got the Nobel in 2000. Noyce had died in 1990 and was ineligible.

## Moore's Law

- In **1965**, Fairchild's research director **Gordon Moore** wrote a four-page article in *Electronics* magazine noticing that the number of transistors on a chip was doubling roughly every year. He later revised the estimate to every two years.
- This was an observation, not a law. It became prescriptive. The industry organized its roadmaps around it. Chip designers, equipment makers, software architects all assumed Moore's Law would hold and planned accordingly.
- It held for over fifty years. A 1971 Intel 4004 had 2,300 transistors. A modern Apple M-series chip has tens of billions. The doubling has slowed since around 2015 and is now widely considered effectively over, which is one reason GPUs and specialized AI chips matter so much.

## Mainframes

- The dominant computing form of the 1960s and 1970s was the room-sized mainframe, sold mostly by IBM.
- The **IBM System/360**, launched in **1964**, was the bet-the-company moment for IBM. One compatible family of machines across all sizes. It standardized commercial computing and made IBM the most powerful technology company in the world for two decades.
- Mainframes were owned by institutions, not people. You did not own a computer. Your university or company did. You submitted jobs and waited.

## The personal computer

- The **Altair 8800** in **1975** is usually called the first personal computer. A hobbyist kit sold by mail order through *Popular Electronics*. Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC interpreter for it, which became Microsoft.
- The **Apple II** in **1977** turned the PC into a real product. Color graphics, a keyboard, an actual case. Steve Wozniak designed it. Steve Jobs sold it.
- The **IBM PC** in **1981** legitimized personal computing for business. IBM, in a hurry, licensed the operating system from a small company called Microsoft instead of writing their own. That decision quietly transferred the economic center of computing from hardware to software over the next decade.

## The internet

- **ARPANET**, the US Defense Department's research network, sent its first message in **1969**. Four nodes at four universities.
- **TCP/IP**, the protocol that makes the modern internet work, was standardized in **1983**. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn did the core design in the 1970s.
- The **World Wide Web** was invented by **Tim Berners-Lee at CERN** in **1989** and released publicly in 1991. The web is one application running on top of the internet. People still confuse the two.
- The 1990s were the commercial buildout. Netscape, Yahoo, AOL, the dot-com boom and bust, and underneath it, the slow construction of broadband.

## Mobile

- The **iPhone** in **2007** is the cleanest single date in the arc. Before it, mobile was a fragmented mess of carriers and feature phones. After it, mobile was a software platform that ate desktop usage in under a decade.
- Android followed in 2008. Together they reorganized commerce, media, transportation, and dating.
- By 2015 most internet traffic was mobile. Most of the world's first computer was now a phone, not a PC.

## The cloud

- **Amazon Web Services** launched **S3** in **2006**, then EC2 the same year. Renting storage and compute by the hour was a small idea that became the substrate for the next decade of software.
- Startups stopped buying servers. They paid AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud and shipped faster.
- The cloud is why a four-person team can now serve a hundred million users. It is also why three American companies sit at the chokepoint of most of the world's software.

## The AI moment

- AI had two earlier hype cycles, the 1960s and the 1980s, both followed by winters when funding dried up.
- The unlock came in **2012** when a deep neural network called **AlexNet**, trained on GPUs by Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton at the University of Toronto, won the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a huge margin. Deep learning was suddenly the right answer.
- Ten years of scaling later, OpenAI released **ChatGPT in November 2022** and the public finally saw what the researchers had been seeing. A hundred million users in two months. The fastest consumer product adoption in history.

## The throughline

- Every era thought it was the destination. The mainframe people thought the PC was a toy. The PC people thought the web was a fad. The desktop people thought mobile was a phone. The mobile people thought AI was a feature.
- Each shift compressed the previous one into a layer underneath. Mainframes did not die. They became the database tier under web apps. PCs did not die. They became development machines for mobile and cloud.
- The current bet on AI is the same shape. It may be a feature. It may be a platform. We will know in ten years which one it was.
- The lesson worth carrying: when you are inside a shift it always feels final. It almost never is.
