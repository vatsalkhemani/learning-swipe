# How Silicon Valley actually formed: Fairchild, Intel, Apple, Microsoft

## What Silicon Valley is and why its origin matters

Silicon Valley is a forty-mile strip of suburbs south of San Francisco that, between roughly 1955 and 1985, became the densest concentration of technical talent and risk capital in human history. It did not happen by accident and it did not happen because of weather. A small set of decisions, a sympathetic university, a quirk of California labor law, and one terrible manager set up a chain reaction. The origin matters because the playbook still runs. Stanford still pushes founders out the door. Venture capital still clusters on a single road. Engineers still leave and start competitors. Understanding how the place was built explains why it is so hard to copy.

## The pre-history: Stanford and Terman

- **Frederick Terman** was an electrical engineering professor at **Stanford** who took over as dean of engineering in the 1940s. He had a strange idea for an academic.
- He pushed students to start companies near Stanford instead of taking jobs back east. Two early ones were **Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard**, whose 1939 garage company in Palo Alto became HP.
- Terman set up the **Stanford Industrial Park** in **1951** on Stanford land, leased cheap to tech companies, on the condition they hire Stanford grads and collaborate on research. It is the original tech park and the model for every imitator since.
- The combination of a serious research university actively manufacturing companies and the cheap dry warehouses of the South Bay was unusual. Most universities in 1950 wanted nothing to do with commerce.

## Shockley and the traitorous eight

- **William Shockley**, the Nobel laureate who co-invented the transistor at Bell Labs, moved to Mountain View in **1956** to start **Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory**. He picked the location to be near his ailing mother in Palo Alto.
- He recruited brilliantly. He managed terribly. Paranoid, abusive, obsessed with lie detector tests for staff, dismissive of his best engineers' ideas.
- In **1957** eight of his top researchers quit together and walked across the valley to start a new company funded by Sherman Fairchild's east coast camera firm. The eight were Robert Noyce, Gordon Moore, Jean Hoerni, Eugene Kleiner, Jay Last, Sheldon Roberts, Victor Grinich, and Julius Blank.
- Shockley called them the **traitorous eight**. The name stuck. Their new company was **Fairchild Semiconductor**.

## Fairchild as the mother company

- Fairchild Semiconductor is where the modern chip industry was actually invented. **Jean Hoerni's planar process** made mass-producing reliable transistors possible. **Robert Noyce's integrated circuit** in 1959 put many transistors on one slice of silicon.
- More importantly, Fairchild itself spawned the valley. Engineers kept leaving to start new chip companies. By one count more than fifty semiconductor companies trace direct lineage to Fairchild. They have a name in the industry: the **Fairchildren**.
- The list includes AMD, National Semiconductor, LSI Logic, and most importantly, Intel.
- Fairchild itself slowly faded because its east coast parent skimmed the cash to fund other businesses and would not let the engineers have equity. Equity, it turned out, was the entire game.

## Intel

- **Intel** was founded in **1968** by Noyce and Moore, leaving Fairchild over exactly that equity problem. Andy Grove, employee number three, would become its most important CEO.
- Intel built memory chips first. Their big shift was the **microprocessor**, the 4004 in 1971, a computer on a chip. Originally built for a Japanese calculator company.
- In the early 1980s Intel pivoted hard from memory to microprocessors after Japanese manufacturers wiped them out in DRAM. Grove called it surviving "the strategic inflection point." The 8086 family powered the IBM PC and made Intel the most valuable chip company in the world for thirty years.

## The Homebrew Computer Club and Apple

- A few miles up the peninsula in **1975**, a group of electronics hobbyists started meeting in **Menlo Park** to share parts, ideas, and bootleg copies of software. It was called the **Homebrew Computer Club**.
- **Steve Wozniak** brought his single-board personal computer to a Homebrew meeting in 1975. **Steve Jobs** saw it and saw something Woz did not. A product.
- In **1976** Wozniak, Jobs, and Ron Wayne founded **Apple Computer**. Wayne sold his 10 percent stake back for $800 within two weeks. The stake would later be worth tens of billions.
- The **Apple II** in 1977 made Apple a real company. The **Macintosh** in 1984, with its graphical interface borrowed from Xerox PARC down the road, made it culturally important.

## Microsoft

- **Microsoft** was founded in **1975** in **Albuquerque, New Mexico**, not California. Bill Gates and Paul Allen moved there to be near MITS, the maker of the Altair 8800, for which they had written a BASIC interpreter.
- They moved the company to **Bellevue, Washington** in **1979**, near Gates's hometown of Seattle. Microsoft is the great counterexample to the rule that you have to be in the valley. It built a parallel software empire in the Pacific Northwest.
- The decision that built Microsoft was the **IBM PC deal in 1980**. IBM, rushing to ship a personal computer, asked Microsoft for an operating system. Microsoft did not have one. They bought QDOS from Tim Paterson at Seattle Computer Products for $50,000, polished it, licensed it to IBM as **MS-DOS**, and crucially kept the right to license it to anyone else.
- When the IBM PC clone market exploded, Microsoft was already on every clone. The hardware became commodity. The software became the platform. Bill Gates understood the asymmetry before IBM did.

## Why the geography mattered

- **Stanford** kept producing technically literate founders. MIT and Carnegie Mellon were just as strong technically but did not encourage commerce the same way.
- **Venture capital** clustered. **Kleiner Perkins** was founded in **1972** by Eugene Kleiner, one of the traitorous eight, with Tom Perkins from HP. **Sequoia Capital** was founded the same year by Don Valentine, a Fairchild alum. Both set up on **Sand Hill Road in Menlo Park**, where most of the major firms still sit.
- **California non-compete law**. California courts, going back to a 1872 statute, refuse to enforce post-employment non-compete agreements. This is the single most underrated reason Silicon Valley exists. Engineers can quit and start competitors. Ideas spread fast. In Boston, on the other hand, Route 128 had similar talent, similar capital, and a similar early start, but Massachusetts enforced non-competes, ideas stayed locked inside companies, and the cluster slowly lost to the valley.
- **Open culture**. Engineers shared at Homebrew. They moved between firms. They started over. The social norm was that you bet on the next thing, not that you stayed loyal to the last one.

## The pattern

- The valley is best understood as a tree. Shockley is the root. Fairchild is the trunk. Intel, AMD, National, and a hundred others are the branches.
- Apple, Microsoft, Oracle, Sun, Cisco, Google, Facebook, Nvidia all grew in the same soil, fed by Stanford grads, Sand Hill money, and the right to leave your employer without legal consequence.
- The two things hardest to copy elsewhere are the non-compete law and the density of capital. Cities have spent thirty years trying to reproduce the rest and mostly failed.
- When people ask why Silicon Valley happened in California and nowhere else, the honest answer is that it took a great university, a terrible boss, eight engineers willing to walk out, a labor law from 1872, and forty years of compounding before anyone could see the pattern.
