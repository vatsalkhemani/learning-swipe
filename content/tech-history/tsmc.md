# How TSMC came to matter more than most countries

## What TSMC is and why it matters

**TSMC** (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company) is a chip foundry headquartered in **Hsinchu, Taiwan**. It manufactures roughly 90% of the world's most advanced semiconductors (anything at the cutting-edge nodes below 10nm). Apple's M-series chips, Nvidia's H100 and B200 AI GPUs, AMD's Ryzen processors, Qualcomm's Snapdragons, and most of what powers modern AI training are all made by TSMC. The company matters because a single corporation on a single island that sits roughly 100 miles from a hostile superpower is the bottleneck for nearly every advanced piece of technology the rest of the world uses. AI compute, smartphones, military systems, electric vehicles, data centers, the rollout of the next decade of products: all of it depends on those fabs continuing to operate.

## How it started

- **1987.** **Morris Chang**, a Texas Instruments veteran of 25 years, was recruited by the **Taiwanese government** to come back to Taiwan and build a semiconductor industry. He was 55. The government took a 48% equity stake. Philips took 27.5%. Private Taiwanese investors took the rest.
- **The bet that nobody else had made.** Pure-play foundry. TSMC would only manufacture chips for other companies. It would never design or sell its own chips under its own brand.
- Chang's insight: every existing chipmaker (Intel, Motorola, Texas Instruments) both designed and manufactured. If a startup wanted to make a custom chip, it had to either build its own fab (impossibly expensive) or partner with a competitor (who might steal the design). A neutral foundry removed that fear.
- **The fabless industry exploded as a direct result.** **Nvidia** (founded 1993), **AMD** (after spinning off **GlobalFoundries** in 2009), **Qualcomm**, **Broadcom**, **MediaTek**, eventually **Apple Silicon**: all of these companies design chips and let TSMC manufacture them. None of them would exist in their current form without the foundry model.

## Why this is so hard to copy

- **A leading-edge fab costs $20 billion or more** to build. A modern TSMC site can be $30B+. Building one takes 3-4 years.
- The precision required is extreme. Modern chips have features measured in single-digit nanometers, smaller than most viruses. Every step (lithography, deposition, etching, packaging) has to be near-perfect across hundreds of layers.
- **EUV lithography**, the technology that lets chips get below 10nm, is made by exactly one company in the world: **ASML** in the Netherlands. Each EUV machine costs around $200 million, weighs about 180 tons, and ships in a fleet of 747s. ASML is itself a chokepoint.
- **Yield** (the percentage of chips that come off the line working) is the real moat. TSMC's yield on bleeding-edge nodes is far ahead of competitors, and the gap compounds because more revenue funds more learning.

## TSMC pulled ahead of Intel

- For decades **Intel** was the process leader. Their fab capability was the envy of the industry.
- Around 2018 the gap closed. TSMC reached **7nm** production while Intel was still struggling to ship **10nm** (the naming conventions diverged, so it's not a direct comparison, but the capability gap was real).
- TSMC then opened a clear lead at **5nm** (2020) and **3nm** (2022). Apple Silicon, which moved from Intel x86 to ARM-based TSMC-fabbed chips in 2020, is the highest-profile customer of that lead.
- Intel's response: a new strategy called **IDM 2.0** under CEO **Pat Gelsinger** (announced 2021), opening Intel's fabs to outside customers. Still behind TSMC on the leading edge as of the mid-2020s.

## What TSMC actually makes for whom

- **Apple.** M1, M2, M3, M4, A-series chips for iPhones and Macs. Apple is TSMC's biggest customer.
- **Nvidia.** H100 and B200 AI GPUs, the chips that train ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and most of the AI industry.
- **AMD.** Ryzen CPUs, Instinct AI accelerators.
- **Qualcomm.** Snapdragon SoCs for Android phones.
- **MediaTek.** Chips for mid-range Android.
- **Broadcom, Marvell, Tesla's FSD chip, Sony image sensors, automotive controllers**: the long tail is enormous.

## The geopolitics

- **Taiwan sits about 100 miles from mainland China.** China formally claims Taiwan as part of its territory. Tensions have escalated through the 2020s.
- If TSMC's fabs were destroyed in a conflict (or captured, or simply taken offline), the global tech industry would seize up for years. There is no spare capacity at this node anywhere in the world.
- This has produced the so-called "**silicon shield**" thesis: Taiwan's chip dominance is itself a deterrent. China can't capture working fabs (the IP, the supply chain, the expertise don't transfer cleanly). The world has a strong interest in keeping Taiwan functional. The argument is debated, not settled.

## The US response

- **CHIPS and Science Act (August 2022).** $52 billion in incentives to bring leading-edge chip manufacturing back to US soil.
- **TSMC Arizona.** TSMC committed to building fabs near Phoenix. Total investment announced around $65 billion across multiple phases. First fab started production in 2024-2025 at 4nm, second phase at 3nm, third at 2nm planned.
- **Intel's Ohio fab** and expansion in Arizona and New Mexico, funded partly by CHIPS Act money.
- **Samsung** building in Taylor, Texas.
- Building the fabs is the easy part. Staffing them with the engineers and operators TSMC trained over decades in Taiwan is much harder. Early reports from TSMC Arizona suggested production timelines and cultural friction were real issues.

## China and the export controls

- **China's leading foundry is SMIC** (Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation). Behind TSMC by several generations.
- **US export controls.** Since 2022, the US has blocked the sale of EUV lithography equipment and advanced AI chips to China. ASML is not allowed to sell its most advanced machines to Chinese customers. The intent is to slow China's progress at the leading edge.
- China has poured enormous state funding into domestic alternatives. Progress is real but the gap to TSMC remains.

## The next race

- **Sub-2nm manufacturing at scale by 2027-28.** TSMC is targeting **N2** for 2025 and **A16** (around 1.6nm) for 2026.
- **Samsung** is investing heavily but has struggled with yields on leading nodes.
- **Intel's 18A** is the bet that puts them back in the leading-edge race.
- **Rapidus** in Japan, a government-backed venture aiming for 2nm production by 2027, is the long-shot entry.

## What people get wrong

- "Chips are commodities." Mass-market chips at older nodes are commoditized. Leading-edge chips are not, and the gap between leading and lagging compounds.
- "The US can just build its own fabs and fix this." Building the buildings is the easy part. The harder parts are tooling, materials supply chains, talent, and decades of accumulated process knowledge.
- "Taiwan is replaceable." Eventually, maybe. Not in the next 5-10 years, and possibly not even then for leading-edge.

## What to carry away

- One company in one country makes most of the world's most important physical product.
- The reason for that single point of failure is a smart bet (the foundry model) compounded by decades of process leadership that nobody has been able to match.
- AI compute, every electronic device, military hardware, the next decade of geopolitics: all of it routes through Hsinchu.
- In one sentence: TSMC is the chokepoint nobody planned to build, and the geopolitics of the next decade run through its parking lot.
