# The mobile revolution: how the iPhone reshaped every industry

## What the mobile revolution is and why it matters

In a single decade (roughly 2007 to 2017), the dominant computing device on Earth went from being a desktop or laptop tethered to a desk to a glass-and-metal rectangle in nearly four billion pockets. The **iPhone** launched in 2007. The **App Store** launched in 2008. From there, more or less every consumer industry got rebuilt on top of the mobile platform: payments, photography, navigation, music, video, taxis, food, dating, news, banking. The shift matters because it's the most recent example of a complete platform turnover, and the case study every aspiring product builder studies. The companies that recognized the shift early (or got lucky and were born in it) became some of the biggest companies in history. The companies that didn't (Nokia, BlackBerry, Kodak, Motorola, TomTom, Garmin's consumer business) collapsed faster than they thought possible.

## What phones were before the iPhone

- **Nokia** dominated. About 51% of global mobile phone market share in 2007.
- **BlackBerry (RIM)** owned the business phone. Physical QWERTY keyboard, push email, enterprise security. Loved by CEOs and lawyers.
- **Motorola Razr** was the consumer status phone of the mid-2000s.
- Smartphones existed (**Palm Treo**, **Windows Mobile** devices, **Symbian** phones from Nokia and Sony Ericsson) but were terrible for normal users. Tiny screens, stylus required, browsers that couldn't render real web pages, no consumer app ecosystem worth using.
- The carriers (**AT&T**, **Verizon**, **Vodafone**) controlled what could be installed on phones. Hardware manufacturers built to carrier specifications. The customer experience was an afterthought.

## The launch that broke everything

- **January 9, 2007.** **Steve Jobs** at Macworld announces the iPhone. The pitch ("an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator") was meant to make people realize these were one device.
- **June 29, 2007.** iPhone goes on sale. $499 (4GB) or $599 (8GB). 2G EDGE network only (3G came in 2008). No App Store yet. No copy-paste. No multitasking. It still felt like the future because of three things: real multi-touch, the accelerometer (rotate the phone, the screen rotates), and a real browser. **Safari** rendered actual web pages, not the dumbed-down WAP versions phones had served for years.
- **July 10, 2008.** **App Store** launches with 500 apps. This was the inflection point. Apps as the platform turned the phone from a device into a marketplace.

## What got disrupted (and how fast)

- **Cameras.** "The best camera is the one you have with you." **Kodak** filed for bankruptcy in January 2012. Compact point-and-shoot sales collapsed. Even DSLR sales eventually fell as phone cameras improved.
- **GPS devices.** **TomTom** and **Garmin** lost the consumer market within about three years. Google Maps on your phone was free, always updated, and you already had it.
- **MP3 players.** The iPod self-cannibalized. Apple was willing to do this. Few companies are.
- **Portable video.** Flip cameras went from category-defining (acquired by Cisco for $590M in 2009) to discontinued (2011).
- **Alarm clocks, voice recorders, calculators, flashlights, basic radios.** All folded into the phone.
- **Maps printed on paper.** A whole industry.
- **Watches.** Briefly, while smartwatches found their footing. Now back, partly as a phone accessory.

## Android arrives

- **2005.** **Google** acquires a small startup called Android for around $50 million. Widely called one of the best acquisitions in tech history.
- **September 2008.** First Android phone, the **HTC Dream / T-Mobile G1**, launches. Slide-out keyboard, primitive by later standards.
- **The model.** Android was open source, given away free to phone manufacturers, in exchange for Google's services (Search, Maps, Gmail, later Play Store) being pre-installed. **Samsung**, **HTC**, **LG**, **Sony**, eventually **Xiaomi**, **Oppo**, **Vivo**, **OnePlus** all built on it.
- Android grew faster than iPhone because it was cheaper, available on every carrier, and came in dozens of form factors.
- **Today.** Android holds roughly 71% of global smartphone share, iOS holds about 28%. But iOS captures around 80% of total smartphone profits because of its premium positioning.

## The companies built on top of mobile

- **Uber.** Founded March 2009. The product (tap a button, a car arrives, no cash exchanged) was only possible because of phones with GPS, payment storage, and always-on data.
- **Instagram.** Launched October 2010. Photo-sharing built mobile-first. Sold to Facebook in April 2012 for about $1 billion when it had 13 employees and zero revenue.
- **Snapchat.** Launched 2011. Built around the camera-first interface that only made sense on a phone.
- **WhatsApp.** Founded 2009. Sold to **Meta** in 2014 for about $19 billion. Used by roughly 2 billion people today.
- **TikTok.** Launched 2016 by **ByteDance**. The recommendation algorithm only works with the constant signal that mobile gives.
- **DoorDash, Robinhood, Venmo, Cash App, Coinbase, Airbnb's app-first growth, Spotify, Netflix's streaming-on-mobile pivot.** Most of the consumer giants of the last 15 years were either built on mobile or rebuilt themselves on it.

## The leapfrog story in India

- India largely skipped the era of widespread landlines and household PCs. The country went from limited connectivity straight to mobile.
- Roughly 700 million smartphone users today.
- **Reliance Jio** launched in September 2016 with free 4G data and ultra-cheap smartphones. It dropped India's data prices to among the lowest in the world and tripled mobile data usage almost overnight. Jio added over 100 million subscribers in its first six months.
- This accelerated India's mobile-first economy faster than almost any other large country.

## Mobile payments

- **M-Pesa** launched in Kenya in 2007 by **Safaricom**. SMS-based mobile money. Today moves a meaningful share of Kenya's GDP through the system.
- **Alipay** (started 2003 as Alibaba's escrow tool, became a payments app on mobile) and **WeChat Pay** (2013) dominate China. Cash usage collapsed in major Chinese cities.
- **UPI (Unified Payments Interface)** launched in India in 2016 by the **National Payments Corporation of India**. By 2024, UPI is processing over **11 billion transactions per month**. It's the largest real-time payments system in the world. PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm are the dominant apps.

## What people get wrong

- "The iPhone was the first smartphone." It wasn't. It was the first one normal humans wanted to use.
- "Android is just a worse iPhone." Android won on volume and on flexibility. Different model, not worse.
- "Mobile is a saturated market, nothing new will happen." Saturation by units doesn't mean saturation by use cases. AI-native mobile experiences are reshaping what the device is used for, and we're still early on that curve.

## What to carry away

- A platform shift this complete only happens every couple of decades. Mainframes to PCs, PCs to web, web to mobile. The next one (AI as a platform, or wearables, or something not yet visible) will reset the leaderboard again.
- The lesson incumbents missed: when the platform shifts, your distribution advantage in the old platform doesn't transfer. Nokia made great phones. The platform stopped being "phones."
- In one sentence: the mobile revolution put a supercomputer in every pocket and rebuilt almost every consumer industry on top of it inside a decade.
