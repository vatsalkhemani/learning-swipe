# The dotcom bubble: 1995-2002, the first internet boom and bust

## What the dotcom bubble was and why it matters

The **dotcom bubble** was a roughly five-year stretch (1995-2000) where public market valuations for any company associated with the internet detached from any reasonable measure of value. The **NASDAQ** roughly quintupled, peaked on March 10, 2000, then fell about 78% over the next two years. An estimated **$5 trillion** of paper wealth disappeared. The bubble matters because it produced the template that every later tech cycle has either repeated or self-consciously tried to avoid. The post-bubble survivors (**Amazon, eBay, Google, Yahoo** for a while) became the giants. The post-bubble lessons (unit economics matter, profitability isn't optional forever) shaped Sand Hill Road's worldview for at least a generation, and they get re-learned every cycle.

## How the web showed up

- **1989-1991.** **Tim Berners-Lee** at **CERN** in Switzerland invented the World Wide Web. HTTP, HTML, URLs, the whole stack.
- **1993.** **Mosaic**, the first widely usable graphical web browser, was released by **Marc Andreessen** and a team at NCSA at the **University of Illinois**.
- **1994.** Andreessen co-founded **Netscape**. Navigator became the dominant browser.
- **August 9, 1995.** **Netscape IPO.** Stock priced at $28, opened at $71, closed at $58 the first day. It established the new template: take a company with no profits public, watch the stock pop, mint paper millionaires.

## The bubble inflates

- **1996.** **Yahoo** IPO. Jerry Yang and David Filo's web directory becomes a public company.
- **1997.** **Amazon** IPO at $18 per share. **Jeff Bezos** was widely ridiculed for selling books online with no profits in sight. He kept saying "Day 1." Most analysts laughed.
- **1998.** **eBay** IPO. **Google** founded by **Larry Page** and **Sergey Brin** in a Menlo Park garage. Google held off on going public.
- **1998-2000.** Anything with ".com" in the name went up. Research later (**Cooper, Dimitrov, Rau, 2001**) found that companies that added ".com" to their name during this period saw stock price increases averaging around 74% over the following 10 days, regardless of whether they had any actual internet business.
- **Super Bowl XXXIV (January 2000).** Sixteen dotcom companies bought commercials. Many were bankrupt within 18 months.
- **March 10, 2000.** NASDAQ peaks at 5,048.

## The most famous flameouts

- **Pets.com.** Sock puppet mascot, IPO in February 2000, bankrupt by November 2000. Selling pet food online with delivery costs that exceeded the order value. The unit economics never worked.
- **Webvan.** Online grocery delivery. Raised about $375 million, blew through it building infrastructure that wasn't profitable at any scale they reached. Bankrupt July 2001.
- **Boo.com.** Online fashion. Burned through $135 million in 18 months. Heavy site that nobody on dial-up could actually load.
- **eToys.** Online toy retailer. Valued at $7.8 billion at peak. Bankrupt 2001.
- **Kozmo.com.** One-hour delivery of small items. Raised $280 million. Couldn't make the economics work. Gone.
- **Pseudo.com.** Online video streaming, a decade too early. The infrastructure (bandwidth, codecs, devices) wasn't there yet. The idea wasn't wrong. The timing was.

## What burst it

- The Federal Reserve raised interest rates six times between June 1999 and May 2000.
- Sentiment cracked in March 2000. Once it cracked, it cascaded.
- Many dotcoms had been spending IPO cash on customer acquisition (Super Bowl ads, free shipping, deep discounts). When new capital dried up, they had no underlying business to fall back on.
- **NASDAQ** fell from 5,048 to about 1,114 by October 2002. Roughly a 78% decline.
- Roughly $5 trillion of market cap evaporated. Pension funds, retirement accounts, day traders, employees with vested options. All hit.

## The survivors

- **Amazon.** Stock fell from $107 to $7. Bezos kept reinvesting, kept saying "Day 1," launched AWS in 2006. Today a multi-trillion-dollar company.
- **eBay.** Profitable through the crash. Survived.
- **Google.** Held its IPO in August 2004, well after the bust, with a Dutch auction process designed to prevent first-day pops. Now the parent of Alphabet.
- **Yahoo.** Survived the crash, then declined for a different reason: failure to adapt to search and mobile. Eventually sold to Verizon in 2017.
- **Cisco.** Lost about 80% of its peak value. Survived. Never recovered the peak market cap.

## The lessons that stuck (briefly)

- **Unit economics matter.** You can't lose money on every sale and make it up in volume. Pets.com proved this with painful clarity.
- **Profitability beats growth at any price.** The fashion shifted toward companies that could show a path to making money.
- **"Eyeballs" is not a metric.** The bubble-era obsession with page views and registered users (independent of revenue) died as a respectable pitch.
- **Sand Hill Road's mood reset.** **Sequoia Capital's** famous 2008 "**RIP Good Times**" memo (during the financial crisis, not the dotcom crash, but in the same spirit) became the template for venture firms warning their portfolios to cut burn and extend runway.

## The lessons that didn't stick

- The **2010s SaaS boom** funded plenty of growth-at-all-costs companies. Some worked, many didn't.
- The **2021 crypto and SPAC mania** repeated nearly every dotcom pattern: detached valuations, retail FOMO, vehicles built to bypass normal IPO scrutiny, celebrity endorsements. Then a brutal reset.
- **AI in the 2020s** has prompted constant comparisons to 1999. The honest answer: the technology is real (more so than most dotcom-era tech was), but valuations on the speculative tail look familiar.

## What people get wrong

- "Everyone lost money." Not everyone. Insiders who sold near the peak made fortunes. Many of those fortunes seeded the next generation of VC funds.
- "The internet was a bad bet." The internet was the right bet. The timing and the prices were the problem. Almost every dotcom idea that failed has since worked: pet supplies online (Chewy), groceries online (Instacart, Amazon Fresh), one-hour delivery (DoorDash), online fashion (Shein, ASOS).
- "The bust killed Silicon Valley." It didn't. The bust cleared the field of companies without business models and left a smaller, more disciplined cohort that became the modern tech giants.

## What to carry away

- The bubble was the first time public markets had to price the internet. They got it badly wrong in both directions.
- The technology was real. The valuations were not.
- Every later tech cycle has been compared to 1999 by someone. Usually too early, sometimes correctly, almost never both.
- In one sentence: the dotcom bubble proved the internet was world-changing, and proved that being right about the future doesn't mean being right about price or timing.
